# The Lesson Journey — template for every topic page

This is the skeleton every lesson page (Identity, Belonging, Courage, Prayer, Forgiveness,
Purpose, and whatever comes after) should follow. It's built from adult-learning theory
(andragogy), current research on how Gen Z/Alpha actually engage, and trauma-informed /
safeguarding practice for youth-facing conversations. Sources at the bottom.

A mentor landing on a lesson page should be able to answer, in under two minutes of reading:
*how long will this take, am I ready, what am I assuming about the room, what am I trying to
do, how do I hold this, what do I need, and what do I do if it goes sideways.*

---

## 1. At a Glance

The very top of the page, scannable in five seconds:

- **Time**: a real range (e.g. "45–60 minutes"), not a false-precise number
- **Group size**: works 1:1 or in a group — say which, or both
- **Age**: written as "high school age (or the equivalent stage in your country)" — never a
  hard number, since school systems vary globally
- **Format**: in-person, virtual-friendly, or either

## 2. Before You Begin

Two short lists, side by side:

**What you should know** — almost always "nothing you don't already have." If a lesson
genuinely needs prep (e.g. reading a passage beforehand), say exactly what and how long it
takes. Default to zero required prep — that's the whole point of this resource.

**What we're assuming** — be explicit about the starting point so a mentor isn't caught off
guard. Typically: varying faith backgrounds in the room, no prior exposure to this topic, and
*not* assuming any shared trauma history, agreement, or willingness to speak. Never assume the
room is homogeneous.

## 3. Learning Goals

2–4 goals, written in plain language, framed as what the young person walks away *feeling* or
*able to do* — not academic objectives. ("Leaves knowing they don't have to have this figured
out" beats "Understands the concept of identity formation.") Andragogy says adults (and by
extension, self-aware teens) engage when they see the relevance up front — so goals should read
like a promise, not a syllabus line.

## 4. How to Hold the Room

Facilitation posture, not lesson content:

- Open with a norm-setting moment: what's said here stays here, nobody has to share, silence is
  fine
- Ask more than you tell — the lesson provides prompts, not speeches
- Build in a pause point — don't rush from activity to activity
- Give an explicit way to pass or opt out of any activity

## 5. The Lesson Flow

The actual activity — lesson-specific, built one topic at a time. Structured in short chunks
(roughly 10–15 minutes each, per Gen Z/Alpha attention research) that alternate between a beat
of input, a beat of doing, and a beat of reflecting. Interactive by default: something the young
person does, makes, moves, or discusses — never a page of text to read at them.

## 6. What You'll Need

A short checklist. Bias hard toward zero-cost, zero-prep: paper and pens, not printed handouts
requiring a printer; nothing that requires purchase. If a lesson needs a printable, link it and
say how many copies.

## 7. If It's Not Going Well

The most important section, and the one every other youth-facing resource skips. Tiered, from
mildest to most serious:

1. **Silence, or nobody's talking** — normal, not a failure. Let it sit. Rephrase the question
   once, then wait again.
2. **Pushback, doubt, or disagreement** — welcome, not a problem to fix. Curiosity beats
   correction, every time. (This lesson's premise: "you are already whole" — that includes
   whoever's pushing back.)
3. **A strong emotional reaction** (tears, shutdown, anger) — pause the activity. Ground the
   room: a breath, a stretch, a change of pace. Check in with that person one-on-one if you can,
   without making them the center of group attention.
4. **A disclosure of real harm** (abuse, self-harm, suicidal thoughts, anything urgent) — stop
   facilitating a "lesson" and respond as a caring adult: stay calm, don't react with shock,
   thank them for telling you, don't promise secrecy. Then **follow your local mandatory-reporting
   obligations** and involve a trained professional or the appropriate authority in your country
   — a mentor is not a substitute for that. This is not legal advice; know your own country's and
   organization's reporting requirements *before* you lead a lesson, not in the moment.

Every lesson page should carry a short, unmissable version of point 4 — not just link to it once
on the For Mentors page. A mentor mid-crisis won't go hunting for a separate page.

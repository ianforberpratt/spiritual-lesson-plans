The three degrees · Science and Health, pages 115–116

There is no retrograde step.

This page is for when something felt fixed and then it wasn’t: loving patience for yourself, and a steady direction toward Love.

Not a ladder. A journey.

Forty-two seconds on the three degrees, the zigzag, and what stays improved.

Read the words of the video

Not a ladder. A journey. Mary Baker Eddy marked out three degrees, in just a few lines. The first: physical. Fear, pride, sickness. The wind, the earthquake, the fire. The Lord was not in them. The second: moral. Honesty, compassion, hope. The moment of coming to our senses. The third: understanding. A gentle whisper. "Lazarus, come out." Wisdom, love, health. And the human road zigzags. Mrs. Eddy calls it a "zigzag course," a pendulum between sin and the hope of forgiveness. So, put it to the test. Dana settles a long disagreement with a colleague. Later, the old resentment is back, louder. Has she lost the second degree? Reason it from the book. "An improved belief cannot retrograde." The road zigzags. What improved, stays improved. Don't take my word for it. Test it in your own day.

The honest wrinkle

Anyone who has tried this knows the road does not run in a straight line. The book says so, and it also says something that looks like the opposite. Both are there.

The road zigzags

Mrs. Eddy writes of a "zigzag course" and of vibrating "like a pendulum" between sin and the hope of forgiveness. She says moral progress, then, "will be slow."

Science and Health 21:24–22:6

Then comes a line to hold: "Wait for your reward, and 'be not weary in well doing.'"

Science and Health 22:12–14

What improved does not retrograde

"An improved belief cannot retrograde."

Science and Health 442:18–19

"In Christian Science there is never a retrograde step, never a return to positions outgrown."

Science and Health 74:29–30

One way to hold both is to see that the zigzag belongs to the human sense of the road, while what has truly improved stays improved. And "an improved belief is one step out of error, and aids in taking the next step" (296:28–30). That is a reading to test, not a ruling. For honesty: the line at 74:29–30 sits in a passage about death and spiritualism, while 442:18–19 speaks more generally. Take the passages to the book and read them in context.

Put it to the test

When it felt fixed, and then it wasn’t

These are real-life situations, told with invented names. Pick the one closest to yours. Read it slowly. You are not being graded.

Dana and a colleague spent weeks at odds over how to run a project, and he overruled her in front of the team. Then they sat down and talked it through. It turned out they were each right about different things. They settled it, she let the hurt go, and she felt light for the first time in a month. A while later his name lands on a reply-all that skips a step they had agreed on, and the old resentment is back, louder than before.

What it can feel like

The sinking thought, "I thought this was fixed." It stings because you meant it when you let it go, and you still mean it.

What the zigzag is not saying

That the settling didn't count, or that you are starting from zero.

What the book offers

"An improved belief cannot retrograde" (442:18–19). The road's zigzag is described plainly at 21:24–22:6, with the counsel to "be not weary in well doing" (22:12–14).

A Bible companion

Peter walked on the water, wavered, and called, "Lord, save me!" "Immediately Jesus reached out his hand and caught him" (Matthew 14:30–31).

The settling still counts. The hand is already reaching.

Maya, 16, decided last month she was finished comparing herself to everyone online. Saturday night she is scrolling and doing it all over again.

What it can feel like

Like proof that nothing really changed. That voice is very sure of itself, and it can sound like your own.

What the zigzag is not saying

That the month before was fake, or that you are one of the people who "can't."

What the book offers

Mortal belief "says to mortals, 'You are wretched!'" and nothing changes "until the belief changes" (297:1–2). Meanwhile "the purification of sense and self is a proof of progress" (324:4–5).

A Bible companion

Elijah collapses under a broom bush and wants to quit. An angel does not scold him. "Get up and eat, for the journey is too much for you" (1 Kings 19:7).

The journey is allowed to feel like too much. You are still on it, and you are not alone.

Sam promised himself he would stay calm with his kids this week. Tuesday at dinner he raises his voice, and the room goes quiet.

What it can feel like

The quiet afterward can feel like a verdict. It isn't one. You care enough to notice, and that caring is the direction you are facing.

What the zigzag is not saying

That you are the parent you feared you'd be.

What the book offers

"Be of good cheer" (Miscellaneous Writings 118:24–25). And "an improved belief is one step out of error, and aids in taking the next step" (296:28–30).

A Bible companion

The father in Jesus' story sees his son "a long way off," is "filled with compassion," and runs to him (Luke 15:20).

Repair is part of the road. Love goes toward you, too.

Chris felt steady after a hard season. The night before an important conversation, the old dread comes back as if nothing had changed.

What it can feel like

Dread often arrives with a false memory of how things always go. You can feel it arrive and still know what you've learned.

What the zigzag is not saying

That the steadiness was luck, or that it is gone.

What the book offers

"In Christian Science there is never a retrograde step, never a return to positions outgrown" (74:29–30). "An improved belief cannot retrograde" (442:18–19).

A Bible companion

"Be still, and know that I am God" (Psalm 46:10).

What you learned about Love did not leave. You can stand on it.

Start here: the 10-minute version

Short on time? Do these three steps tonight and come back to the rest later. A few words about the words: overfunctioning means doing more than your share, often out of love, worry, or habit. Underfunctioning means doing less than you could, often because the other person already covers it. Neither word is about worth. They describe roles in a dance two people learned together.

  1. Each of you, alone, for two minutes: finish this sentence. “The thing I carry in our marriage that nobody sees is ______.”

The thing I carry in our marriage that nobody sees is ______.

  1. Together, for five minutes: read your sentences aloud. The only rule is to listen. No fixing, no defending, no “but.”
  2. Together, for three minutes: pick one thing from Part 6 to try for the next 30 days.

Worth doing the rest when you have an evening. The parts below show why you each carry what you carry, which is usually the missing piece.

Part 1: The scripts we were handed

Long before you met, each of you absorbed a script for how a marriage works. Nobody sat you down and taught it. You watched it at the dinner table. Those scripts are why two loving people can both feel unseen. Fill in your own column alone, then compare. Where the answers differ, you have found a place worth being curious about, not a place where one of you is wrong.

Who made the big decisions in the home I grew up in?

Who did the daily work: meals, money, kids, errands?

What did I see a "good" husband or wife doing?

How did the adults around me handle disagreement? (Talked it out, went quiet, got loud, left the room)

What was I praised for as a child? (Helping, achieving, being quiet, being strong)

What did I learn about asking for help?

What did I learn about saying no?

How did my family or culture expect a marriage to begin? (Arranged, chosen, something in between)

What do I still expect without having said it out loud?

A note on scripts you are rewriting. Many couples are living differently from how they were raised, and that is hard in both directions. One person may have grown up expecting the other to decide, while both of you now want to decide together. The old script does not vanish because you changed your mind. It shows up as a pause before speaking, a “whatever you think,” or a habit of going along. The other person may have learned to carry everything alone and call it strength. Neither habit is a flaw in character. Both are old instructions, and old instructions can be updated slowly, on purpose.

Check yourself: Did I describe my partner’s script as “the old way” and mine as “normal”? If so, rewrite both as simply different.

Part 2: Who carries what

Each of you picks an answer for every row, separately, without looking at the other’s. Then compare. The surprises are the point. The feeling box under each answer asks how you each feel about the current split, because a fair split on paper can still feel heavy.

Money, bills, big financial decisions

Planning, calendars, schedules

Kids: school, doctors, daily care

Household work and errands

Extended family and in-laws

Emotional temperature (noticing moods, smoothing things over)

Starting hard conversations

Affection, romance, quality time

Faith, traditions, holidays

Saying no and setting limits

Look for three things:

  1. Rows where you both said “mostly me.” You may both be overfunctioning there, each carrying alone.
  2. Rows where you both said “mostly my partner.” Nobody feels in charge, and things slip.
  3. Rows with a heavy feeling in the feeling box, even if the split looks fair. That is where the unseen weight lives.

Part 3: The dance

Overfunctioning and underfunctioning feed each other. When one person does more, the other has less room to step in. When one person steps back, the other feels they must do more. Each move makes the other move feel necessary. Nobody planned it, and nobody is the villain.

Pick one row from Part 2 where you carry the most, and trace the loop below. Write one line for each step. (If you are doing this together, one of you can go first, then the other.)

1. The area where I tend to step in first:

2. What I tell myself when I do: ("It's faster if I do it," "Nobody else will," "I'm the responsible one")

3. What I feel while carrying it: (Tired, proud, resentful, needed, alone)

4. What my partner probably does in response:

5. What that response makes me believe:

6. What I am afraid would happen if I stopped:

Now flip it. If you tend to do less in some area, answer these:

What do I assume my partner will handle?

What have I stopped offering to do because it never goes my way?

Where do I go along with something because speaking up feels costly?

What would I take on, if I felt it was truly safe to?

The quiet kind. Overfunctioning is not always tasks. It can be emotional: managing the mood in the room, going along to keep the peace, carrying everyone’s expectations, or never asking for what you need. This kind is easy to miss because it looks like harmony. Ask yourself whether either of you does it, and what it costs.

Part 4: Faith and values

Faith, or the lack of it, shapes how each of you thinks about marriage, authority, sacrifice, and what a good life asks of a person. You do not need to share beliefs to share a home. You do need to understand what the other person’s beliefs ask of them. The goal here is not to decide whose view is right. It is to know what each of you is holding.

Write in your own words, in your own terms. Each person describes their own faith or worldview, and nobody describes the other’s.

What did I learn, from faith or family values, about what a marriage is for?

What did I learn about who leads in a marriage, if anyone?

What does my faith or worldview ask of me as a spouse?

What does it ask of me as a parent?

What practices or rhythms keep me steady? (Prayer, meditation, rest, service, community)

Where do my beliefs and my partner's meet easily?

Where do they rub, and what does the rub feel like in daily life?

What do I want our children to receive from each of our traditions?

What would make me feel my faith is respected in our home?

A gentle rule for this section: ask questions to understand, not to win. If you notice yourself preparing a rebuttal, stop and ask instead: “Can you tell me more about what that means to you?”

Part 5: Talk it through

Pick a calm time, not the middle of an argument. Fifteen to thirty minutes, phones away. Mornings often work better than late nights. If your first language differs, use the one that feels most like home to the person who is sharing.

Ground rules. One person speaks at a time. The listener repeats back what they heard before answering. No fixing, no defending, no “but.” Either of you can pause the conversation and resume it later.

  1. Start with what’s good. Each of you names one thing about your partner that you are grateful for, and be specific.
  2. Share what you found. Each of you shares one surprise from Parts 1 to 4. Not a complaint, a discovery.
  3. Ask the two care questions. “When I do or say [something], what happens for you?” and “What would you like more of from me?”
  4. Listen for the ‘no.’ Ask: “When you say no to me, how does it land? When does it get heard, and when doesn’t it?” Then honor the answer, even if it is uncomfortable.
  5. Name one thing you each want to carry less, and one thing you each would like to carry more. Sometimes the two fit together like puzzle pieces.
  6. End with thanks. Thank each other for being brave enough to look.

If it gets heated: stop, breathe, and say, “I want to keep going. Can we take ten minutes and come back?” Then actually come back.

If something painful comes up that either of you does not want to explore, that is okay. Some memories belong to the person who holds them. You can say, “I’m not ready to talk about that,” and the other person’s job is to respect it.

Part 6: One small experiment

Pick one. Only one. Trying to change everything at once is its own kind of overfunctioning. Try it for 30 days, then check in.

This worksheet is a listening tool, not a diagnosis. If you are feeling unsafe, or if something heavy keeps coming up that you cannot work through together, please reach out to a trusted counselor or clergy person.

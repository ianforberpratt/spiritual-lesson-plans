A lesson

Talking About the Increase of Pornography Use in Our World

A steady, shame-free way in — because a habit is a cloud passing in front of the sun, not the sun itself.

Time45–60 min
Group1:1 or group
AgeHigh school (or equiv.)
FormatIn-person or virtual

A note before you begin: This isn't about assuming anyone in the room has a problem, or that anyone doesn't. This is common enough, across every age, including plenty of adults, that treating it as something normal and discussable serves everyone better than treating it as a secret. Nobody here is being singled out.

Before you begin

What you should know

A lot of parents and mentors have started talking about this more openly, because pretending it isn't happening rarely helps anyone. Kids run into this material younger and more automatically than a generation ago — not usually because anyone goes looking on purpose, but because algorithms, ads, and shared devices make it hard to avoid entirely. It's common enough that plenty of adults leading this lesson have their own story with it too. That's not a disqualifier. It might be exactly why you can lead this with warmth instead of alarm — no special preparation required beyond that.

What we're assuming

The starting point

Almost everyone in the room has already run into something like this, on purpose or by accident, whether they'd ever say so out loud. We're not assuming anyone will name their own experience. This works the same whether someone's actively struggling with it, saw something once and felt strange about it, or just wants to understand why it matters.

Learning goals

What a young person walks away with

  • Leaves knowing that having seen this kind of material, or struggled with it, isn't proof of being broken, dirty, or beyond repair — that idea is the lie, not the person.
  • Leaves with language for the difference between a habit and an identity: a habit is something you have, not something you are.
  • Leaves understanding why shame tends to make a habit stronger, not weaker, and what tends to actually loosen its grip instead.
  • Leaves knowing that wanting comfort, connection, or relief isn't the problem — it's worth a second look at where they're actually searching for it.

How to hold the room

Before you start talking

  • This isn't a lecture about danger, and it isn't a confession booth. Nobody should feel cornered into admitting anything.
  • If you have your own history with this, you don't need to disclose it to lead well — but knowing how common it is, including among adults, might help keep judgment out of your voice.
  • Avoid clinical or graphic detail entirely. This lesson is about identity and habit, not content — there's never a reason to describe what anyone has seen.
  • If something shared sounds like more than a personal habit, shift out of lesson mode immediately — see the safety note below.

The lesson flow

Four beats, about 45–60 minutes total

Each beat alternates a little input, a little doing, and a little reflecting. Skip, slow down, or stretch any of these to fit your group.

1
~10 min

Not About the Content

Privately, without describing anything you've seen: think about what this material tends to promise — comfort, connection, control, escape — versus what it actually leaves you feeling afterward. Just notice the gap. Nobody has to share.

2
~12–15 min

The Wrong Shelf

Share this, in your own words: Christian Science teaches that everyone is already whole and complete — not an empty container waiting to be filled by anything, including this. The craving says “you're missing something.” The truth is closer to: you're not missing anything, you're just reaching for the wrong shelf.

Reflect or discuss: underneath this, what are you actually hungry for — comfort? connection? feeling wanted? feeling in control? Name the real hunger. It's rarely actually about what's on the screen.

3
~15 min

A Cloud, Not the Sun

A habit is a cloud passing in front of the sun. It is not the sun. Practice rewriting a shame-statement into an identity-first one: instead of “I'm disgusting for looking at that,” try “That's inconsistent with who I actually am. It's a thief of my peace, not proof of who I am. I'm whole, and I get to just adjust and keep moving.”

Try it: write your own shame-statement, then rewrite it identity-first, using that pattern.

4
~10 min

The Sky Was Never Just the Cloud

Whatever cloud passed over today, or any day, close with this: it was never the whole sky. You are still whole underneath it, completely, right now — not later, not once you've earned it back.

If it's not going well

When this one gets heavy

Read this before you lead it, not during it: this is not a confession moment, and if someone discloses a personal struggle, meet it with the identity-first language above, not alarm. If what surfaces sounds like a real behavioral pattern disrupting sleep, relationships, or daily life, gently point toward a counselor alongside anything spiritual — one conversation isn't meant to resolve that alone.

Separately, and this matters: if a disclosure involves content depicting other minors, anything that sounds non-consensual, “deepfake” images of themselves or a peer, or someone sharing explicit images of them without consent — stop. This is not the same category as a personal viewing habit, and it may be illegal content or a safety crime, including if the young person in front of you is also a minor in the images. Don't ask to see it, don't forward it, don't try to investigate or handle it yourself. Follow your own country's and organization's reporting obligations immediately and involve a trained professional or the right authority — this isn't legal advice, so know your local requirements before you're ever in this seat.

For the fuller tiered guidance — including what to do if someone discloses something serious — see For Mentors.

The Dragon on the Wall

A flashlight, two little hands, and a very big shadow — and a surprise about what a dragon is actually made of.

Time15-20 min
Groupsmall group or full class (4-15 kids)
AgeK-2
Formatin-person, needs a blank wall and a way to dim the room
UpdatedSeptember 21, 2026
Also built for:Grade 3-5Grade 6-8TeenAge 21+

Assumptions & Caveats

Before you begin

What you should know

This age thinks in pictures and hands, not ideas. They won’t follow “fear has no power of its own” as a sentence, but they can watch it happen: a giant, roaring dragon on the wall, and then the light comes on and there’s nothing there. That is the whole lesson, and it happens to be a very accurate picture of the sentence underneath it (“Truth and Love prevail against the dragon because the dragon cannot war with them”). A shadow can’t fight a light. The light doesn’t even try. Don’t explain that. Resist adding a spoken wrap-up after the lights come on. If it landed, they have it, and saying it out loud flattens what they just saw with their own eyes.

Nothing you don’t already have: one flashlight (a phone light is fine), a blank wall, and paper and crayons. Run the shadow once yourself before the group arrives so you know where to stand.

What we're assuming

  • Every child in the room has been scared by something that looked much bigger than it turned out to be: a shadow, a noise, a costume. Some have also been scared by things that were genuinely scary. This lesson is only about the first kind, so never say “there’s nothing to be scared of.” Say “it feels so big. That’s what shadows do.”
  • Some children don’t like a dark room, and some don’t like loud roaring. Dim, never dark.
  • A few children live with something at home that is really frightening. A dragon game can stir that up even when it’s meant to be silly, so it’s worth reading the last section of this page before you begin.
  • Faith backgrounds vary. “God’s Love” is offered plainly as an idea in the room, and no child is asked to say they believe it.

Learning goals

What they walk away with

  1. A felt sense, from watching it rather than being told, that something can look enormous and scary and turn out to be made of almost nothing.
  2. One phrase to say together, with motions: “Big shadow, little hand — Love is the light!”
  3. A dragon of their own to take home and show someone.

How to hold the room

Before you start talking

  • Keep it silly and keep it moving. Roars and giggles are the lesson working.
  • Dim, don’t darken: close the blinds, turn off the overhead lights, and leave the door open or a hallway light on. A child who wants to sit right next to you or right next to the light is doing it exactly right.
  • Let the children make the shadows. The moment their own hand makes the dragon is the moment the idea becomes theirs.
  • After the lights come on, stay quiet for a beat. Don’t summarize.
  • If a child says the dragon was real, believe the feeling and don’t argue about the dragon: “It felt really real, didn’t it?”

The lesson flow

Step by step

Each beat alternates a little input, a little doing, and a little reflecting. Skip, slow down, or stretch any of these to fit your group.

1
~3 min

The worst dragon-fighter

Gather in a circle. Start with a confession: “I have to tell you something. I would be a terrible dragon-fighter. If a dragon showed up at my house, do you know what I’d have? A garden hose!” (Use your own silly, true one: a wooden spoon, a slipper, a pool noodle.) Then ask, “What would you have?” and take three or four silly answers, laughing with them. “Good news: we don’t need any of that today. We’re going to make a dragon instead.”

2
~5 min

Make a dragon

Dim the room (not dark: blinds closed, overhead lights off, door open). Aim the flashlight at the blank wall from a few feet away. Hold your hand close to the light and make a dragon: a fist for a head, two fingers for horns, a wiggling thumb for a jaw. Give it a voice: “Grrrr! Look at those TEETH!” Show them the trick: hand close to the light, the dragon gets HUGE; hand farther away, it gets small. Then let the children make their own, one or two at a time, with their own roars. Let them hold the flashlight if they want to. Follow the giggles.

3
~3 min

Lights on

“Now watch what happens when I turn on the big light.” Flip the lights on (or open the blinds). The dragon is gone. Pause. Then ask, one at a time and only a few answers each: “Did we have to fight it?” and “What did the light do?” If a child says “you turned it on,” say “Yep. That’s all it did.” Then hold up your own hand: “And what was making that whole giant dragon?” Let them shout “your hand!” Then stop talking. No explaining.

4
~4 min

Big shadow, little hand

Keep it warm and short: “Sometimes a worry can look just like that dragon. A loud noise, a scary dream, a big dog, someone yelling. It feels SO big. And there’s something so bright that big scary feelings can’t even fight it. It’s Love, God’s Love. Here’s what the Bible says:”

“There is no fear in love; but perfect love casteth out fear.”

1 John 4:18, KJV

“Love is so bright that fear can’t stay. And there’s a sentence in a book by Mary Baker Eddy that says Truth and Love are so bright that a dragon can’t even fight them.” Then teach the phrase with motions: hands up big for “Big shadow,” one little hand for “little hand,” and arms sweeping wide for “Love is the light!” Say it together two or three times.

5
~5 min

A dragon to take home

Give each child paper and crayons or markers. “Draw the biggest, silliest dragon you can.” If you have craft sticks, tape, and a helper for scissors, they can cut it out and tape it to a stick to make a shadow puppet. If not, drawing is plenty. If there’s time, hold the puppets up to the flashlight for a quick parade of dragons on the wall, then lights up once more. Close by circling up and saying the phrase together once. “See you next time.” No moral.

If it's not going well

When this one gets heavy

Silence or a shy child is normal. Let them watch, and don’t pull them into the shadows. Pushback (“that’s not a real dragon, it’s your hand!”) is exactly right: “You’re right! It’s just a hand. Isn’t that funny?” and keep going. A strong reaction, like a child who’s frightened by the roaring or the dim room, calls for the lights up, you close by, and a quiet “I’m right here, and we can be done with the dragon.” If a child says something that sounds like a real person or a real situation is frightening or hurting them (not a shadow, an actual thing), don’t probe in the moment. Stay warm, say something like “thank you for telling me, I care about that,” and follow the site’s mentor safety guidelines afterward, including your own mandatory-reporting obligations. This age often tells the real thing inside a game that feels safe, so take it seriously even though it arrived gently.

For the fuller tiered guidance — including what to do if someone discloses something serious — see For Mentors.

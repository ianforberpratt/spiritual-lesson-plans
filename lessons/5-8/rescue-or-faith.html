Hands That Ask First

A wobbly block tower, a friend who wants to help, and one easy habit: if someone is hurt, get a grown-up right now; otherwise, ask first.

Time25-30 min
Groupcircle-time or small group (4-12 kids)
AgeK-2
Formatin-person (works virtually with adult support)
UpdatedOctober 12, 2026
Also built for:Grade 3-5Grade 6-8TeenAge 21+

Assumptions & Caveats

Before you begin

What you should know

Little kids are natural helpers, and their help is wonderful, and also sometimes arrives as a pair of hands that just takes over: grabbing the crayon, fixing the drawing, finishing the tower. Nobody is in trouble for that. This lesson gives them one tiny habit that keeps their kindness and adds a pause: ask first. And one rule that never changes: if someone is hurt, a grown-up comes right now. That’s the whole lesson. There is no theology to explain at this age, and no wrap-up sentence to say; the blocks and the story do the work. You’ll need a few building blocks (or cups, or cardboard boxes) and nothing else.

What we're assuming

  • The room holds many families and many ways of talking about God, or not talking about God at all. This lesson works without ever needing the word.
  • Some kids are the “helper” at home, doing a lot for a little brother or a parent. Some are the ones who get everything done for them. Don’t ask anyone which.
  • Kids this age don’t need to answer in words. Pointing, building, and showing with hands is full participation.

Learning goals

What they walk away with

  1. Feels the difference between a helping hand that takes over and a helping hand that asks first.
  2. Leaves with one easy move to try: ask, “Do you want help?” before reaching in.
  3. Knows the one rule that is always okay: if someone is hurt, get a grown-up right now.

How to hold the room

Before you start talking

  • Keep your voice warm and light. This is about hands and blocks, not about who was bad at helping.
  • If a child says, “I always do it for my sister,” receive it gently: “That’s a loving thing. Today we’re just trying a different trick.”
  • Don’t ask any child to confess, explain, or defend. Doing the motions is plenty.
  • Offer an easy way to pass on anything: “You can just watch today. Watching counts.”
  • Don’t end with a spoken moral. When the tower is standing and the hands are traced, you’re done.

The lesson flow

Step by step

Each beat alternates a little input, a little doing, and a little reflecting. Skip, slow down, or stretch any of these to fit your group.

1
~7 min

The wobbly tower

Tell this short story in your own voice, with blocks (or cups) in front of you. Build slowly as you tell it.

Mo is building a tall tower. It’s almost as high as Mo’s chin. Mo’s friend Ravi sits down, watches for one second, and says, “Oh no, it’s wobbly!” Ravi’s hands zoom in. Ravi moves the blocks. Ravi puts the top one on. The tower is straight now! But Mo’s face goes scrunchy. “I was doing that,” says Mo.

Ravi feels a little confused. He was only trying to help!

Next day, Mo builds again. The tower wobbles again. This time Ravi sits on his hands. (Sit on your hands.) “Do you want help, or do you want me to watch?” Mo thinks. “Hold the bottom one still,” says Mo. Ravi holds it. Mo puts the top block on. The tower stands. Mo and Ravi both clap.

Let the story end there. Don’t ask what it “means.”

2
~7 min

Two kinds of hands

Show two hand shapes and give each a name the kids will remember: Grabby Hands (fingers reaching in, like a claw) and Asking Hands (open palms, held out, waiting). Do three quick rounds of call-and-response:

  • You say: “Mo’s tower is wobbling!” Ask: “Which hands zoomed in?” (Grabby.) “Which hands waited and asked?” (Asking.)
  • Pretend to spill a cup of crayons. “Someone wants to help. Show me Asking Hands!”
  • Pretend someone is stuck on a puzzle. “What do Asking Hands say?” Teach the words together: “Do you want help?”

Then, quietly and clearly, add the one rule. Say it slowly and have kids say it back:

“If somebody is hurt, we get a grown-up right now. Always.”

Practice it once, standing up and looking around the room for the grown-ups, as a pretend: “Somebody fell down! What do we do?” Everyone points to or walks toward a grown-up. This one isn’t a game of asking first. This one is speed.

3
~10 min

Build, then ask

Kids pair up (a grown-up can be someone’s partner if the numbers are uneven). Each pair gets a handful of blocks, cups, or cardboard boxes. One kid builds. The other sits with Asking Hands and, when the tower wobbles (it will, on purpose, if you hand out wobbly stuff), says, “Do you want help?” The builder answers however they like: “Yes, hold this,” or “No, I’ve got it,” or “Just watch.” The helper does exactly that. After a minute or two, trade jobs.

Walk around and give a quiet nod whenever you see a helper wait. If a helper grabs, don’t correct, just whisper, “Try the Asking Hands,” and let them try again. Both answers, “yes” and “no thanks,” are good answers. A “no thanks” isn’t a mean thing; it’s just a friend saying what they need.

If a tower falls, nobody is in trouble. Say cheerfully, “Ooh! Do you want to build it again?” and let the builder decide.

4
~4 min

Trace your helping hands

Give each child a half-sheet of paper. Have them trace one hand. Inside the hand, they draw or write one thing they could ask a friend this week (“Do you want help?” is plenty). Collect the hands in the middle of the circle, or let kids take them home. If your group uses a wallet card, hand it out now.

No closing speech. Stand the tower up one more time, together, and finish.

What you'll need

The materials

Wallet card Hands That Ask First Helping hands are wonderful. Asking hands are even better. Open printable handout →
  • Building blocks, plastic cups, or small cardboard boxes (enough for pairs)
  • Paper and crayons or markers
  • This lesson’s printable wallet card, “Hands That Ask First” — one per child to take home to a grown-up.

If it's not going well

When this one gets heavy

If the kids are wiggly or it all turns into knocking towers down, that’s normal at this age; shrink the activity, build one tower together, and keep the story short. If a child is very quiet, they might be the one who always helps (or is always helped); there’s no need to draw it out of them. Warm silence is fine. If a child gets upset or says someone at home does everything for them, or that they take care of a lot at home, receive it kindly (“Thank you for telling me”) and check in privately afterward. If a child tells you something that sounds like real harm, stop leading the activity, stay calm and warm, and follow the site’s mentor safety guidelines along with your own local mandatory-reporting obligations. This script doesn’t cover further than that.

For the fuller tiered guidance — including what to do if someone discloses something serious — see For Mentors.

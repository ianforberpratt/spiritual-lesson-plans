The Wobbly Wheel and the Cup

When a friend is sad and you feel wobbly inside, there’s a still place in the middle, and one small kind thing to do.

Time20–25 min
GroupCircle-time (4–15 kids)
AgeK-2
FormatIn-person, needs floor space
UpdatedNovember 17, 2026
Also built for:Grade 3-5Grade 6-8TeenAge 21+

Assumptions & Caveats

Before you begin

What you should know

Kids this age don’t need the word “center.” They already know what it feels like when a friend cries and their own tummy goes wobbly: they want to run, or say “it’s just sand!”, or fix it too fast. The lesson gives that wobble a picture (a wheel) and four little body moves, and ends with a real cup of water they pour for each other. Don’t explain the idea. Resist a wrap-up sentence (“so you see, boys and girls...”) — if the water and the wheel worked, they have it, and a spoken moral flattens what they just felt.

This is the little-kid cousin of Rescue or Faith? (helping from solid ground). If you’ve taught that one, this is the “and here’s what your hands do” follow-up. Nothing else is required prep.

What we're assuming

  • Every child here has seen a friend or a sibling cry, and has felt something about it, even if they can’t say what.
  • Some children will want to fix it fast; some will freeze; some will tell a grown-up. All of those are normal. None is “bad.”
  • Faith backgrounds vary. The words “Love” and “God” are used plainly and warmly; nobody is asked to say anything they don’t believe.
  • Some kids may tell a story about a real, current sadness at home. That is the lesson working. Receive it warmly (see “How to hold the room”).

Learning goals

What they walk away with

  1. A felt sense (not a definition) that you can be wobbly on the outside and still have a still place in the middle.
  2. Four little moves they can do with their hands and breath: wobble, peek, middle, cup.
  3. One small, real kind thing they could offer a friend this week, and the experience of giving a cup of water and receiving one.

How to hold the room

Before you start talking

  • Keep it moving. A story, a body move, a pour. If the room gets restless you’re lingering; go to the next beat.
  • Use your face and your voice, not just words. This age reads tone first.
  • Everything is optional. A child who just wants to watch is participating. Nobody has to say what the wobble is.
  • If a child shares something tender (“my brother is sad and I don’t know what to do”), say “I’m glad you told me” and keep going. Don’t turn it into a group conversation.

The lesson flow

Step by step

Each beat alternates a little input, a little doing, and a little reflecting. Skip, slow down, or stretch any of these to fit your group.

1
~2 min

The cup in the middle

Before anyone arrives, put a small paper cup of water on the floor in the middle of the circle. Don’t mention it. When everyone is seated: “I’m so glad you’re here, every one of you.” (Say names if the group is small.) Then, only if someone asks: “That’s for later.”

2
~6 min

The story

Tell it simply, in your own words. A stuffed animal or puppet can be Teo.

“Juno had a red wagon with a squeaky wheel. One windy day she went to the park, and her friend Teo had built the tallest sand castle you ever saw. Then a gust of wind came, and whoosh. The castle fell down. Teo’s face went crumply, and he started to cry. And Juno’s tummy went wobbly. She wanted to run away. She wanted to say, ‘It’s just sand!’ She wanted to build it back up herself, super fast, so Teo would stop crying. Then Juno did something small. She put her hand on her tummy and took a big breath. She thought, ‘I’m wobbly, and that’s okay.’ She thought about her wagon wheel. The outside bumps over every rock. But the middle of the wheel doesn’t move. It just holds. And she thought, ‘Love is in the middle. Love isn’t wobbly.’ Then Juno poured a little water from her bottle into the cup lid, and she sat down next to Teo, and she said, ‘Do you want a drink?’ That’s all. And Teo said, ‘Okay.’”

Ask, one at a time, not as a discussion: - “Where do you feel wobbly in your body when a friend is sad?” - “Who sits next to you when you’re sad?”

Let a few answer if they want to. No pressure.

3
~3 min

The wheel

Show a real wheel: a toy truck, a bike wheel, a paper plate on a pencil, or a hula hoop. Have one child be “the middle” by standing still holding the center while two or three walk slowly around the outside, bumping and wobbling. “Look. The outside bumps and wobbles. The middle just holds.” Let the child in the middle say, if they want to: “I’m the middle. I’m not moving.” Swap two or three times so several kids get to be the middle.

Read (no need to explain the words):

“I will fear no evil: for thou art with me.”

Psalm 23:4

“That’s a good thing to know when you’re wobbly. You are not alone in the middle.”

4
~5 min

Four little moves

Teach four moves with your hands. Say each one, do the motion, have them do it with you:

  1. Wobble. One hand on your tummy, a big breath. “I’m wobbly, and that’s okay.”
  2. Peek. Make a little window with your fingers and peek through it. “My worry says ___. It’s just a picture.”
  3. Middle. Press your hands flat together at your heart, or stand very still like the middle of a wheel. “Love is in the middle.”
  4. Cup. Open your hands like a cup. “One small kind thing.”

Do it together twice, then play it once: “Sam’s balloon pops and Sam is sad. What do we do? Wobble... peek... middle... cup.” Let the kids call out one small kind thing for the cup (“sit by him,” “give him my balloon,” “say ‘I’m here’”).

5
~4 min

Cups of cold water

Bring out the cup that was waiting. Pour (or have a grown-up pour) a little water into a small paper cup for each child. Read, gently:

“Whosoever shall give to drink unto one of these little ones a cup of cold water only... he shall in no wise lose his reward.”

Matthew 10:42

Then everyone offers a cup to the person next to them, using their name: “Would you like a cup of cold water, Maya?” The other child says “thank you.” Then they swap. If water isn’t practical, pass pretend cups with the same words, the same way, with the same seriousness.

6
~5 min

Craft: my wheel and my cup

Give each child the wheel handout. They color the wheel, put a heart in the middle, and draw one small kind thing on the cup. If you have a paper fastener or a pencil, they can cut the wheel out and spin it: the outside goes around; the middle stays. If you’re short on time, this is the beat to send home.

7

Closing

Circle up. Do the four moves one more time together, quietly: “Wobble, peek, middle, cup.” Then: “See you next time.”

  • A small paper cup of water per child, and a pitcher an adult pours (water only). Or pretend cups.

If it's not going well

When this one gets heavy

If the room is silly, let it be silly for a minute, then do the wheel again with the “bumping” a little slower. If a child cries (real tears, not play), pause, take a breath together, and check in with that child privately or with a helper while the others pour water. That’s the lesson happening, not interrupting it. If a child discloses something that sounds like real harm, neglect, or a grown-up who is frightening (not just an ordinary sad day), don’t probe in the moment: stay warm, say “thank you for telling me, I care about that,” and follow the site’s mentor safety guidelines (For Mentors page) afterward, including notifying the appropriate adult under your organization’s child-safety policy and your own local mandatory-reporting obligations. This age sometimes tells something real inside a story that feels safe.

For the fuller tiered guidance — including what to do if someone discloses something serious — see For Mentors.

Grade 3-5

The Worst Dragon Slayers’ Club

No sword, no sling, maybe a garden hose. Membership requires being a terrible dragon-fighter, and the club’s secret is what a dragon is really made of.

Time30-35 min
Grouppairs and small groups, full class (4-20 kids)
AgeGrade 3-5
Formatin-person or virtual
UpdatedSeptember 21, 2026
Also built for:K-2Grade 6-8TeenAge 21+

Assumptions & Caveats

Before you begin

What you should know

This age can compare two things side by side, the loud outside and the small inside, and that comparison is exactly what this lesson asks of them. Humor is the door. A club for terrible dragon-fighters turns “I’m scared of something big” into a shared joke instead of a private worry, and laughing at a dragon is one of the fastest ways to see its real size. The drawing does the quiet work: a worry becomes a picture that can be turned over.

Keep every worry kid-sized: a test, lunch, a kid being mean, something scary they heard about. This lesson doesn’t need the word “enemy,” and it doesn’t need any news topic. The idea that a person is never the dragon (the scary voice or feeling is) is built in as a club rule.

Nothing you don’t already have: index cards and paper.

What we're assuming

  • Every kid in the room has had a “what if” voice loud enough to feel like a dragon, including the ones who would never say so out loud.
  • Someone may pick a worry that’s really about a person: a kid who’s mean, or something at home. The rule for the day is that the dragon is the scary feeling or voice, never the person. And being kind is not the same as staying close. If someone keeps hurting them, telling a trusted adult isn’t “not being loving enough.” It’s just what you do to stay safe. See also When Someone Feels Like the Enemy.
  • Faith backgrounds vary. “Truth” and “Love” are offered as two names for God, as an idea to try, not something anyone has to say they believe.

Learning goals

What they walk away with

  1. Can name one “what if” worry and watch it shrink to its real, smaller size.
  2. Can say the two tools in their own words: stand steady and find what’s true, and do one kind, useful thing.
  3. Leaves with a club card to keep and a real worry to try the tools on this week.

How to hold the room

Before you start talking

  • Go first with your own confession, one that’s genuinely silly and genuinely true. An adult being bad at fighting dragons in public is the whole permission slip.
  • Keep the worry-drawing optional and private. Kids may draw a pretend worry or a “medium” one, and nobody has to say which. Never rank worries, and don’t fix them.
  • Don’t add a spoken moral after the flip. The flip is the point.
  • If a kid says “but the dragon’s real,” agree with the feeling: “The worry is real. The costume is what’s bigger than the thing.”

The lesson flow

Step by step

Each beat alternates a little input, a little doing, and a little reflecting. Skip, slow down, or stretch any of these to fit your group.

1
~5 min

Join the club

“Today we’re starting a club, and there’s exactly one requirement: you have to be a terrible dragon-fighter.” Tell your own confession (a garden hose is a fine one). Hand out index cards. Each kid writes their name and “my worst dragon-fighting weapon” (a spatula, a sleeping bag, my little brother). Share a few out loud and enjoy the laughing. “Welcome to the Worst Dragon Slayers’ Club. Here’s the twist: it turns out the best dragon-fighters don’t use weapons at all. Let’s find out why.”

2
~7 min

Draw the scariest dragon

Read one line from the very last book of the Bible: “And there appeared another wonder in heaven; and behold a great red dragon, having seven heads and ten horns” (Revelation 12:3, KJV). “That dragon is dramatic on purpose.” In pairs, on a big piece of paper, draw the biggest, scariest dragon you can, with seven heads. On each head, write one “what if” the dragon whispers: “What if I fail the test?” “What if nobody sits with me?” “What if it all goes wrong?” They can be pretend or real, and nobody has to say which. Give it about four minutes.

3
~8 min

Flip the dragon over

“Now the costume comes off.” Say: “A woman named Mary Baker Eddy wrote a lot about this dragon. She said it stands for a lie, a big, scary costume with no real power of its own.” Then: “Turn your dragon over. On the back, draw one small box. Inside it, write the real worry underneath, in the smallest, truest words you can.” (Examples: “I’m afraid I’ll be alone at lunch.” “I’m afraid of a bad grade.” “I don’t want to mess up in front of everybody.”) Partners have one job: ask “What’s the real worry inside?” and then “Is it as big as the dragon?” Let each kid answer for themselves, then switch. No fixing, no advice.

4
~8 min

The two things a dragon can’t fight

Read this once, slowly, without explaining it:

“Truth and Love prevail against the dragon because the dragon cannot war with them.”

Science and Health, Mary Baker Eddy, p. 567:10–12

“That’s from a book about what God is. Truth and Love are two names for God, and it says a dragon can’t even fight them.” Name the two tools out loud: Truth is standing steady and finding what’s really there (you just did that when you flipped the dragon over). Love is doing one kind, useful thing about it. Then, in the box on the front of the card, each kid writes one kind, useful thing for their worry: “Ask someone to sit with me.” “Study for ten minutes.” “Tell a grown-up.” “Say something kind to someone who’s scared.” Add the safety line plainly: “Kind and useful sometimes means telling a grown-up. If someone keeps being mean or hurts you, that’s not a dragon you have to face by yourself.”

5
~5 min

The club rules

Hand out the wallet card (it doubles as the membership card). Read the three club rules together: We go first with our own worst dragon-fighting. We ask “What’s the real worry?” before we do anything else. Nobody is ever the dragon. Ask each kid to put their card somewhere they’ll see it this week, in a backpack pocket, on a mirror, or in a shoe. Close the club.

If it's not going well

When this one gets heavy

Silence or a shy pair is normal. Let them draw without talking and give them a minute. Pushback (“dragons aren’t real” or “this is for little kids”) is welcome and fair: “You’re right, they aren’t. The scary feelings are. This is just a fun way to look at them.” Older kids sometimes settle in if you make them the club’s official skeptics. A strong emotional reaction, like a kid who tears up about a real worry, calls for a pause and a private word: “Thank you for telling me. You don’t have to draw any more today.” If a child discloses something that sounds like real harm, neglect, or someone frightening in their life, don’t probe in the moment. Stay warm, say something like “thank you for telling me, I care about that,” and follow the site’s mentor safety guidelines afterward, including your own mandatory-reporting obligations.

For the fuller tiered guidance — including what to do if someone discloses something serious — see For Mentors.

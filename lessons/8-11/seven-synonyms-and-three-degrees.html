A lesson · Seven Names, One Journey

Upper Elementary (Grades 3–5)

Seven Names, One Journey

One multi-tool, seven real uses — and the honest, ordinary journey from a hard day toward actually understanding something.

Time35–40 min
GroupSmall group or full class (4–20 kids)
AgeUpper Elementary (Grades 3–5)
FormatIn-person or virtual
Also built for:5–8 · Early Elementary (K–2)11–14 · Middle School (Grades 6–8)14–21 · Teen & Early College21+ · College Student & Adult (21+)

Assumptions & Caveats

Before you begin

What you should know

This is the first band where both halves of this lesson show up together — the seven names, and the three-degrees journey (BUILD-BRIEF.md 6.2). Keep both at a concrete, comparison-based level: this age can compare two systems side by side (a multi-tool with several uses, a skill they’re learning) but isn’t ready for abstract theological reasoning about why God needs seven names instead of one — that question belongs to older bands.

For the three degrees, use something every kid this age has actually lived: learning a skill that starts wobbly, gets better with real effort, and eventually stops feeling like effort at all. Riding a bike is the suggested anchor, but swap in whatever fits your group — a sport, an instrument, reading.

What we're assuming

  • Most kids this age have learned at least one real skill recently enough to remember what “wobbly and bad at it” felt like, compared to “actually good at it now.”
  • This isn’t a lesson about being a beginner versus an expert as a permanent identity. The point is narrower: everybody moves through all three stages, over and over, their whole life — it’s not shameful to be in the wobbly stage of something.
  • Some kids may already feel real pressure to skip straight to “good at it” and feel embarrassed by the struggle stage. Name that directly if it comes up.

Learning goals

What they walk away with

  1. Compare a multi-tool’s several real uses to God’s seven real names — one thing, several true jobs, not confusing.
  2. Name, in their own words, what the three stages of learning something new actually feel like.
  3. Carry one question into the week: “Am I in the wobbly stage of something right now — and is that actually okay?”
  4. Locate one real skill or moment in their own life somewhere on that journey.

How to hold the room

Before you start talking

  • Let them do the comparing rather than lecturing the connection — ask “what’s this multi-tool like?” and let them land on “it’s one thing that does a bunch of stuff.”
  • Small groups of 2–3 for the discussion moments work better than one big circle at this age.
  • If a kid says learning something new feels embarrassing, don’t rush past that — it’s exactly what Beat 3 is there to reframe.

The lesson flow

Step by step

Each beat alternates a little input, a little doing, and a little reflecting. Skip, slow down, or stretch any of these to fit your group.

1
~5 min

Warm-up: one tool, many jobs

Show or describe a multi-tool (or a smartphone, or a Swiss Army knife) — one object, several real uses. Ask: “Is it confusing that one tool does more than one job? Or does that make it more useful?”

2
~8 min

Seven names for God

“God is like that, but with names instead of blades — seven real names, each one true, each one useful for something different.” Introduce the seven, briefly:

Mind, Spirit, Soul, Principle, Life, Truth, Love.

“God is incorporeal, divine, supreme, infinite Mind, Spirit, Soul, Principle, Life, Truth, Love.”

Science and Health, p. 465

Ask (pairs, 2 min): “Pick one of the seven names. What’s one time this week you could have used exactly that — like Truth when something felt confusing, or Love when you needed to feel liked?”

3
~10 min

The bike-learning journey

Ask: “Think of something you learned that started out wobbly and hard — riding a bike, a sport, an instrument, reading. What did the very beginning feel like?”

Let a few kids share. Then walk through three stages together, writing them where everyone can see:

  1. Wobbly and scared — you might fall, it feels hard, sometimes embarrassing.
  2. Getting the hang of it — still work, but you’re actually improving.
  3. You don’t even think about it anymore — it just happens.

“Christian Science calls this a journey through three stages too — struggling, working at it, and finally really understanding something. Nobody skips the wobbly stage. Everybody who’s good at something now used to be bad at it.”

“In the third degree mortal mind disappears, and man as God’s reflection appears.”

Science and Health, p. 115

4
~7 min

Map it onto real life

Give each kid paper folded into three columns labeled “Wobbly,” “Getting it,” and “Got it.” In each column, they write or draw one thing from their life that’s currently at that stage — it can be a skill, a friendship, a feeling, anything.

Pairs share one column of their choice with a partner (not the whole group).

5
~5 min

One question to carry

Teach the question: “Am I in the wobbly stage of something right now — and is that actually okay?”

“This week, if you feel embarrassed about being bad at something new, try asking yourself that question. Everyone who’s good at it now started wobbly too.”

6

Closing

“You have seven true names for God to reach for, and you’re allowed to be in the wobbly stage of anything. That’s not behind — that’s just the first part of the journey.”

What you'll need

The materials

  • A multi-tool, Swiss Army knife, or smartphone for Beat 1 (optional but helpful)
  • Paper (folded into three columns) and pencils/markers, one per child
  • A whiteboard or poster paper for Beat 3

If it's not going well

When this one gets heavy

If a child seems more ashamed than curious about being “bad” at something — especially if it sounds like real pressure from home, a coach, or harsh self-talk — don’t press for more in the group setting. Follow up individually after class, and consult the site’s mentor safety guidelines (For Mentors page) if it sounds like it goes beyond ordinary kid perfectionism.

For the fuller tiered guidance — including what to do if someone discloses something serious — see For Mentors.

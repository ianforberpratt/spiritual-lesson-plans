Grade 3-5

When Someone Is Being Mean

You can be kind to someone without having to be their friend.

Time30–38 min
Group1:1 or group
AgeGrade 3-5
Formatin-person or virtual
UpdatedAugust 19, 2026
Also built for:Grade 6-8TeenAge 21+

Assumptions & Caveats

Before you begin

What you should know

This age is dealing with real, concrete friction — a kid who teases, excludes, or is a repeat source of trouble at school or in the neighborhood. Don’t reach for the word “enemy” here; it’s bigger and scarier than what’s actually happening. Keep it to “someone who’s being mean.” Don’t try to teach the full “loving isn’t liking, excusing, staying close, or trusting” sort — that’s four ideas layered on top of each other, and this age reasons in concrete, one-at-a-time terms. One dial is plenty: you can be kind to someone without having to be their close friend. Being kind and being close aren’t the same thing.

What we're assuming

  • Almost everyone in the room has someone right now who’s been mean to them, or has a hard time with — a kid who teases, leaves them out, or is a repeat source of friction. We’re not asking anyone to say who.
  • Nobody has to defend the other kid, excuse what happened, or decide to be friends with them by the end of this lesson.
  • Some children may bring up something more serious than ordinary friction. That’s covered in “If it’s not going well” below.

Learning goals

What they walk away with

  1. Knows that noticing someone has been mean to them, and feeling upset about it, is honest — not something wrong with them.
  2. Understands that being kind to someone and being their close friend are two different things — you can do one without the other.
  3. Has practiced naming one small, safe, kind thing they could do or say toward someone who’s been hard to be around.
  4. Knows clearly and without doubt that if someone keeps hurting them, telling a trusted adult is always the right move — and that doing so is not “not being kind enough.”

How to hold the room

Before you start talking

  • Keep language concrete and simple: “someone who’s being mean,” not “enemy” or “adversary.” Big words here can make an ordinary school problem feel scarier than it is.
  • This is not about forcing forgiveness, and definitely not about deciding to become friends with someone who’s been unkind. Nobody has to like the other kid by the end of this.
  • If a child names an example, don’t ask for more detail than they offer. Keep it general and let them stay as vague as they want.
  • Watch for children trying to “solve” the other kid’s behavior or explain it away — gently steer back to what the child in front of you can do, not what the other kid should do.

The lesson flow

Step by step

Each beat alternates a little input, a little doing, and a little reflecting. Skip, slow down, or stretch any of these to fit your group.

1
~5–7 min

Someone who’s been mean

Think of someone — you don’t have to say who — who’s been mean to you, left you out, or been hard to get along with. You don’t have to decide anything about it right now. Just notice the feeling is there. That’s not a bad thing to notice — it’s just true.

2
~8–10 min

Kind and close friend are not the same thing

Jesus taught something people sometimes get wrong: “love your enemies.” That doesn’t mean you have to like everybody, or be best friends with everybody, or spend all your time with someone who’s mean to you. It means you can still be kind — even to someone who isn’t being kind back — without that person becoming your close friend. Discuss (in pairs): Can you think of a way to be kind to someone without having to be their close friend? (For example: not saying mean things back, not joining in if others are teasing, saying hi even if you don’t sit together at lunch.)

3
~10–13 min

What kind can look like

As a group or in pairs, brainstorm small, safe, kind things a person could do that don’t require becoming close with someone: not laughing along if others are unkind to them, not spreading rumors back, saying something neutral and polite instead of ignoring them completely. Kind doesn’t mean doing whatever they want, playing every game with them, or pretending everything’s fine. Discuss (in pairs): What’s one kind thing on our list you could actually picture yourself doing this week?

Here’s one more thing worth knowing: when you keep replaying mean thoughts about someone — thinking of them as “the bad guy” over and over — that can actually make you feel worse, not better. Kind of like carrying a heavy backpack around all day. You can still stay away from someone who’s being unkind. But you don’t have to also carry them around in your head as “the enemy.” Remembering that they’re still a whole person — even while they’re doing something not kind right now — can make that backpack a little lighter for you. Discuss (in pairs): Have you ever noticed that thinking really mean thoughts about someone kept the yucky feeling around longer? What happened when you let some of it go?

4
~7–8 min

If it keeps happening

Close with this, said plainly: being kind to someone never, ever means you have to keep letting them hurt you. If someone keeps being mean to you, or does something that scares you or hurts you, the right thing to do is tell a trusted grown-up — a parent, teacher, or Sunday School teacher. That’s not “not being kind enough.” That’s just what you do to stay safe, and it’s always okay to do it.

What you'll need

The materials

  • Paper and crayons/pens, or a shared whiteboard if meeting virtually
  • Optional: a simple two-column handout — “Kind” and “Close Friend” — for children to sort example actions into

If it's not going well

When this one gets heavy

Keep this simple and direct for this age: if a child describes something that sounds like ongoing bullying, hitting, or anything that scares them or makes them feel unsafe, stop the lesson activity and respond as a caring adult first. Reassure them clearly that telling you, or telling another trusted grown-up, was the right thing to do — it is never “tattling” and it is never a failure to be kind. Follow the site’s mentor safety guidelines (For Mentors page) for next steps, including when and how to loop in a parent or guardian.

For the fuller tiered guidance — including what to do if someone discloses something serious — see For Mentors.

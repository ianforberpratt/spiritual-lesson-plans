A lesson · Is God Keeping Score?

Upper Elementary (Grades 3–5)

Is God Keeping Score?

The game-points version of one truth taught at every age here: God’s love isn’t a score that goes up or down.

Time30–35 min
GroupSmall group or full class (4–20 kids)
AgeUpper Elementary (Grades 3–5)
FormatIn-person or virtual
UpdatedAugust 19, 2026
Also built for:5–8 · Early Elementary (K–2)11–14 · Middle School (Grades 6–8)21+ · College Student & Adult (21+)

Assumptions & Caveats

Before you begin

What you should know

This age group is right at the edge of understanding metaphor and analogy — old enough to compare two systems side by side, not yet ready for pure abstraction like “God’s nature is Love, not a reward.” The bridge here is something they already understand deeply: points. Video game scores, app streaks, reading logs, grades starting to show up on report cards. They know exactly how it feels when a score resets or a streak breaks.

Unlike the youngest kids, this age can do real (if simple) discussion — pairs or small groups work better than a single open floor, since not every 9-year-old will speak up in a big group but most will talk to a partner.

What we're assuming

  • Most kids this age can name at least one thing that keeps score in their life — a game, an app, a reading chart, a grade.
  • Some kids may already feel real pressure around scores (perfectionism, competitive siblings, grades starting to matter to adults around them). Stay alert to a kid who seems more anxious than curious during this lesson — that’s worth a gentle follow-up after, one-on-one.
  • This isn’t the moment to say grades or games are bad. The point is narrower: whatever those systems are for, God’s love isn’t one of them.

Learning goals

What they walk away with

  1. Name, out loud, one place in their life that works like a scoreboard.
  2. Understand — as an actual comparison, not just a phrase — the difference between “loved because I performed well” and “loved no matter what.”
  3. Carry one question into the week: “Am I trying to win this, or trying to understand it?”
  4. Locate one real relationship (a friend, a sibling, a teammate) where this applies.

How to hold the room

Before you start talking

  • Let them do the comparing. Don’t tell them the answer — ask “which one sounds more like a video game, and which one sounds more like a good friend?” and let them land on it.
  • Small groups of 2–3 for the discussion moments work much better than one big circle at this age — more kids actually think out loud that way.
  • If the room gets silly around the “game points” opener, that’s fine and expected — ride it into the lesson rather than fighting it.

The lesson flow

Step by step

Each beat alternates a little input, a little doing, and a little reflecting. Skip, slow down, or stretch any of these to fit your group.

1
~5 min

Warm-up: name a scoreboard

Ask: “Think of a game, app, or chart that gives you points or a score for doing well. What happens to your score when you mess up or lose a round?”

Let several kids share quickly, round-robin style. Write a few examples on the board if you have one.

Ask: “How does it feel when your score goes down, or your streak breaks?”

2
~7 min

A story with two friends

Tell or read aloud:

“Imagine two kids on the same soccer team. One friend only wants to hang out with you on days you score a goal — texts you afterward, says ‘great game,’ invites you over. But on days you mess up, don’t score, or the team loses — that friend goes quiet. Doesn’t say much. You start to notice you’re only ‘good enough’ on the good days.

The other friend is different. Doesn’t care if you scored or not, won or lost. Still wants to sit with you at lunch. Still thinks you’re great. Same amount, every day, win or lose.”

Ask (pairs, 2 min): “Which friend actually sounds like a good friend? Have you ever felt like the first kind of friend was watching you — waiting to see if you’d earn it?”

3
~8 min

Naming it as God

“A lot of people picture God like the first friend — only pleased with you on the days you’re ‘good,’ quiet or disappointed on the days you’re not. Christian Science teaches something different: God’s nature actually is love. Not a score you can raise or lower. Read this together:”

“The Lord your God is with you... he will take great delight in you; in his love he will no longer rebuke you, but will rejoice over you with singing.”

Zephaniah 3:17

Ask: “Not just ‘okay with you’ — rejoicing, singing over you. What’s the difference between someone who tolerates your score and someone who sings about you no matter what the score is?”

“Man is God’s reflection, needing no cultivation, but ever beautiful and complete.”

Science and Health, p. 527:4

”‘Needing no cultivation’ — that means not needing to be improved first to be loved. Already complete. Already loved.”

4
~7 min

Draw it out

Give each kid paper folded in half. On one side: draw or write what a “scorekeeper God” would feel like — a scoreboard, a chart, whatever comes to mind. On the other side: draw or write what a God who “rejoices over you with singing” feels like.

Pairs share their two sides with a partner (not the whole group) — 2 minutes each way.

5
~5 min

One question to carry

Teach the question: “Am I trying to win this, or trying to understand it?”

“This week, if you’re in an argument with a friend or sibling and you notice you’re trying really hard to be ‘right’ — try asking yourself that question. Not because winning is bad, but because you don’t actually need to win to be loved.”

6

Closing

“You are not being scored. You’re already fully loved — same amount today as any other day.”

What you'll need

The materials

  • Paper (folded in half) and pencils/markers, one per child
  • A whiteboard or poster paper for Beat 1 (optional)

If it's not going well

When this one gets heavy

If a child seems more distressed than curious when talking about grades, competition, or performance — especially if it sounds like real pressure from home, a coach, or a pattern of harsh self-talk — don’t press for more in the group setting. Follow up with them individually after class, and consult the site’s mentor safety guidelines (For Mentors page) if what you’re hearing sounds like it goes beyond ordinary kid perfectionism.

For the fuller tiered guidance — including what to do if someone discloses something serious — see For Mentors.

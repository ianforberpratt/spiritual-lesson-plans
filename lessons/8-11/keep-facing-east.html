Tightrope or Compass?

On a tightrope, one wobble and you’re out. With a compass, you can find your way from anywhere in the room — even after you get spun around.

Time25-30 min
Groupsmall group or full class (4-16 kids), with pair work
AgeGrade 3-5
Formatin-person (needs a little floor space)
UpdatedSeptember 28, 2026
Also built for:K-2Grade 6-8TeenAge 21+

Assumptions & Caveats

Before you begin

What you should know

Kids this age know exactly what it feels like to be on a tightrope: the video game where one mistake sends you back to level one, the spelling test, the recital, the feeling of trying really hard to be “good” and worrying about slipping. Some of them already carry a quiet sense that God works like that too — one narrow line, and they’re probably off it.

This lesson lets them feel two different systems side by side, which is right where this age thinks best: a masking-tape tightrope you can fall off, and a paper sun on the wall that everyone can point to from wherever they’re standing. You don’t need to tell them which one is “God’s way.” Let them compare the two, and let them notice for themselves.

Nothing you don’t already have for prep: a strip of masking tape on the floor and a paper sun taped to one wall.

What we're assuming

- The room holds kids from many faith backgrounds, and some from none. Nobody is asked what they believe about God, and nobody is told their family’s beliefs are wrong. - Some kids are already very hard on themselves. Nobody is asked to share a specific thing they got wrong — the examples stay general (games, tests, trying). - Not every kid can walk a line comfortably. Anyone can walk it with a hand on a partner’s shoulder, trace it with a finger on a paper version, or be the “judge.”

Learning goals

What they walk away with

1. Feels, in their own body, the difference between “one wobble and you’re out” and “you can always find your way back to facing the sun.” 2. Notices that everyone in the room can point at the same sun from different spots — and nobody is pointing wrong. 3. Has a simple question to carry: “Which way am I facing?” 4. Leaves with a paper compass they made, with their own words on it.

How to hold the room

Before you start talking

- Keep the tightrope beat light and a little silly — the point is to feel the pressure, not to make anyone fail in front of the group. - In the compass beat, move kids to very different spots on purpose so the “everyone points differently at the same sun” moment is obvious. - Discussion here is simple and concrete: “What did that feel like?” and “Which one would you rather have?” — not big abstract questions. - Anyone can pass on any activity and still be part of it as a judge or helper.

The lesson flow

Step by step

Each beat alternates a little input, a little doing, and a little reflecting. Skip, slow down, or stretch any of these to fit your group.

1
~6 min

The tightrope

Lay a long strip of masking tape across the floor. “This is a tightrope. Walk it heel to toe. If you step off — you’re out.” Let a few volunteers try, maybe with a book balanced on their head. Keep it playful. Then ask the room two quick, concrete questions:

- “How did it feel to walk that?” (Nervous, wobbly, careful, scared to mess up.) - “Where in real life do you ever feel like you’re on a tightrope?” Take general answers — a game, a test, a recital, trying to be good. No one needs to share a specific story.

2
~7 min

The compass game

Point to the paper sun on the wall. “That’s the sun. It’s in the east, where the sun comes up.” Scatter kids to very different places in the room. “Point to the sun!” Everyone points. Ask: “Is everybody pointing the same direction?” (No — they’re all pointing different ways.) “Is anybody pointing wrong?” (No — they’re all pointing at the sun.)

Move them to new spots and do it again. Then: “Close your eyes. Spin around slowly two times.” (Carefully, and seated spinning is fine.) “Now open your eyes and find the sun.” They’ll turn and find it. Let them notice how quickly they got back.

3
~7 min

Side by side, in pairs

Give each pair a paper folded in half. One side is labeled Tightrope, the other Compass. Ask them to write or draw what each one “says” to you. Examples to get them started if they need it:

  • Tightrope: “One wobble and you’re out.” Compass: “You can find it from anywhere.”
  • Tightrope: “Only one person fits on it at a time.” Compass: “Everybody can point at the sun.”

Invite a few pairs to share one line from each side. Then ask just one comparison question: “If you got to choose how God works, which one would you choose?” Let them answer. Don’t correct anyone who picks the tightrope — ask what they like about it and listen.

4
~4 min

Two lines from the Bible

Read these two lines aloud, simply, with no explanation afterward:

His compassions fail not. They are new every morning: great is thy faithfulness.

Lamentations 3:22–23, KJV

He maketh his sun to rise on the evil and on the good.

Matthew 5:45, KJV

If a kid asks, “Does that mean the sun comes up even for people who mess up?” you can simply say, “That’s what it says,” and let it sit.

5
~6 min

Make a pocket compass

Each kid draws a simple compass on a small square of paper — a circle with an arrow pointing to a little sun. Around the edge, they write the question: “Which way am I facing?” They can decorate it however they like. Let them keep it. No wrap-up speech — the compass is the wrap-up.

What you'll need

The materials

- A long strip of masking tape (or a jump rope laid straight) - A paper sun taped to one wall (ideally the actual east side of the room, if you know it) - Paper, pens or markers; small squares of paper for the pocket compasses - This lesson’s printable handout, “A Road or a Sunrise?” — the two-column side reads well at this age; hand it out at the end to take home

If it's not going well

When this one gets heavy

Silence during the pair work is normal — walk around and offer a starter line. Pushback (“the tightrope is more fun!”) is completely fine — agree that it’s exciting, and ask whether they’d want to live on one all day. A strong emotional reaction (a kid who gets upset about stepping off the line, or who says “I always mess up”) calls for a pause, a calm word, and letting them be the judge for a while: “Stepping off a piece of tape doesn’t mean anything about you.” If a kid says something that points to real harm — being hurt, being very afraid at home, being told they’re bad or unwanted — don’t ask for details in front of the group. Stay warm, thank them, find a private moment, and follow the site’s mentor safety guidelines along with your own church’s child-safety and mandatory-reporting obligations. Kids this age sometimes share real worries through a joke or a drawing; take it seriously even when it arrives lightly.

For the fuller tiered guidance — including what to do if someone discloses something serious — see For Mentors.

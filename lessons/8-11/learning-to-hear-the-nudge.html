Learning to Hear the Nudge

The quiet voice vs. the loud worried voice — a first practice in noticing which one is talking.

Time25–30 min
Groupsmall-group or full class (4–20 kids)
AgeGrade 3-5
Formatin-person or virtual
UpdatedAugust 19, 2026
Also built for:Grade 6-8TeenAge 21+

Assumptions & Caveats

Before you begin

What you should know

This age can absolutely notice a feeling inside them, but they can’t yet reliably classify or compare abstract internal states side by side the way an older kid can — “guidance feels calm and certain, pressure feels rushed and controlling” is too abstract as stated. What works instead is two concrete, almost cartoon-simple voices: a quiet voice and a loud worried voice. Anchor it in something they already live in daily — recess, trying something new, or a friend pushing an idea that feels off. Don’t ask them to compare traditions or name “a word for it” the way older kids do later — that’s a step for later, not this one, and it will produce confused silence here rather than insight. Do keep the safety line explicit and simple: this isn’t about a voice telling you to hurt yourself or someone else, and that always goes straight to a grown-up, every time.

What we're assuming

  • Most kids this age can recall a specific moment of hesitation — “should I do this or not?” — even if they’ve never named it as a “voice.”
  • Kids this age are highly susceptible to peer influence; a friend’s idea that “feels off” is a live, current experience for most of them, not a hypothetical.
  • Some kids may connect this to a real, unresolved situation — being pressured to go along with something, feeling uneasy about a friend group. Stay alert for a kid who brings up something heavier than “should I try the monkey bars.”
  • This is a first practice, not a mastery goal. Missing the quiet voice sometimes is normal and not a big deal — say so out loud during the lesson.

Learning goals

What they walk away with

  1. Name, in their own words, the difference between a quiet voice and a loud worried voice inside them.
  2. Recall one real moment — recess, a friend’s idea, trying something new — where they noticed one of these voices.
  3. Carry one simple question into the week: “Is this the quiet voice, or the loud worried voice?”
  4. Know clearly and without confusion: a voice that tells you to hurt yourself or someone else is never the quiet voice — that always goes straight to a grown-up.

How to hold the room

Before you start talking

  • Use very concrete, sensory language — “quiet” and “loud,” “calm” and “worried” — not “guidance” or “discernment.”
  • Simple pair discussion works better than open floor at this age; not every 9-year-old will talk in front of the whole group, but most will talk to one partner.
  • If a kid brings up something that sounds like real, current pressure from a friend group, don’t try to solve it live in front of the class — note it and follow up one-on-one after.
  • Keep the tone light where you can (voices, pretend, even silly voices are fine) — this isn’t a heavy lesson, it’s a first noticing practice.

The lesson flow

Step by step

Each beat alternates a little input, a little doing, and a little reflecting. Skip, slow down, or stretch any of these to fit your group.

1
~5 min

Two voices

Tell the kids: “Everybody has two voices inside them sometimes. One is quiet and calm — it doesn’t yell, it just kind of... knows. The other is loud and worried — it talks fast, and it really wants you to do what it says right now.”

Discuss (open floor, quick): “Has anyone ever felt like there were two different feelings pulling at you about the same thing?” Let a few kids answer quickly, then move on.

2
~7 min

A recess story

Tell or read aloud: “Imagine you’re at recess and a friend says, ‘Let’s sneak over past the fence where we’re not supposed to go — it’ll be fun, nobody will notice.’ Part of you feels excited. But another part of you feels a little quiet ‘wait’ — not scared exactly, just... unsure.”

Discuss (pairs, 2 min): “Have you ever felt that quiet ‘wait’ feeling? What did it feel like — in your tummy, your chest, your head?”

3
~6 min

Telling them apart

“The quiet voice usually doesn’t yell. It doesn’t need you to hurry. It just sort of sits there, calm, even if you try to ignore it. The loud worried voice talks fast, wants you to decide right now, and gets louder the more you try to ignore it.”

Discuss (pairs, 3 min): “Think of a time you tried something new — a new food, a new game, raising your hand in class. Was there a quiet voice or a loud worried voice happening? What did each one say?”

4
~5 min

Practice on something small

“This week, try noticing — just noticing, that’s it — when you feel one of these voices. Maybe it’s about trying out for something, sitting with someone new at lunch, or deciding whether to go along with a friend’s idea. You don’t have to get it right. Just notice: was that the quiet voice, or the loud worried voice?”

5
~3 min

The one thing that’s never the quiet voice

Say clearly and simply: “One more really important thing. If a voice inside you ever tells you to hurt yourself, or to hurt somebody else — that is never the quiet voice. That’s not what we’re talking about today. If that ever happens, you tell a grown-up you trust right away, every single time.”

6

Closing

“You already have this quiet voice. Today was just about starting to notice it.”

What you'll need

The materials

  • Nothing required
  • Optional: paper and crayons if you want kids to draw their “quiet voice” and “loud worried voice” as characters

If it's not going well

When this one gets heavy

If a child discloses that an inner voice is telling them to hurt themselves or someone else, or if what surfaces sounds like real fear of a person or situation rather than ordinary kid uncertainty, stop the activity and address it directly and calmly with that child one-on-one — don’t fold it back into the group lesson. Never suggest that something bad happening was because the child “didn’t listen well enough” to their quiet voice; that idea has no place near a child processing something frightening. Follow the site’s mentor safety guidelines (For Mentors page) and loop in the appropriate adult per your program’s child-safety policy.

For the fuller tiered guidance — including what to do if someone discloses something serious — see For Mentors.

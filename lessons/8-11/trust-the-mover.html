When Your Plan Falls Through

Not making a team, a canceled sleepover, a game rained out — and something good that happened anyway that you didn’t see coming.

Time25–30 min
Groupsmall-group (4–16 kids)
AgeUpper Elementary (Grades 3–5)
Formatin-person or virtual
UpdatedAugust 19, 2026
Also built for:5–8 · Early Elementary (K–2)11–14 · Middle School (Grades 6–8)14–21 · Teen & Early College21+ · College Student & Adult (21+)

Assumptions & Caveats

Before you begin

What you should know

This age can hold a little more than 5-8 year-olds — they can pair up and talk about a real example, and they can start to generalize a bit (“sometimes plans change and something good happens anyway” as a small rule, not just a single story). But they’re not ready for the self-reflective work the 11-14 version does (checking whether they’ve made themselves the center of a situation) — that’s still a couple years off developmentally. Keep this concrete: real disappointments they’ve actually had, a simple structured pair conversation, not an open floor.

Not making a team or getting un-invited from something is a real social sting at this age, not a small thing. Don’t breeze past it to get to the lesson — let it be named as genuinely disappointing before moving toward “and something good happened too.”

What we're assuming

  • Most kids this age have a specific, nameable disappointment on hand: not making a team, a sleepover that got canceled, a game that got rained out, a friend who couldn’t come to something.
  • Kids this age can do a short structured pair-share with a partner, but full open-floor group discussion is still a stretch for most of the room.
  • This lesson is for everyday disappointment, not a bigger loss. If something heavier comes up, see “If it’s not going well” below.

Learning goals

What they walk away with

  1. Names one real, specific disappointment (a team, a sleepover, a rained-out game) without being rushed past it.
  2. Hears and can repeat the idea that a changed plan isn’t proof that nothing good is happening.
  3. Talks with a partner about a time a plan changed and something good happened that they didn’t expect.
  4. Leaves with one phrase to remember: “The plan changed. The good didn’t stop.”

How to hold the room

Before you start talking

  • Let the disappointment be real before moving to “and something good happened anyway.” Don’t silver-line it.
  • Keep discussion in pairs, not whole-group — this age is more honest one-on-one than in front of everyone.
  • If a kid’s example is about being excluded (not invited, cut from a team), take that seriously — it can carry real hurt, not just mild disappointment.

The lesson flow

Step by step

Each beat alternates a little input, a little doing, and a little reflecting. Skip, slow down, or stretch any of these to fit your group.

1
~5 min

Naming it

Quick check-in: “Has a plan of yours ever changed and you were really disappointed? Raise your hand.” Let a couple of kids share a one-sentence example out loud if they want to. No pressure.

2
~8 min

A story

Tell a short, relatable story (in your own words): a kid who really wanted to make a team and didn’t, and felt awful about it — and then ended up finding a different activity, or a friend, or a skill they wouldn’t have found otherwise. Keep it real, not too neatly wrapped — the disappointment in the story should still sound like it hurt.

“That’s the thing about a plan changing — it doesn’t mean something good stopped happening. Sometimes it means something good is about to happen in a way you couldn’t have planned for.”

3
~8 min

Pair discussion

Pair kids up. Give them this one question to talk through together:

Discuss: “Has a plan ever changed on you, and something good happened anyway that you didn’t expect?”

Give each partner a turn. Walk around and listen in, but don’t require pairs to report back to the whole group unless someone wants to.

4
~5 min

The phrase

Bring the group back together. “You are not the one who has to make every good thing happen. Even when your plan doesn’t work out, good things are still allowed to keep happening — sometimes because of something you didn’t even do.”

Teach the phrase: “The plan changed. The good didn’t stop.” Say it together once or twice.

5
~4 min

Carry it

Give each kid paper. Write or draw one thing: a plan that might change this week (a game, a sleepover, a tryout) where they want to remember this phrase.

What you'll need

The materials

  • Paper and pens, one per kid
  • Nothing else — no printouts or purchases needed

If it's not going well

When this one gets heavy

Not making a team or getting excluded from something can surface real pain around belonging at this age — sometimes more than the disappointment on the surface. If a kid’s story sounds like ongoing exclusion, bullying, or something happening at home rather than a one-time disappointment, don’t try to resolve it with this lesson’s framework. Stay warm, listen, and follow the site’s mentor safety guidelines (For Mentors page), including notifying the appropriate adult per your church’s child-safety policy if what surfaces sounds like more than a typical hard day.

For the fuller tiered guidance — including what to do if someone discloses something serious — see For Mentors.

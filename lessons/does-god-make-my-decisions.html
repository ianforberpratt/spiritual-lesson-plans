A lesson

Does God Make My Decisions For Me?

A practical way to move through the toughest choices.

Time45–60 min
Group1:1 or group
AgeHigh school (or equiv.)
FormatIn-person or virtual

Before you begin

What you should know

It's tempting to think of a power greater than yourself like a vending machine — you ask, it picks Option A or B for you. This lesson works from a different idea: your job isn't to get an answer handed to you, it's to get clear and quiet enough that the next right step becomes visible on its own. No background needed to lead it.

What we're assuming

The starting point

Almost everyone in the room is sitting on some real decision right now — a class, a friendship, whether to say something to a parent, something bigger. We're not assuming they'll share what it is. The tool works either way.

Learning goals

What a young person walks away with

  • Leaves with a different way to think about "what a power greater than me wants" — not a hidden answer to guess at, but clarity they can actually build toward.
  • Leaves with one concrete tool (10-10-10) they can use on a real decision this week.
  • Leaves knowing the difference between only looking for reasons they're right, and actually testing an assumption.
  • Leaves feeling like they don't need to see the whole future to take the next honest step.

How to hold the room

Before you start talking

  • No decision is too small to use as practice — a friendship, a class choice, whether to speak up. Let each person pick their own, and let it stay private if they want.
  • This isn't about handing anyone the answer to their real decision. Your job is to hand them the tool, not solve their choice for them.
  • Some young people are facing decisions with real stakes attached — see the safety note before you lead this.

The lesson flow

Four beats, about 45–60 minutes total

Each beat alternates a little input, a little doing, and a little reflecting. Skip, slow down, or stretch any of these to fit your group.

1
~10 min

What We Actually Mean by "Guidance"

Share this reframe: the point isn't asking a power greater than yourself to pick Option A or B like a vending machine. It's getting quiet and clear enough that the next right step becomes visible.

Discuss: When you're stuck on a decision, are you usually waiting for a sign — or avoiding actually deciding?

2
~12–15 min

Getting Some Distance

Teach the 10-10-10 rule: how will you feel about this decision in 10 minutes? 10 months? 10 years? This isn't about ignoring your gut — it's about noticing when fear of judgment ("what will people think," "am I failing") is doing the deciding instead of you.

Try it: Pick a real or made-up decision and run it through all three time frames, out loud or on paper.

3
~12–15 min

Checking Your Own Assumptions

We tend to only look for reasons we're already right. Fight that by asking instead: why might I be wrong about this? And a deeper check: am I leaning this way for growth, or for ego?

Try it: For that same decision, name one assumption you've been treating as fact that you haven't actually tested.

4
~10 min

You Don't Need to See the Whole Staircase

Once you've done that work, you don't have to have it all figured out to take the next step. Close with this: you're not carrying this alone, and you don't need the whole picture — just the next honest step, in the light you actually have right now.

If it's not going well

When this one gets heavy

Read this one before you lead the lesson, not during it: this tool is for everyday tough choices — not crisis decisions. If what surfaces sounds like a young person is facing a genuinely unsafe situation (leaving home, reporting harm, their own safety), don't apply "take your time and reflect" — some decisions need an adult's help right away, not a decision-making framework.

For the fuller tiered guidance — including what to do if someone discloses something serious — see For Mentors.

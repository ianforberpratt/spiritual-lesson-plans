Fear Shows Up First: The Cup and the Center

A four-move practice for the moment someone you care about is hurting and you feel scared. Written to be tried, not agreed with.

Time50–60 min
Groupsmall group or class (4–20 people)
AgeTeen
Formatin-person or virtual
UpdatedNovember 17, 2026
Also built for:K-2Grade 3-5Grade 6-8Age 21+

Assumptions & Caveats

Before you begin

What you should know

This age is living the exact version of the problem: a roommate who’s melting down, a group project where two people stopped speaking, a friend texting at 1 a.m., a headline that makes you feel helpless. The two classic failures show up as: (a) knowing the “right” spiritual answer and delivering it like a verdict, or (b) pouring yourself out until you have nothing left and a little resentment. The lesson doesn’t ask them to feel brave first. It shows that fear is named first, a picture in thought is seen as a picture, a center is held, and then a small cup is offered.

The three degrees (Science and Health, pp. 115–116) show up as step two. If the group has done Seven Names, One Journey, they already have the map. If not, this lesson gives enough to use it. The one rule that must be said out loud: the map describes what appears in thought; it does not rank persons. This is also the practice-sized follow-up to Rescue or Faith?. Required prep: none beyond a pitcher of water and cups.

What we're assuming

  • Varying faith backgrounds. We use “God” and “Love” plainly and nobody is asked to agree. Passages are shown or read in full so anyone can use them.
  • Nobody has to share anything real. Every beat has a “pass” and a “private write” option. No names, ever.
  • Some people in the room are in a hard situation right now. The cases are deliberately side-less and fictional so nobody has to confess to practice.
  • This is not a lesson about any particular political or social issue, and the facilitator will not name or take a side on one.

Learning goals

What they walk away with

  1. Recognize that fear tends to run the first move, and that naming it plainly is not dwelling; it’s meeting.
  2. Use a four-step practice (address the fear, notice what appears in thought, turn to the center, choose the cup) on a real-feeling case.
  3. Write a one-sentence “center” in their own words that passes five tests.
  4. Leave with one real, small cup to offer this week, and one person to tell.

How to hold the room

Before you start talking

  • State the ground rules and mean them: think first; anyone may pass; no names.
  • Keep your own part small. Model steps one and two tentatively (“here’s how it might look”), then let them do three and four.
  • Run a “How clear is it?” check-in (1 to 5) two or three times, and say out loud what you’ll change because of it.
  • If a group starts arguing the issue instead of practicing, send them back to step one. The method matters more than any one question.
  • Quiet is not disengagement. Offer private writing before sharing.

The lesson flow

Step by step

Each beat alternates a little input, a little doing, and a little reflecting. Skip, slow down, or stretch any of these to fit your group.

1
~8 min

The cup on the table, and the two hands

A small cup of water waits in the middle. Don’t explain it. Ask everyone to write, on a first paper, “This hour will be worth it for me if...” (two or three lines, not collected).

Then say: “I keep finding I have to hold two things that both matter. One is the truth that God is All, and every person is whole and loved, never broken. The other is a real person in a real need today. On some days I reach for the first and my hands forget the second. On other days it’s the other way around. I don’t think they compete. I think they belong in one hand.”

Read, very slowly:

“Jesus said to her, ‘I am the resurrection and the life.’... Jesus wept.”

John 11:25, 35

“At his friend’s tomb he said the biggest thing a person can say, and he wept. He did not choose between them.”

Name the picture: a wheel. The rim is the cup (touching the road, muddy, real); the hub is the center (never moves). “A cup without a center runs out. A center without a cup is correctness nobody can feel.”

2
~10 min

A map of what appears in thought

Put up the three degrees. Read them together from the printout.

“First Degree: Depravity. PHYSICAL. Evil beliefs, passions and appetites, fear, depraved will, self-justification, pride, envy, deceit, hatred, revenge, sin, sickness, disease, death.”

Science and Health, Mary Baker Eddy, p. 115

“Second Degree: Evil beliefs disappearing. MORAL. Humanity, honesty, affection, compassion, hope, faith, meekness, temperance.”

Science and Health, Mary Baker Eddy, p. 116

“Third Degree: Understanding. SPIRITUAL. Wisdom, purity, spiritual understanding, spiritual power, love, health, holiness.”

Science and Health, Mary Baker Eddy, p. 116

Say three things, slowly. One: the heading reads “scientific translation of mortal mind.” It’s a map of what appears in thought; it does not rank persons. The person is on the line above: “God’s spiritual idea, individual, perfect, eternal.” Two: each degree calls for a different cup. First degree (fear, hurt, hatred, pain): the cup is safety, a steady voice and presence. Arguing doesn’t help. Second degree (honesty, compassion, hope, faith): the cup is encouragement, truth shared kindly. Third degree (wisdom, understanding, love): the cup is clarity, the plain truth said simply. Three: the center comes first, then the cup.

Quick pairs (about four minutes): “Write a real situation from this week or month, no names, where you didn’t show up the way you wanted to. Which degree was it nearer? What cup would have fit that degree?” One person tells it in thirty seconds. The partner asks only two questions, no advice: “What’s showing up in thought here? Which degree is it nearer?” and “What would a cup look like at that degree?”

3
~12 min

The four steps on a case

Put up the four steps. Read the case once, no questions yet:

“Two people who’ve known each other for years and care about each other see a divisive public question differently. We are deliberately not saying what it is. They’re at a small gathering after something else, and the subject comes up. Voices rise a little. Each feels the other stopped listening, and each is sure, from the inside, that their view is a matter of principle. Both go home unsettled. You overheard it, and you love them both.”

You model step one out loud, with the book:

“Christian scientific practice begins with Christ’s keynote of harmony, ‘Be not afraid!’”

Science and Health, Mary Baker Eddy, p. 410:29–30

“What’s the fear here, humanly? Mine: ‘if I say something I’ll lose one of them; if I say nothing I’m a coward.’ Naming it plainly isn’t dwelling. It’s meeting.”

You model step two: “What’s showing up in thought? For the first person it might be ‘I have to win this or I’m nothing.’ For the second, ‘nobody’s even listening.’ For me as I listen: ‘I have to fix this.’ It’s a picture in thought, not the truth about anyone.”

Groups of three or four do step three (turn to the center; about three minutes) and step four (choose the cup; about three minutes). Ask only questions: “Is it true of both at once? Does it need an opponent? Is the cup small enough to do this week? Would the same warmth go to both?” Reflection on paper, one line each: Step one, in my own words. What I noticed in thought. What I’m left questioning.

Check-in: “How clear is the cup, 1 to 5? Fingers up.” Name what you’ll adjust.

4
~10 min

Write your center

Say: “A center isn’t a feeling or a hope. It’s what’s held to be true when circumstances say the opposite. We can all recite one; a borrowed sentence is a cold cup. So write your own.” Put the five tests on the board:

  1. It’s true in a hospital waiting room.
  2. It’s in the present tense.
  3. It’s not about anyone else and doesn’t need an opponent.
  4. It’s in plain words.
  5. It can stand up to the evening news.

On an index card: Whatever is happening, what is true is... Three ways in: God as All and each person as God’s image; Paul’s promise below; something you’ve proved yourself. Write three versions fast, circle the one you’d say, quietly, to someone you love who’s frightened.

“These clearer, higher views inspire the Godlike man to reach the absolute centre and circumference of his being.”

Science and Health, Mary Baker Eddy, p. 262:14–16

“Neither death nor life... nor anything else in all creation, will be able to separate us from the love of God.”

Romans 8:38–39

Trios, four minutes each, three rounds: speaker reads the center aloud, then draws a circumstance card (see printable), reads it, and says in their own words what’s true about it. The listener says: “It held when...” and “It wobbled when...” Anyone may pass or trade a card.

5
~8 min

Three seats at the table

Short Luke 15 (full text on the handout). Three volunteers read in three scenes while everyone follows. “Mark one word that surprises you.” Notice where the turn happens: not in the pigsty, but in thought (“he came to himself”). Notice the father: nothing in him changed. His center never moved, and his cup was a run, a robe, a ring, and a feast. And the elder son never left and is standing outside a party in his own house. The father goes out to him too.

Trios, one seat each: the younger son, the father, the elder brother. Answer in one minute each: What does this one believe? What’s actually true, in the one Mind? (the center). What would a cup look like from this seat? (the cup). The others say: “What I heard at the center was...” Then on your own paper, one word: which seat am I in today?

6
~7 min

My card

Hand out the four-step card. One line for each step, written about a real situation coming up this week: the fear I’ll meet first; the degree I expect to see in my own thought; my center (revised from the card in Beat 4); my cup, small and specific, something I’d offer anyone. Optional: one person I’ll tell.

Pairs, partner not yet spoken with, four minutes each: read the card, every line. The partner says one sentence beginning “I heard...” and one of encouragement. Then close by returning to the first papers: “Did it come true? Yes, partly, or not yet?” Pour the water. Everyone offers a cup to a neighbor by name.

7

Optional extension (~5 min) — One headline

If time and the room allow: pick a public sorrow with no sides (a natural disaster or an accident), not a policy question. One minute of silence for the people in it. Then all four steps, out loud, in the whole room. Name this rule first: the point is practice, not opinion, and not graphic detail.

If it's not going well

When this one gets heavy

Silence is normal; let it sit. Disagreement (“this is naive,” “you can’t love everyone,” “some people are just wrong”) is welcome. Curiosity beats correction: “I hear you. The cup isn’t about deciding who’s right. It’s the next small thing.” A strong emotional reaction means pausing the activity, grounding the room, and checking in privately. If someone discloses self-harm, abuse, danger, or anything urgent, stop facilitating a lesson and respond as a caring adult: stay calm, thank them, don’t promise secrecy, and follow the site’s mentor safety guidelines (For Mentors page) and your own local mandatory-reporting obligations. A mentor is not a substitute for a trained professional. If a case lands close to home, let anyone step out or pass.

For the fuller tiered guidance — including what to do if someone discloses something serious — see For Mentors.

Sharing Without Steering

You can love what you believe, live it out loud, and still let the people closest to you have their own relationship with God, or none.

Time40-45 min
Groupsmall group or full class (4-16), with a private-reflection option
AgeTeen
Formatin-person or virtual
UpdatedOctober 8, 2026
Also built for:Grade 3-5Grade 6-8Age 21+

Assumptions & Caveats

Before you begin

What you should know

This age is actively deciding what they believe, and living next to people who believe differently: a roommate, a teammate, a partner, a best friend who left the faith they grew up in (or joined one). Some feel the pull to defend or convert; others feel judged, or have been the target of someone else’s pull. This lesson isn’t a script for either. It offers one steadying idea, that each person’s relationship with God is their own, and one concrete test for staying honest about it. Be ready for real disagreement and treat it as the lesson working. Nothing to prepare beyond paper.

What we're assuming

  • The room holds a real range of faith backgrounds, from devout to doubting to none. Don’t assume shared belief, shared church history, or shared feelings about religion, which may be painful for some.
  • Some participants may carry hurt from someone who tried to convert them, or from leaving or being pushed out of a community. No one has to say so.
  • Nobody has to speak. A private reflection option is offered before any sharing.

Learning goals

What they walk away with

  1. Leaves with a steadier sense that they don’t have to be anyone’s savior, and that no one else has to be theirs.
  2. Can name the difference between sharing what they love and needing a response, in a real relationship of their own.
  3. Walks out with a repeatable test and a clear safety line, so “letting go of beliefs” never turns into “letting go of someone in danger.”
  4. Feels free to hold a tension without resolving it neatly.

How to hold the room

Before you start talking

  • Open with curiosity, not a position. You’re not there to teach “the right way to handle other faiths.”
  • Welcome disagreement, including from students who think this is too soft or too hard. “That’s a real objection. Say more.”
  • Never ask a student to explain or defend a faith, or the lack of one. Nobody speaks for anybody.
  • Offer a private-reflection option before any sharing, and a clear way to pass on any beat.
  • You’re not there to resolve every tension by the end of the hour. If the room leaves still thinking, that’s a good outcome.

The lesson flow

Step by step

Each beat alternates a little input, a little doing, and a little reflecting. Skip, slow down, or stretch any of these to fit your group.

1
~7 min

Where the pull shows up

Ask the room to name, out loud or privately, a relationship where belief differences show up: a roommate, a friend group, someone they’re dating, a family member, a teammate. Offer scenarios and ask students to silently place themselves on a line between two ends: sharing (I say what I love and I’m fine either way) and steering (I need them to come around, or I’m anxious until they do).

Quick scenarios to read: a friend says they don’t believe in God anymore. Your roommate prays differently than you do, and you notice every time. Someone you’re dating asks about your faith and you feel defensive. A friend keeps inviting you to their church and you keep saying no. A classmate makes a joke about people like you.

Don’t discuss yet. Let them stand with where they landed.

2
~10 min

What the texts actually say

Read Psalms 18:28 and Isaiah 45:5 together: “For thou wilt light my candle,” and “I girded thee, though thou hast not known me.” Then:

”‘Work out your own salvation with fear and trembling,’ says the apostle, and he straightway adds: ‘for it is God which worketh in you both to will and to do of His good pleasure.’”

Science and Health, Mary Baker Eddy, p. 99:5–9

Notice who is working: the person, and God, in them. Not the friend, and not the church. Then add, as a counterweight:

“Let us be faithful in pointing the way through Christ, as we understand it, but let us also be careful always to ‘judge righteous judgment,’ and never to condemn rashly.”

Science and Health, Mary Baker Eddy, p. 444:16–19

Name it honestly: this text holds both. It encourages being faithful to what you understand, and it warns against condemning. It does not say all paths are the same, and we shouldn’t put that in her mouth. What it asks is harder than “agree” or “argue”: hold what you love, share it faithfully, and leave the other person’s relationship with God to them and to God.

3
~13 min

Pairs: the harder cases

In pairs (or privately, if they’d rather), take one of these and talk it through. Don’t solve. Notice what’s hard:

  • You really believe something that matters deeply to you, and someone you love believes the opposite. Is it possible to hold both your conviction and their freedom?
  • What’s the difference between being honest about what you believe and pressing?
  • When someone’s belief seems to be hurting them, where does respect end and care begin?
  • What if you’re the one who feels pressed, by family, by a community, by someone who “just wants what’s best”? What would help you?

After a few minutes, invite anyone to share a piece of what came up. Say clearly that nobody has to.

4
~12 min

A test, a line, and a private locate

Teach the test: “Do I need them to respond a certain way for this to count as a good conversation?” If yes, I’ve slid from sharing to steering. If I can say what I love, ask real questions, and be at peace however they respond, I’m sharing.

Then the safety line, said directly: this idea never means “not my problem.” You can’t control anyone’s beliefs, and you can always bring in a caring adult when someone is unsafe, hurting themselves, or being hurt. That isn’t pushing a belief; it’s love.

Finish with a short private locate: “Think of one real relationship right now. What would it look like to share what I love there, and let go of needing a response?” Write it down or just hold it. Hand out the wallet card.

What you'll need

The materials

If it's not going well

When this one gets heavy

Silence is fine; let it sit. Pushback (“this ignores that some beliefs are harmful” or “isn’t this just relativism?”) is welcome. Take it seriously and say what the lesson does and doesn’t claim: it doesn’t say every belief is equally true, it says your relationship with God isn’t yours to run for someone else, and safety is never up for negotiation. A strong emotional reaction (someone who’s been rejected by a faith community, or is carrying pressure from family) calls for a pause, grounding the room, and a private check-in; offer space to step out. A disclosure of real harm (abuse, danger, or a friend at risk) is not something to work through in the room. Stop facilitating, respond as a caring adult, and follow the site’s mentor safety guidelines along with your own local mandatory-reporting obligations.

For the fuller tiered guidance — including what to do if someone discloses something serious — see For Mentors.

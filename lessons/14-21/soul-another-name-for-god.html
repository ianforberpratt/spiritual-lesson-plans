Soul: Another Name for God

Reading, symbolism, a creative class definition, and real life — Soul as one of the seven synonyms for God in Christian Science.

Time45–50 min
GroupClass or small group
AgeTeen
Formatin-person or virtual
UpdatedAugust 20, 2026
Also built for:Grade 6-8Age 21+

Assumptions & Caveats

Before you begin

What you should know

In Christian Science, “Soul” isn’t a ghost-in-the-machine or a wispy inner feeling — it’s one of seven names for God, alongside Mind, Spirit, Life, Truth, Love, and Principle. Each name points at the same infinite good from a different angle. Soul is the angle that answers the question every teenager is quietly asking: where does my real identity actually come from?

The world’s usual answer is that identity comes from the body — its appetites, its impulses, its reputation, its reflection in a phone screen. Christian Science reverses the source: Soul is the one true source of identity, sense, and selfhood, and the body is meant to express Soul, not define it. That reversal is the whole engine of the passage below. It doesn’t say “suppress your propensities and hate yourself for having them.” It says: locate your real government somewhere higher than the propensity itself, and the propensity loses its authority.

“Christian Science commands man to master the propensities, — to hold hatred in abeyance with kindness, to conquer lust with chastity, revenge with charity, and to overcome deceit with honesty. Choke these errors in their early stages, if you would not cherish an army of conspirators against health, happiness, and success.”

Science and Health, p. 405:5–11

“The good man finally can overcome his fear of sin. This is sin’s necessity, — to destroy itself. Immortal man demonstrates the government of God, good, in which is no power to sin.”

Science and Health, p. 405:18

What we're assuming

  • Some students will hear “Soul” and think feelings, vibes, or the thing that leaves your body when you die. Some won’t have a working definition at all. Both are fine starting points.
  • The 1875 vocabulary — propensities, abeyance, conspirators — is genuinely unfamiliar to a 2026 teenager and needs to be unlocked before it can be discussed, not glossed over.
  • Naming hatred, lust, revenge, and deceit out loud in a room of teenagers is not an invitation to confess anything specific. Keep it at the level of the pattern, not the person, unless a student chooses otherwise.
  • This is not a lesson about being a “good kid.” It’s a lesson about where power actually lives.

Learning goals

What they walk away with

  1. A working answer to “why does Christian Science call Soul a name for God?” — Soul as the true source of identity, not a possession or a feeling.
  2. The ability to unpack the passage’s imagery in their own words — what a “mental conspirator” is, why errors get “choked” early, why sin is described as self-destroying.
  3. Their own creative definition or image of Soul, built with the group, that they could actually explain to a friend.
  4. One real, current situation in their own life where they can practice answering from Soul instead of from impulse or reputation.

How to hold the room

Before you start talking

  • Translate the Victorian language together in Beat 1 rather than assuming it lands. Don’t apologize for the vocabulary — treat cracking it open as part of the fun.
  • Keep the four “propensities” (hatred, lust, revenge, deceit) at the level of pattern and story, not personal confession. If a student volunteers something real and specific, follow their lead — don’t chase it.
  • This isn’t a behavior-correction lesson. If the conversation drifts into shame (“I guess I’m just a bad person”), redirect immediately: the passage’s whole claim is that the error has no real power and no real root in anyone’s true identity — that’s the relief, not a new rule to fail at.
  • Soul belongs to everyone in the room equally, regardless of background, orientation, family situation, or how “spiritual” they’d currently describe themselves. Say so if it’s useful.

The lesson flow

Step by step

Each beat alternates a little input, a little doing, and a little reflecting. Skip, slow down, or stretch any of these to fit your group.

1
~10–12 min

Read it, then decode the symbolism

Read both citations aloud once, straight through, slowly. Then read them again, phrase by phrase, and unpack the imagery together:

  • “Mental conspirators” — a conspirator doesn’t announce itself; it plots quietly, in the dark, waiting for the right moment. What does it mean that hatred, lust, revenge, and deceit are described as conspiring rather than just “happening” to you?
  • “Hold hatred in abeyance with kindness” — “abeyance” means suspended, put on hold, not yet in force. Notice the passage doesn’t say erase hatred — it says hold it off with something else. What’s the difference between erasing a feeling and outweighing it?
  • “Choke these errors in their early stages” — what image comes to mind? (A weed before it roots. A spark before it’s a fire.) Why would timing matter this much?
  • “An army of conspirators against health, happiness, and success” — why “army”? What does it suggest about what happens when small errors go unchecked?
  • “Sin’s necessity — to destroy itself” — this is the most counterintuitive line in the passage. It claims error is self-limiting, self-defeating, built to collapse under its own weight. What would it mean to actually believe that, instead of believing error is powerful and permanent?
  • “Immortal man demonstrates the government of God, good, in which is no power to sin” — “government” is a strong word. What is being governed here, and by what?

Quick pair-share: in your own words, no big vocabulary allowed, what is this passage actually claiming?

2
~12–15 min

Define Soul, creatively, together

Bridge: this passage is really a passage about Soul, even though the word never appears. Explain briefly — Soul is one of seven names Christian Science uses for God (alongside Mind, Spirit, Life, Truth, Love, Principle). Soul answers where does my real identity come from — and the passage’s whole claim (kindness outweighs hatred, chastity outweighs lust, charity outweighs revenge, honesty outweighs deceit) only works if there’s a real, permanent, higher identity underneath the propensity that these qualities can be drawn from. That underneath-identity is Soul.

Pick one creative format for the group (or let students choose their own lane):

  • Found-poem build: Each student writes one line finishing the prompt “Soul is —” using at least one image or word from the passage (conspirator, choke, army, government, immortal). Collect the lines, read them aloud in sequence as one class poem.
  • One image, no words: Students sketch a single symbol or scene for Soul — no explanation allowed until everyone’s done, then a rapid show-and-tell.
  • Caption it: Students write the definition as if it were a caption under a photo they’d actually post — six words or fewer.

Whatever format, close the beat by asking: does your definition sound anything like the God you grew up picturing, or the world’s usual definition of “soul”? What’s different?

3
~12–15 min

Turn it into life

Split into four small groups (or rotate through all four solo if the group is small). Each group gets one pairing from the passage:

  1. Hatred → kindness
  2. Lust → chastity
  3. Revenge → charity
  4. Deceit → honesty

For their pairing, each group finds a real, current version of that “mental conspirator” in teenage life — not a hypothetical from 1875. (Hatred might show up as pile-on comments under a post. Lust might show up as pressure around hookup culture or how a body “should” look online. Revenge might show up as subtweeting, screenshotting to expose someone, freezing a friend out. Deceit might show up as a curated identity online that doesn’t match anyone’s actual life.) And one honest example of what “holding it in abeyance” with the opposite quality could actually look like this week — specific enough to try, not a slogan.

Groups report back. As a class, notice: every single example is really the same move — answering from Soul (the real, permanent, already-whole identity) instead of from the reflex.

4
~8–10 min

The experiment

Close with an invitation, not an assignment: this week, notice one moment when a “mental conspirator” shows up — even something small — and consciously reach for its opposite quality before reacting. Name out loud that this might be hard, and that’s expected; the passage doesn’t promise it’s easy, only that the error has no real staying power.

“Immortal man demonstrates the government of God, good, in which is no power to sin.”

Science and Health, p. 405:18

Quick close: What’s one specific moment this week where you might get to try this?

What you'll need

The materials

  • A printed or projected copy of the passage (both citations) for every student
  • Paper and pens
  • Poster paper or a shared doc for the found-poem or one-word definitions in Beat 2
  • Sketch paper if using the “one image, no words” option
  • Optional handout: “Not the Impulse”

If it's not going well

When this one gets heavy

If the conversation opens up real disclosure — self-harm, abuse, or a genuine safety concern — pause the lesson itself and follow the site’s mentor safety guidelines (For Mentors page). If a student gets visibly emotional naming their own struggle with one of the four propensities, that’s not a sign to move faster; slow down and let them lead how much they share.

For the fuller tiered guidance — including what to do if someone discloses something serious — see For Mentors.

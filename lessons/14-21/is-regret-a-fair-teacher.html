A lesson · Is Regret a Fair and Just Teacher?

Teen & Early College

Is Regret a Fair and Just Teacher?

A practical path from a hard decision to moving forward — without pretending it didn’t happen.

Time45–60 min
Group1:1 or group
AgeTeen & Early College
Formatin-person or virtual
Also built for:11–14 · Middle School (Grades 6–8)21+ · College Student & Adult (21+)

Assumptions & Caveats

Before you begin

What you should know

No prerequisites needed. The lesson draws from an ancient narrative about a woman facing public condemnation for a mistake. Rather than argue her innocence, the response centers on this principle: “no one here is without fault, so who’s left to condemn her?” The figure then tells her directly that she isn’t condemned and encourages her to move forward differently — not by pretending the mistake vanished, but by refusing to be crushed by its weight indefinitely.

What we're assuming

  • Everyone present carries at least one decision they still think about — something done or left undone. Participants aren’t expected to disclose their specific regret.

Learning goals

What they walk away with

  1. Participants distinguish between honest acknowledgment of a mistake and being permanently sentenced to relive it.
  2. Participants gain a concrete, applicable framework: observe clearly, release shame intentionally, identify underlying fear, make a deliberate choice, remain open to adjustment, and recognize what the experience revealed.
  3. Participants understand that changing course after a decision demonstrates growth, not failure.
  4. Participants leave feeling permitted to stop self-punishment around something, even without external permission to do so.

How to hold the room

Before you start talking

  • Emphasize that sharing the specific regret is entirely optional.
  • Avoid premature reassurance (“don’t worry, it’s fine”), which can unintentionally dismiss something genuine.
  • Monitor for shame spirals. If someone appears to be deepening into despair rather than processing, pause or decelerate significantly.

The lesson flow

Step by step

Each beat alternates a little input, a little doing, and a little reflecting. Skip, slow down, or stretch any of these to fit your group.

1
~10 min

Naming the Decision, Without Shame

Recall a decision — something enacted or omitted — that you continue to carry. Disclosure is optional. Highlight: “Seeing a mistake honestly isn’t the same as being sentenced by it.”

2
~12–15 min

The Clean Slate

Deliberately set shame aside — not by forgetting what occurred, but by ceasing daily self-prosecution. Notice fear underlying the narrative about it (“I’ll always be this way”) and question whether this fear reflects reality.

Discuss: What distinguishes remembering something from remaining imprisoned by it?

3
~12–15 min

Choosing, and Being Willing to Adjust

At a certain point, circling must stop and a choice — however modest — becomes necessary. Later course corrections represent student behavior, not failure.

Discuss: Recall a moment you shifted direction following a choice. Did that feel like failure or learning?

4
~10 min

Gratitude, Even for the Rocky Parts

Identify, silently or aloud, one thing the difficult decision actually taught you. Closing statement: “You are not what you regret. You’re allowed to learn from it and put it down — both, not just one.”

What you'll need

The materials

  • Paper and pens, or a shared digital document/whiteboard

If it's not going well

When this one gets heavy

Regret discussions can surface genuine shame spirals beyond simple reflection. If a participant expresses that they don’t deserve forgiveness, that they’re fundamentally bad, or anything suggesting hopelessness about themselves, treat it seriously — decelerate significantly, and follow the site’s mentor safety guidelines (For Mentors page).

For the fuller tiered guidance — including what to do if someone discloses something serious — see For Mentors.

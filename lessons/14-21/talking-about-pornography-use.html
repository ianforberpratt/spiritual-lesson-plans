Talking About the Increase of Pornography Use in Our World

“A steady, shame-free way in — because a habit is a cloud passing in front of the sun, not the sun itself.”

Time45–60 min
Group1:1 or group
AgeTeen
Formatin-person or virtual
UpdatedAugust 25, 2026

Assumptions & Caveats

Before you begin

What you should know

This lesson doesn’t assume anyone has a problem or doesn’t. The approach normalizes discussion rather than treating the topic as secretive, since nearly everyone encounters this material given modern technology and algorithms.

Parents and mentors increasingly discuss this openly because avoidance rarely helps. Young people encounter such material younger than previous generations — often through algorithms and shared devices rather than deliberate searching. Adults leading this lesson may have their own history with it, which isn’t disqualifying; it can enable warmth instead of alarm.

What we're assuming

The lesson assumes most participants have encountered this material intentionally or accidentally but won’t necessarily disclose it. The approach works whether someone actively struggles, saw something once and felt confused, or simply wants understanding.

Learning goals

What they walk away with

Young people walk away: 1. Knowing exposure or struggle doesn’t equal being broken, dirty, or beyond repair. 2. Understanding the distinction: “a habit is something you have, not something you are.” 3. Recognizing shame strengthens habits rather than weakening them. 4. Aware that seeking comfort or connection isn’t the problem — but where they search for it matters.

How to hold the room

Before you start talking

  • This isn’t a lecture on danger or a confession booth.
  • Avoid clinical/graphic descriptions; focus on identity and habit.
  • If you have personal history, you needn’t disclose it to lead effectively.
  • If disclosures suggest serious behavioral disruption, shift into counselor referral mode immediately.

The lesson flow

Step by step

Each beat alternates a little input, a little doing, and a little reflecting. Skip, slow down, or stretch any of these to fit your group.

1
~10 min

Not about the content

Participants privately reflect — without describing anything seen — on what this material promises (comfort, connection, control, escape) versus what it actually leaves them feeling. No sharing required; just notice the gap.

2
~12–15 min

The wrong shelf

Core teaching: “Christian Science teaches that everyone is already whole and complete — not an empty container waiting to be filled by anything, including this. The craving says ‘you’re missing something.’ The truth is closer to: you’re not missing anything, you’re just reaching for the wrong shelf.”

Reflection/discussion: What are you actually hungry for — comfort? connection? feeling wanted? feeling in control? Name the real hunger. It’s rarely about what’s on screen.

3
~15 min

A cloud, not the sun

Practice reframing shame statements into identity-first language. Instead of “I’m disgusting for looking at that,” try: “That’s inconsistent with who I actually am. It’s a thief of my peace, not proof of who I am. I’m whole, and I get to just adjust and keep moving.”

Activity: Write your own shame-statement, then rewrite it identity-first using that pattern.

4
~10 min

The sky was never just the cloud

Closing reflection: whatever cloud passed over today was never the whole sky. Participants remain whole underneath it, completely, right now — not later, not once earned back.

If it's not going well

When this one gets heavy

Read before leading, not during: This isn’t a confession moment. Meet personal disclosures with identity-first language, not alarm. If someone describes a pattern disrupting sleep, relationships, or daily life, gently point toward counseling alongside spiritual guidance.

Critical boundaries: If disclosure involves minors in images, non-consensual content, deepfakes, or non-consensual sharing of explicit images — stop immediately. This exceeds personal habit and may involve illegal content or safety crimes. Don’t investigate yourself. Follow your country’s and organization’s reporting obligations and involve trained professionals or authorities per local requirements.

For the fuller tiered guidance — including what to do if someone discloses something serious — see For Mentors.

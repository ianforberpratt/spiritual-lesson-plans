A lesson

When Someone Feels Like the Enemy

Why "love your enemies" isn't the same as "there are no enemies."

Time40–55 min
Group1:1 or group
AgeHigh school (or equiv.)
FormatIn-person or virtual

Before you begin

What you should know

Nothing required. If it helps to know where this came from: a teaching of Jesus's is "love your enemies" — and there's a distinction worth noticing in that. He never said there are no enemies. He said something harder and more honest: they're real, and love them anyway. This lesson sits inside that gap.

What we're assuming

The starting point

Almost everyone in the room has someone right now who feels like an adversary — a bully, a family member, an ex-friend, someone online. We're not assuming anyone will name who. We're assuming the feeling is real and won't be argued away, and that's fine.

Learning goals

What a young person walks away with

  • Leaves knowing that naming something as hard, or someone as acting like an enemy, isn't a spiritual or personal failure — it's honesty.
  • Leaves understanding the difference between pretending a hurt isn't real and refusing to let it decide how you act.
  • Leaves with one concrete, felt difference between "loving" someone and "liking," "excusing," or "staying close to" them.
  • Leaves having practiced imagining one response rooted in love instead of retaliation — without anyone telling them they have to feel ready to forgive.

How to hold the room

Before you start talking

  • This is not a lesson about forcing forgiveness or minimizing real harm. Be careful never to let "love your enemy" sound like "let them keep hurting you." Loving someone never requires staying close to them.
  • Real, current conflict may surface here — bullying, family estrangement, worse. Nobody has to name names.
  • Watch the pace. This can tip into guilt ("I'm supposed to love everyone, so what's wrong with me") if you rush it. Slow down, especially on beat three.

The lesson flow

Four beats, about 40–55 minutes total

Each beat alternates a little input, a little doing, and a little reflecting. Skip, slow down, or stretch any of these to fit your group.

1
~10 min

Naming It

Think of someone — you don't have to say who — who currently feels like an adversary to you: someone who's hurt you, opposed you, or just feels like "the enemy" in some part of your life. Don't judge the feeling. Just notice it's there. Naming a hard thing honestly isn't weakness — it's the starting move.

2
~10–15 min

Two Things Jesus Didn't Say

Share this out loud: the teaching is "love your enemies" — not "pretend you don't have any." That's a real difference. It doesn't say the friction isn't there. It says something harder: see it clearly, and respond with love anyway.

Discuss: What's the difference between pretending someone didn't hurt you, and choosing not to hate them back?

3
~15 min

What Love Actually Requires (and Doesn't)

This is the important one — take it slow. As a group or in pairs, sort through the difference between these, out loud: loving someone, liking them, excusing what they did, staying close to them, trusting them again. Love doesn't require any of the other four. You can love someone, mean it, and still keep real distance and real boundaries.

Discuss: What would loving this person — without excusing what happened or staying close — actually look like, concretely?

4
~10 min

Practicing the Harder Thing

Quiet reflection, or share if comfortable: without naming the person, what's one small way you could respond to that hard relationship this week from love instead of anger — even something as small as not gossiping about them, or just letting the anger stop with you?

Close with this: you don't have to pretend it doesn't hurt. You don't have to like them. You just get to choose what kind of person you are in how you respond — and that choice is where the power actually is.

What you'll need

The materials

  • Paper and pens — or a shared doc / whiteboard if you're meeting virtually
  • Nothing else. No printouts, no purchases.

If it's not going well

When this one gets heavy

Read this one before you lead the lesson, not during it: "love your enemies" can be dangerously misread as "stay, and endure it." It never means that. If a young person describes anything that sounds like ongoing abuse, bullying that's escalating, or an unsafe relationship, gently set the lesson's discussion aside and go straight to safety — loving someone is never a reason to stay somewhere unsafe.

For the fuller tiered guidance — including what to do if someone discloses something serious — see For Mentors.

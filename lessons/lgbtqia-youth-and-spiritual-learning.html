A lesson

LGBTQIA+ Youth and Spiritual Learning

Every young person deserves a safe way to think about their relationship with God. This one names why that hasn't always been true for this group specifically.

Time45–60 min
Group1:1 or group
AgeHigh school (or equiv.)
FormatIn-person or virtual

A note before you begin: This site doesn't build lessons around any one identity group, and doesn't plan to make a habit of it. This lesson exists because the research on LGBTQIA+ youth and marginalization is real and specific — not because we think this group's spiritual questions matter more than anyone else's. They don't. Every young person, every single one, deserves a safe way to think about their relationship with a power greater than themselves. This lesson exists because, right now, that safety isn't a given for this group in a lot of rooms. It should be.

Before you begin

What you should know

Nothing required. If it helps to know where this came from: a Christian Science Sunday School teacher spent years running a class openly welcoming to LGBTQIA+ youth and their peers. When her small congregation closed, she went looking for a new home for the class, and heard “not a right fit” more than once before a larger church finally opened its doors. What she found, once the class was actually running, surprised her: they didn't talk about sexuality or gender much more than any group of teenagers does. What mattered was one question she kept coming back to: is there anyone in the world who shouldn't have access to Truth? This lesson borrows that question.

What we're assuming

The starting point

Almost everyone in this room fits one of two situations, and some fit both: someone who is LGBTQIA+ or questioning, or someone who has a friend, sibling, or peer who is. We're not assuming which, and nobody has to say. This lesson is built to be useful either way — sometimes both at once, in the same person.

Learning goals

What a young person walks away with

  • Leaves knowing that being LGBTQIA+ was never a barrier to their spiritual identity or worth — that was never actually in question.
  • Leaves with one concrete way to show up for a friend or peer who is LGBTQIA+, even without having every answer worked out themselves.
  • Leaves understanding that their own convictions and their care for someone they know don't have to cancel each other out — those are two separate things, not one.
  • Leaves knowing that whichever one of these describes them today — figuring out their own identity, or figuring out how to support someone else's — they fully belong in this room.

How to hold the room

Before you start talking

  • This isn't about arguing anyone into a position, including your own. You don't need your own theology fully settled to lead this well — your job is making sure every young person here leaves knowing they're not alone and not excluded.
  • Some young people here may be LGBTQIA+, questioning, or have someone close to them who is — and may not be out, or ready to be. Nothing here should ever require anyone to disclose their own identity or someone else's.
  • If a young person seems to be speaking on behalf of a friend, don't press for more detail than they've offered. Keep it about how they can show up well, not about extracting more of the friend's story.
  • Shaming a young person for where they currently are, in either direction, works against everything this lesson is trying to do.

The lesson flow

Four beats, about 45–60 minutes total

Each beat alternates a little input, a little doing, and a little reflecting. Skip, slow down, or stretch any of these to fit your group.

1
~10 min

A Message You Got Somewhere

Privately, on paper: think of one message you've received — from family, church, friends, social media, anywhere — either about your own identity, or about people who are LGBTQIA+. Did it make room for you or them, or shrink the room? You don't have to share which message, or where it came from. Just notice it landed somewhere.

2
~12–15 min

Seeing Past the Label

Share this, in your own words: Christian Science teaches that everyone's real identity is spiritual — a reflection of divine Love, not defined by material labels. Mary Baker Eddy wrote about “looking long enough” to see past gender and human categories entirely, to something she called “the intelligent compound idea… showing forth the infinite divine Principle, Love, called God.” That's not this site's invention — it's about 150 years old.

Reflect or discuss: if you knew for certain this was true — that everyone is fundamentally a reflection of Love, no exceptions — what would that change about how you see yourself, or a friend?

3
~15–18 min

What This Looks Like for You

Two quiet prompts. Do whichever fits — you're allowed to do both.

If this is about you: One truth about who I am, spiritually, that doesn't depend on anyone else's opinion: ___

If this is about someone you know: One way I could show up for them that doesn't require me to have all the answers: ___

If your own beliefs and your care for that person ever feel like they're pulling against each other: those are two separate dials, not one. You don't have to turn your convictions down to turn your curiosity and care up.

4
~10 min

You Were Never Excluded

Whether you came into this room still figuring out who you are, or figuring out how to be there for someone who is, close with this, unqualified: “Love is impartial and universal in its adaptations and bestowals. It is the open fount which cries, ‘Ho, everyone that thirsteth, come ye to the waters.’” Everyone thirsts. Everyone's invited to the water. That was never in question.

If it's not going well

When this one gets heavy

Read this before you lead it, not during it: LGBTQIA+ youth face meaningfully higher rates of family rejection and self-harm than their peers — that's not a side note, it's the room you're actually holding. If someone discloses family rejection, rejection from a faith community, or anything sounding like hopelessness or self-harm, stop being a “lesson leader” and be a caring adult instead. Keep The Trevor Project (a crisis line built specifically for LGBTQ+ youth) alongside your general safety resources for this one. If a young person is speaking on a friend's behalf, remember that friend's story isn't yours to press for — help the young person in front of you get their friend connected to real support rather than trying to solve it through them. And if disagreement ever turns into mockery, targeting, or pressure on someone to declare who they are, that's not “differing views” anymore — redirect immediately, same as any other unsafe moment.

For the fuller tiered guidance — including what to do if someone discloses something serious — see For Mentors.

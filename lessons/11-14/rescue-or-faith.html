A lesson · It’s Not All on You

Grade 6-8

It’s Not All on You

When a friend is struggling and your phone is lighting up, the pull to fix it can feel like love, but help from a calm, steady place is wiser than help from panic.

Time35-45 min
Groupsmall group or full class (6-20), with a private-reflection option
AgeGrade 6-8
Formatin-person or virtual
UpdatedOctober 12, 2026
Also built for:K-2Grade 3-5TeenAge 21+

Assumptions & Caveats

Before you begin

What you should know

This age lives in the group chat. A friend vents again, a sibling forgot a project, a teammate is behind, and the message list starts to feel like a sixty-second scroll of it’s all on me. Many middle schoolers are very good at caring and very quick to carry other people’s problems as their own. Some also quietly wait for someone else to fix things for them. This lesson offers one steadying idea (no one is anyone’s savior; God, divine Love, is the only power, and the people around us are able) and one repeatable check before stepping in. It isn’t about helping less. Hurried, panicked help just isn’t wise help yet, and a short pause often makes the help better. The room is open to every faith background; there is a plain-language version of the big idea below. Nothing to prepare beyond paper and pens, and an optional printout.

What we're assuming

  • The room holds a range of faith backgrounds, from devout to doubting to none. Offer the faith language as an invitation, never a requirement. A plain-language version is: “something good and bigger than any problem is in charge here — not the problem, not them, and not me.”
  • Some students are the one everybody leans on. Some are the one leaning. Some have helped imperfectly and still feel it. No one has to say which.
  • Nobody has to speak. A private-reflection option is offered before any sharing.

Learning goals

What they walk away with

  1. Can name the “it’s all on me” feeling when it shows up while helping a friend.
  2. Feels the difference between help that carries a person and help that stands beside them.
  3. Leaves with a quick check to run before stepping in, and the clear safety rule: if someone is in danger, tell a trusted adult right away.
  4. Can name one real situation, theirs, where they might try the pause.

How to hold the room

Before you start talking

  • Open with curiosity. You’re not teaching “the right way to help”; you’re offering one check.
  • This is never about shaming a past decision. If someone says, “I stepped in and it went badly,” say, “That sounds like it came from real care. This is just something to try next time.”
  • Offer a private-reflection option before any sharing, and a clear way to pass on any beat.
  • Welcome pushback: “but sometimes people really do need help!” is exactly right. Say so.
  • Never ask a student to talk about a specific friend’s private struggle. Categories (“a friend,” “my sibling”) are enough.

The lesson flow

Step by step

Each beat alternates a little input, a little doing, and a little reflecting. Skip, slow down, or stretch any of these to fit your group.

1
~8 min

The sixty-second scroll

Ask the room to imagine this: it’s 9:40 p.m. A friend sends a string of messages about the same problem, again. Another friend texts, “Can you check on her?” Your sibling says they forgot their project is due tomorrow. Your phone is buzzing in your hand.

Say: “Notice what happens in your chest. What does it say?” Give a minute of silent thinking (or writing). Then offer a few common voices as possibilities, not as answers: If I don’t answer, something bad happens. I’m the only one who gets them. If I fix this, I’ll feel better. If I fix this, they’ll like me.

Invite volunteers to name what it feels like, in one word or less. Then say: “That feeling has a name. It’s the part of us that crowns itself the savior. It isn’t bad. It’s just the sign that we’ve started to think it’s all on us.”

2
~10 min

Three grounds, and what the texts say

Offer a simple frame with three questions to check before you help. Write them where the room can see them:

  • The only power: Is God the only power here? (Not the problem, not them, not me.)
  • Them: Are they able and whole? (Capable of their own good. They don’t need me to survive.)
  • Me: Am I on solid ground? (Calm and clear, able to hear wisdom before I act.)

Plain-language version if it’s helpful: “Is something good and bigger than this problem in charge? Can they handle their own life? Am I steady enough to listen?”

Read these together, one at a time, and let them sit:

“Wilt thou be made whole?”

John 5:6, KJV

Notice: before doing anything, he asked. Then:

“Rise, take up thy bed, and walk.”

John 5:8, KJV

Notice: the man did the walking.

”...to show them how to do theirs, but not to do it for them nor to relieve them of a single responsibility.”

Science and Health, Mary Baker Eddy, p. 18:6-9

If anyone asks about “God as the only power”: say it plainly. In this tradition it means nothing has power apart from God, so the problem doesn’t have the last word, the other person isn’t helpless, and I’m not holding the whole world up.

Then name the honest tension: “Does this mean never help? No. It means check the thought you’re helping from.”

3
~12 min

Same moment, two thoughts

In small groups (or pairs, or privately), read these three moments. Each has two versions of the same help, with the thought behind it different. Ask the group to talk, or write silently, about what each person was thinking, and what might be different afterward.

  • A friend keeps venting about the same thing. One helper hands out advice nobody asked for, because “I have to fix this pain.” Another listens, because “they already hold their next step.”
  • A sibling forgot a project. One helper drives it over and panics until it’s done, because “if I don’t step in, they’ll fail.” Another asks, “What’s your plan?”, stays close, and helps where it’s truly needed.
  • A teammate is behind. One helper quietly redoes their part and feels needed, because “nobody else will catch it.” Another asks what’s blocking them and gives what they can freely.

Say: “From the outside, the help can look almost the same. The thought behind it doesn’t.”

A short bit on the mirror images, gently: “There are two other disguises for the same mistake. One is waiting for someone else to rescue us, and the other is saying ‘I’m just trusting God’ as a polite way of stepping back. Neither is faith. Faith stays close.” Don’t dwell on it.

Then ask, openly: “What did you notice? Where did you recognize yourself, or someone you know?”

4
~10 min

A quick check, a safety rule, and a private locate

Teach the quick check, in the simplest form. Say that hurried, panicked help isn’t wise help yet. The check has five questions, in this order. Write them where the room can see them:

  1. Is anyone in danger right now? If yes, tell a trusted adult right away. Their need is real, and you don’t have to hold it alone.
  2. Am I calm enough to listen? If not, breathe and pause. Come back when I am.
  3. Do I see them as able? If not, name one real strength they have, then ask again.
  4. Did they ask me? If not, I ask, “I’m here. What would help?”
  5. Would I be doing what they can do themselves? If yes, stay beside them. If no, give freely.

Then: Either way, stay close. I’m a friend, not the source.

Say plainly that this is a way of thinking, not a rule that fits every situation, and that pausing never means leaving a friend alone with something serious.

Close with a private locate. Hand out paper. “Think of one real situation: someone you’ve been ‘carrying,’ or someone you’ve been waiting on to fix something for you. What would the pause look like?” Write it or just hold it. Nobody shares. Hand out the wallet card and the flow handout.

If it's not going well

When this one gets heavy

Silence is fine; most middle schoolers go somewhere honest in their own head that they won’t say out loud. Pushback (“but sometimes my friend really needs me!”) is welcome and correct. Say so: the lesson never asks anyone to pretend a need isn’t real. It asks them to check the thought the help comes from. A strong emotional reaction, such as a student who has been carrying a lot for a long time, calls for a pause, a breath, and a private check-in; offer space to step out. If a student describes a friend or family member who may be in danger or hurting themselves, don’t work through it in the room: thank them for telling you, don’t promise secrecy, and follow the site’s mentor safety guidelines along with your own local mandatory-reporting obligations. Bring in a trusted adult. That is what this lesson would say, too.

For the fuller tiered guidance — including what to do if someone discloses something serious — see For Mentors.

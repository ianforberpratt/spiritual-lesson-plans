Grade 6-8

Their Candle, Your Candle

When a friend believes differently, the pull to fix it can feel like love, but your relationship with God and theirs are each their own.

Time35-40 min
Groupsmall group or full class (6-20), with a private-reflection option
AgeGrade 6-8
Formatin-person or virtual
UpdatedOctober 8, 2026
Also built for:Grade 3-5TeenAge 21+

Assumptions & Caveats

Before you begin

What you should know

At this age, belonging is everything, and a friend who believes or practices differently can feel like a quiet threat to it. Some students feel pulled to bring friends along; others feel judged for what they do or don’t believe. Both feel real. The idea at the center of this lesson, that each person’s relationship with God is their own, is a relief for the first group and a gift to the second. Quiet in this room is very often engagement, not disinterest, and the private-reflection beat exists because most middle schoolers will go somewhere honest in their own head that they won’t say out loud. There’s nothing you need to prepare beyond paper.

What we're assuming

  • The room holds a range of faith backgrounds, including students who don’t practice any. Don’t assume everyone shares a word for God, a belief in prayer, or a home that talks about any of it.
  • Some students may have friends or family members who’ve told them their way is wrong. Don’t assume anyone wants to say so.
  • No one has to share out loud. Private thinking is a complete, successful use of this lesson.

Learning goals

What they walk away with

  1. Leaves feeling lighter about not being responsible for how anyone else’s relationship with God turns out.
  2. Can name the difference between sharing something they love and needing someone to respond a certain way.
  3. Has one repeatable private question for the next time they feel the pull: “Do I need them to respond a certain way for this to count?”
  4. Knows that “not my job to convert” never means “not my job to care”: if someone is unsafe, bring in a caring adult.

How to hold the room

Before you start talking

  • Open with real curiosity, not a setup for a “right answer.” This is a topic where some students will have complicated feelings in both directions.
  • Never require sharing. Offer it, and mean it when most of the room stays quiet.
  • Don’t let anyone become the “representative” of a faith or lack of one. If someone gets put on the spot, step in gently: “Nobody has to speak for anybody.”
  • If a student says “other religions are wrong,” don’t correct them; ask, “What makes you feel that way?” or “Has someone told you that?” Honest disagreement is the lesson working.
  • Anyone can pass on any beat.

The lesson flow

Step by step

Each beat alternates a little input, a little doing, and a little reflecting. Skip, slow down, or stretch any of these to fit your group.

1
~6 min

The pull

Ask, no sharing required yet: “Have you ever wanted someone you care about to see something the way you do? A friend, a cousin, someone in your group chat? Maybe about a game, a show, a belief, anything?” Let a couple of volunteers offer a one-word category if they want (“music,” “a team,” “what I believe”). Then, a little more quietly: “And has anyone ever felt that pull toward you, someone who wanted you to come around to their way?” Let that sit. Don’t push.

2
~10 min

The candle, told well

Tell it like a scene, in your own words. Read Psalms 18:28 and let it land: “For thou wilt light my candle: the Lord my God will enlighten my darkness.” Then Isaiah 45:5: “I girded thee, though thou hast not known me.”

Slow down on two things. Who lights the candle? The person who wrote it wasn’t talking about a friend, a teacher, or a parent. And: God was with the person “though thou hast not known me.” The relationship didn’t begin when someone told them about it, and it doesn’t depend on whether someone else did their job.

Then add a modern picture: “Imagine you’re sitting with a friend who thinks about God completely differently than you do, or not at all. Your job isn’t to light their candle. You couldn’t if you tried. Your job is to keep yours lit, honestly, and to be a good friend while theirs burns the way it burns.”

3
~12 min

Private reflection

Hand out paper. Ask students to think of one person, privately, whose way of thinking about God (or not) is different from their own. They don’t write the name. Offer these three prompts, and let them take as long as they want with any of them:

  1. What do I genuinely love or respect about this person?
  2. Is there anything I’ve been tempted to fix, push, or prove to them?
  3. If I let go of needing them to see it my way, what would I do instead?

Give real quiet time, at least five uninterrupted minutes. Afterward, offer, don’t require, sharing: “If anyone wants to share what they noticed, I’d love to hear it. And it’s completely fine to keep it.”

4
~10 min

A test and a safety line

Read this aloud:

”‘Work out your own salvation,’ is the demand of Life and Love, for to this end God worketh with you.”

Science and Health, Mary Baker Eddy, p. 22:11–12

Say it plainly: salvation is the old word for being brought into the fullness of God’s care, and this passage says each person works it out with God, and that God is the one working with them. Not us, and not anyone else.

Teach the test, simply: “Do I need them to respond a certain way for this to count as a good conversation?” If the answer is yes, I’ve slid from sharing into steering. If I can say what I love and be at peace however they respond, that’s sharing.

Then say the safety line directly: “None of this means you stop caring. If a friend is ever unsafe or hurting themselves or someone else, you don’t wait or stay quiet, you bring in a caring adult. That isn’t pushing your beliefs; that’s love.” Hand out the wallet card.

What you'll need

The materials

Wallet card God Lights Every Candle "*Your relationship with God is yours. Theirs is theirs. Your part is to keep your own light honest.*" Open printable handout →
  • Paper and pens, or a private digital note, for the reflection beat
  • This lesson’s printable wallet card, “God Lights Every Candle” — hand it out as something to keep, not to fill out.

If it's not going well

When this one gets heavy

Silence is expected and successful; don’t fill it. Pushback (“but what if my religion IS the right one?”) is welcome. Meet it honestly: “You can absolutely believe that. This isn’t asking you to give it up. It’s asking what you do with someone who sees it differently.” A strong emotional reaction (a student who’s been told their family is wrong, or who’s carrying a fear for someone they love) calls for a pause, a private check-in, and space to step out if needed. A disclosure of real harm, something well beyond an ordinary disagreement about belief, should not be worked through in the moment. Stay warm, receive it (“thank you for telling me, I take that seriously”), and follow the site’s mentor safety guidelines along with your own local mandatory-reporting obligations. This age can disclose real concerns inside a quiet written reflection rather than out loud, so read what’s handed to you with that in mind.

For the fuller tiered guidance — including what to do if someone discloses something serious — see For Mentors.

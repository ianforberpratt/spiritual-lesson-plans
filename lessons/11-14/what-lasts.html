A lesson · What’s Already There

Grade 6-8

What’s Already There

A scared servant sees an army surrounding the city. His teacher sees the same mountain and asks God for one thing: open his eyes.

Time35-40 min
Groupsmall group or full class (6-20), with a private-reflection option
AgeGrade 6-8
Formatin-person or virtual
UpdatedSeptember 13, 2026
Also built for:K-2Grade 3-5TeenAge 21+

Assumptions & Caveats

Before you begin

What you should know

This age’s self-worth is unusually externally anchored — comparison, grades, and group chats can loom as large and real as an actual army. That’s exactly the shape of the story in II Kings 6: the servant isn’t wrong about what he sees, he’s just not seeing the whole picture yet. Use the story as the centerpiece, not background reading — tell it, don’t summarize it. Quiet in this room is very often engagement, not disinterest; the private-reflection format exists because most middle schoolers will go somewhere honest in their own head that they will never say out loud in front of peers, and that’s a complete, successful use of this lesson.

What we're assuming

  • The room holds a real range of faith backgrounds and starting points — don’t assume everyone already believes prayer changes what someone sees, and don’t assume shared trauma or a shared “army.”
  • Every student can privately name at least one thing that currently feels like it’s looming over them — this doesn’t require anyone to share what it is.
  • Comparison and social pressure at this age can shade into real distress, not just ordinary discomfort — watch for a student who seems more anxious than reflective during the private-thinking beat.

Learning goals

What they walk away with

  1. Leaves with the private, felt experience of imagining their own eyes opened to something already true — not with an answer handed to them.
  2. Understands that whatever looms biggest in a moment — comparison, a fear, a group chat — isn’t automatically the most real or permanent thing in the room.
  3. Has a repeatable private question for when something feels like it’s surrounding them: “What might already be true here that I can’t see yet?”
  4. Can fully participate without saying a single word out loud.

How to hold the room

Before you start talking

  • Tell the story like it matters, not like background reading — the servant’s fear, the mountain, Elisha’s one request. Slow down at “Lord, I pray thee, open his eyes.”
  • Never require sharing. Offer it — “if anyone wants to share, I’d love to hear it” — and mean it when most of the room stays quiet.
  • If the room goes very still during the private-reflection beat, that’s the lesson landing, not a stall. Let it sit.

The lesson flow

Step by step

Each beat alternates a little input, a little doing, and a little reflecting. Skip, slow down, or stretch any of these to fit your group.

1
~5 min

Naming what looms

Ask, no sharing required yet: “Has anything ever felt like it was ‘surrounding’ you — a fear, a comparison, a problem that seemed way bigger than everything else?” Let a couple of volunteers offer a one-word category if they want (not details) — “grades,” “a friend group,” “how I look.”

2
~10 min

The story, told well

Read or tell II Kings 6:15–17 as a real scene: the servant wakes up, sees the city surrounded by horses and chariots, panics — “Alas, my master! how shall we do?” Elisha’s answer: “Fear not: for they that be with us are more than they that be with them.” Then the prayer: “Lord, I pray thee, open his eyes.” And the servant sees — the same mountain, now full of horses and chariots of fire. Nothing in the physical situation changed. What he could see did.

3
~12 min

Private reflection

Hand out paper, or have students open a private note. Ask them to think of something right now that feels like it’s “surrounding” them — without writing what it specifically is, if they’d rather not. Prompt: “If your eyes were opened right now, what do you think you’d actually see was already there, that you can’t see yet?” Give real quiet time — at least five uninterrupted minutes. Offer, don’t require, sharing afterward.

4
~8 min

Ground it and carry it

Introduce, in plain language: “Spirit is the only substance and consciousness recognized by divine Science... Spirit can have no opposite.” — Science and Health, Mary Baker Eddy, p. 278:16–19. Put it simply: the fear-army was never the substantial thing in that story — it just looked biggest. Teach the carry-question: “What might already be true here that I can’t see yet?” — something they can use privately, any time something starts to feel like it’s surrounding them.

If it's not going well

When this one gets heavy

Silence is the expected, successful shape of this lesson — don’t fill it or read it as failure. Pushback or skepticism about prayer “changing what you see” is welcome — meet it honestly (“I don’t think it’s magic, either — I think it’s more like your attention shifting to what was already real”) rather than defending it. A strong emotional reaction during the private-reflection beat (visible distress, tears) calls for a pause, a private check-in, and space to step out if needed. A disclosure of real harm — something well beyond ordinary middle-school comparison and pressure — should not be worked through in the moment; stay warm, receive it (“thank you for telling me, I take that seriously”), and follow the site’s mentor safety guidelines along with your own church’s mandatory-reporting obligations. This age can disclose real concerns inside a quiet written reflection rather than out loud — read what’s handed to you with that in mind.

For the fuller tiered guidance — including what to do if someone discloses something serious — see For Mentors.

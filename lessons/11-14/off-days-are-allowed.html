A lesson · Off Days Are Allowed

Middle School (Grades 6–8)

Off Days Are Allowed

Feeling off doesn’t mean you’re doing it wrong — it means you’re having a day.

Time35-40 min
Groupsmall-group or full class (4-16 kids)
AgeMiddle School (Grades 6–8)
Formatin-person or virtual
Also built for:5–8 · Early Elementary (K–2)8–11 · Upper Elementary (Grades 3–5)14–21 · Teen & Early College21+ · College Student & Adult (21+)

Assumptions & Caveats

Before you begin

What you should know

This is arguably the age that needs this lesson most and can handle it least comfortably out loud. Abstract thinking is coming online, so they genuinely can grasp “an off day doesn’t mean I’m doing something wrong” as an idea — but this is also the age where self-worth gets tied up in performance and peer perception, and where seeming fine in front of others starts to matter intensely. Don’t be surprised if the room goes quiet rather than talkative during the vulnerable parts — quiet is often engagement at this age, not disinterest. Give a private option before asking anyone to share out loud; forced vulnerability at this age often produces performance, not honesty.

What we're assuming

  • Everyone in the room has had an off day — low, disconnected, more human than usual. We’re not assuming anyone will describe theirs, or that it’s tied to anything specific.
  • Some kids this age already carry the sneaky belief that a bad day is evidence they’re failing at something — being positive, being spiritual, being put-together. That belief, not the off day itself, is what this lesson is aimed at.
  • Some kids in the room may be dealing with real anxiety or pressure right now. This lesson can be moving for them — or it can surface something that needs more support than a class setting. See “If it’s not going well” below.

Learning goals

What they walk away with

  1. Name the sneaky belief — “if I were doing this right, I wouldn’t feel this way” — as the actual problem, not the off day itself.
  2. Understand the difference between a real feeling and the guilt trap of grading themselves for having it.
  3. Practice saying a hard truth and a hopeful one in the same breath, without picking one to fake.
  4. Have at least one written, private reflection they don’t have to share with anyone — this age needs the option of privacy to be honest at all.

How to hold the room

Before you start talking

  • This can surface real low mood, not just a rough afternoon. Watch for signs it’s more than “off” — see the safety note below.
  • Don’t rush to fix the feeling. The goal here is normalizing it, not curing it in one session.
  • Resist correcting a kid’s honest cynicism if they say something like “yeah but I actually am behind on stuff.” They might be right that something’s behind — the lesson isn’t that nothing’s ever actually wrong, it’s that a hard feeling by itself isn’t proof of failure.

The lesson flow

Step by step

Each beat alternates a little input, a little doing, and a little reflecting. Skip, slow down, or stretch any of these to fit your group.

1
~5 min

Naming an off day

Ask (open floor or written on index cards if the group is shy): “Think of a recent day you felt ‘off’ — disconnected, low, more human than usual. No shame in it. Just notice it happened, and that it’s not unusual.”

You don’t need everyone to share out loud. Let a few go first; others can just think or write.

2
~7 min

The trap of grading yourself

Name the sneaky belief out loud: “if I were doing things right, I wouldn’t feel this way.” That belief is the actual problem — not the off day.

Discuss (pairs or small groups of 3, more comfortable than whole-group at this age): “Have you ever felt guilty about feeling bad — like the feeling itself was proof you were failing at something?”

3
~8 min

Private reflection, before any sharing

Give everyone paper. This part is not shared aloud unless they choose to.

Prompt: “Where do you feel like you’re supposed to seem fine even when you’re not? What would change if a hard day stopped counting as evidence against you?”

Give real silence for this — 4-5 minutes of actual quiet, not filled with talking.

4
~10 min

You’re not being tested

“You’re not a student being graded on how spiritual, positive, or put-together you’re managing to feel today. Being honest — ‘I know things are okay, but right now I feel scared’ — isn’t a failure. It’s just honest, and both things can be true at once.”

Read together:

“God is Love. Can we ask Him to be more?”

Science and Health, p. 2:23

Try it (optional sharing, not required): “Practice saying, out loud or on paper: ‘I know ___ is true, but right now I feel ___.’”

5
~6 min

Information, not indictment

“An off day isn’t a punishment or a sign you’re behind. Sometimes it’s just where you are today.”

“Man is God’s reflection, needing no cultivation, but ever beautiful and complete.”

Science and Health, p. 527:4

“You don’t lose your wholeness on a bad day. It was never something you had to keep earning.”

What you'll need

The materials

  • Paper and pens for each participant (index cards work well for Beat 1 if the group is shy)
  • A whiteboard, poster paper, or shared doc if useful for discussion
  • Optional printables: Handout “Both Things Are True”; Keepsake “Permission Slip”

If it's not going well

When this one gets heavy

This age carries real developmental risk around self-worth and self-judgment — this lesson can surface genuine distress, not just useful discomfort. If a student’s private reflection, body language, or comments suggest something beyond typical middle-school pressure — persistent hopelessness, losing interest in things they used to enjoy, or any mention of self-harm — don’t try to reframe it away. Follow the site’s mentor safety guidelines (For Mentors page), loop in the appropriate adult per your church’s child-safety policy, and take it as seriously as you would any other disclosure at this age — it can arrive quietly, inside a written reflection rather than spoken aloud.

For the fuller tiered guidance — including what to do if someone discloses something serious — see For Mentors.

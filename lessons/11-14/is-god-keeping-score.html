A lesson · Is God Keeping Score?

Grade 6-8

Is God Keeping Score?

One truth taught at every age here, anchored in what actually keeps score in a middle schooler’s life right now — grades, likes, and peer comparison.

Time35–40 min
GroupSmall group or full class (4–16 kids)
AgeGrade 6-8
FormatIn-person or virtual
UpdatedAugust 25, 2026
Also built for:K-2Grade 3-5Age 21+

Assumptions & Caveats

Before you begin

What you should know

This is arguably the age group that needs this lesson most and can handle it least comfortably out loud. Abstract thinking is coming online, which means they can now genuinely grasp “God’s nature is Love, not a performance-based reward” as an idea — but this is also the age where social comparison, grades, and online approval start to feel like the realest scoreboard in the room, and self-worth is unusually tied up in performance and peer perception. Don’t be surprised if the room goes quiet rather than talkative during the vulnerable parts — quiet is often engagement at this age, not disinterest.

Unlike the adult version, keep the examples relational and personal (friendships, family, social media, grades) rather than geopolitical or workplace. This age group is still building the capacity to reason about large-scale conflict in the abstract; keep it close to their actual daily life, where the lesson will land harder anyway.

What we're assuming

  • Most kids this age are already fluent in some form of visible scorekeeping — grades, followers, likes, streaks, team rankings, comparison with siblings or classmates.
  • Self-worth is genuinely more fragile and more externally anchored at this age than either younger or older groups — this is a known developmental feature, not a flaw in any individual kid. Treat the topic with real care, not just as a fun analogy.
  • Some kids in the room may currently be dealing with real anxiety, body image concerns, or family pressure around performance. This lesson can be moving for them — or it can occasionally surface something that needs more support than a class setting. See “If it’s not going well” below.
  • Give kids a private option (see Beat 4) before asking anyone to share out loud. Forced vulnerability at this age often produces performance, not honesty.

Learning goals

What they walk away with

  1. Name specifically how “scorekeeping” shows up in their own life right now (grades, social media, comparison, sports).
  2. Understand the real distinction between being loved for something and being loved as someone — not as a slogan, but as something they’ve tested against a real memory.
  3. Carry the three-question framework into one real relationship or moment this week.
  4. Have at least one written, private reflection they don’t have to share with anyone — this age needs the option of privacy to be honest at all.

How to hold the room

Before you start talking

  • Don’t require anyone to share. Offer it, make it easy, and let silence be okay.
  • Resist correcting or “fixing” a kid’s honest cynicism if they say something like “yeah but grades actually do matter.” They’re right that grades matter practically — the lesson isn’t that performance doesn’t matter in the world, it’s that a person’s worth isn’t the same thing as their performance. Hold both truths rather than arguing one away.
  • Watch for the kid who goes quiet in a way that feels heavier than typical middle-school reserve. Note it, check in one-on-one after.

The lesson flow

Step by step

Each beat alternates a little input, a little doing, and a little reflecting. Skip, slow down, or stretch any of these to fit your group.

1
~5 min

Naming the scoreboard

Ask (open floor or written on index cards if the group is shy): “Where in your life right now does it feel like you’re being scored? Grades, social media, sports, comparison to a sibling or friend — name it.”

You don’t need everyone to share out loud. Let a few go first; others can just think or write.

2
~8 min

The ledger-God, named honestly

“A lot of people — even people who go to church or Sunday School every week — quietly believe God is one more thing keeping score. Not because anyone taught it directly, but because it’s the pattern everything else in life runs on, so it’s the pattern that sneaks into how we picture God too.”

Read together:

“God is Love. Can we ask Him to be more?”

Science and Health, p. 2:23

“The Lord your God is with you... He will take great delight in you; in his love he will no longer rebuke you, but will rejoice over you with singing.”

Zephaniah 3:17

Ask: “That’s a pretty different picture than a report card or a follower count. What’s actually different about a love that doesn’t grade you?”

3
~8 min

Loved for vs. loved as

“There’s a real difference between being loved for something — your grades, your looks, your performance on a team, how many people like your posts — and being loved as someone, just because you exist. Most of us have felt both kinds, sometimes from the same people, in the same week.”

Discuss (pairs or small groups of 3, more comfortable than whole-group at this age):

  1. “Think of a time you felt loved for something you did. Now think of a time you felt loved as who you are, no performance attached. What was actually different about how each one felt in your body?”
  2. “Is there a relationship in your life right now where you’re not sure which kind you’re getting?”
4
~8 min

Private reflection

Give everyone paper. This part is not shared aloud unless they choose to.

Prompt: “Where in your life do you feel like you have to perform to be worth loving? What would change if you actually believed that wasn’t true — starting with God, and maybe spreading from there?”

Give real silence for this — 4–5 minutes of actual quiet, not filled with talking.

5
~6 min

Three questions, middle-school version

Introduce (write where everyone can see):

  1. Am I trying to win this, or trying to understand it?
  2. Does someone disagreeing with me — or not liking my post, or being better at something than me — actually threaten who I am?
  3. Do I need someone else to lose (an argument, a comparison, a spot on a team) for me to feel secure?

“This week, pick one real moment — a group chat, a comparison with a friend, an argument with a parent — and try answering these honestly, not impressively.”

6

Closing

“Divine Love always has met and always will meet every human need.”

Science and Health, p. 494:10

“You are not currently being graded by God. You never have been. Whatever the rest of this week scores you on, that one stays true.”

What you'll need

The materials

  • Paper and pens for each participant (index cards work well for Beat 1 if the group is shy)
  • A whiteboard, poster paper, or shared doc for the three questions in Beat 5

If it's not going well

When this one gets heavy

This age carries real developmental risk around self-worth, body image, and social comparison — this lesson can surface genuine distress, not just useful discomfort. If a student’s private reflection, body language, or comments suggest something beyond typical middle-school pressure (persistent hopelessness, signs of self-harm, disclosure of harm from someone else), don’t try to resolve it in class. Follow the site’s mentor safety guidelines (For Mentors page), loop in the appropriate adult per your church’s child-safety policy, and treat it as seriously as you would any other disclosure at this age — it can arrive quietly, inside a written reflection rather than spoken aloud.

For the fuller tiered guidance — including what to do if someone discloses something serious — see For Mentors.

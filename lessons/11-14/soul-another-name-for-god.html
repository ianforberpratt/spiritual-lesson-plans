A lesson · Soul: Another Name for God

Grade 6-8

Soul: Another Name for God

Where your real identity actually comes from — and why a bad moment, a mean impulse, or a hard feeling about your body doesn’t get to define you.

Time40–45 min
GroupSmall group or full class (4–16 kids)
AgeGrade 6-8
Formatin-person or virtual
UpdatedAugust 20, 2026
Also built for:TeenAge 21+

Assumptions & Caveats

Before you begin

What you should know

This age is old enough to genuinely grasp “my identity comes from somewhere deeper than my worst moment” as a real idea, not just a story — but the four propensities named in the original passage need real adaptation, not a direct translation. “Lust” in particular gets reframed here toward body image and appearance comparison rather than anything sexual — that’s the developmentally appropriate landing spot for this age, and it’s also genuinely rich territory, since body image pressure is one of the sharpest, most constant “mental conspirators” middle schoolers actually live with.

Keep hatred, revenge, and deceit anchored in real middle-school life too: pile-on comments and group exclusion, subtweeting or freezing a friend out, and a curated online identity or lying to fit in. The goal isn’t to introduce new shame about any of these — most kids in the room have already done at least one of them — it’s to locate a real, permanent identity underneath all four that the propensity can’t actually touch.

What we're assuming

  • Most kids this age have felt real pressure around how they look, especially online, and many have already participated in at least one of the four patterns named here (a pile-on, a freeze-out, a curated post) without necessarily thinking of it that way.
  • Body image is genuinely sensitive territory at this age — some kids in the room may be dealing with real anxiety, disordered eating patterns, or comparison-driven distress. Treat this beat with real care, not just as one example among four.
  • This is not a confession exercise. Nobody should be asked to admit which propensity is “theirs” out loud.
  • Give kids a private-reflection option before any sharing — this age needs the option of privacy to be honest at all.

Learning goals

What they walk away with

  1. A working sense that Soul — not the body, not a screen, not a mistake — is where real identity actually comes from.
  2. The ability to name, in their own words, one real version of each of the four patterns (hatred, body-image pressure, revenge, deceit) as it actually shows up at their age.
  3. One phrase or question they can reach for in a real moment: “This isn’t who I am — it’s just what showed up.”
  4. A private, written reflection on one real situation where they could practice answering from that deeper identity.

How to hold the room

Before you start talking

  • Handle the body-image beat with extra care — don’t rush it, and don’t let it become a group critique session of anyone’s appearance, including celebrities or classmates.
  • Keep all four patterns at the level of “this happens to lots of people,” not “raise your hand if you’ve done this.”
  • If a student seems more distressed than curious during the body-image discussion, don’t press for more in the group setting — follow up individually after.
  • Resist any drift toward “good kid vs. bad kid” framing. The whole point is that a mean moment or a hard body-image day doesn’t define anyone.

The lesson flow

Step by step

Each beat alternates a little input, a little doing, and a little reflecting. Skip, slow down, or stretch any of these to fit your group.

1
~6 min

Where does “you” actually come from?

Ask: “If someone said something mean to you, or you did something you regret, does that become who you are — or does it just... happen, and then pass?” Let a few kids answer.

“Christian Science has an idea about this: there’s a name for God — Soul — that’s also about where your real identity comes from. Not your body, not a mistake, not a bad day. Something underneath all of that, that doesn’t change.”

2
~8 min

Read it, then decode it together

Read, slowly:

“Christian Science commands man to master the propensities, — to hold hatred in abeyance with kindness, to conquer lust with chastity, revenge with charity, and to overcome deceit with honesty. Choke these errors in their early stages...”

Science and Health, p. 405:5–11

Unpack a few key words together: “abeyance” means put on hold, not erased — you don’t have to make a feeling disappear, just not let it run the show. “Choke these errors in their early stages” — catching something small before it grows, like a weed before it roots.

Ask: “What’s the difference between erasing a feeling and just not letting it be in charge?”

3
~15 min

Four real patterns, one at a time

Introduce the four, one at a time, each with its real middle-school version:

  1. Hatred → kindness. Pile-on comments, ganging up on someone, group exclusion.
  2. Body pressure → self-respect. Comparing your body to someone online, feeling like you’re not enough because of how you look.
  3. Revenge → charity. Subtweeting, freezing a friend out, petty payback.
  4. Deceit → honesty. A curated online identity that doesn’t match real life, lying to fit in.

For each one, ask (privately, on paper — not shared aloud): “Has something like this ever shown up for you? What would it look like to notice it without letting it decide who you are?”

Give real quiet time for this — several minutes of actual silence.

4
~8 min

Your own definition

In pairs or small groups, have kids build a simple, real definition of Soul in their own words — a caption, a drawing, or a single sentence finishing “The real me is —.” Encourage something they could actually say to a friend, not a vocabulary-word answer.

5

Closing

“None of the four things we named today — a mean moment, a hard body-image day, a petty payback, a lie to fit in — is who you actually are. Soul is who you actually are, and it doesn’t change based on your worst five minutes.”

What you'll need

The materials

  • Paper and pens for each participant
  • A whiteboard or shared doc for Beat 2’s word-by-word unpacking
  • Optional handout: “Not the Impulse”

If it's not going well

When this one gets heavy

This age carries real developmental risk around body image, self-worth, and social comparison — the Beat 3 body-pressure reflection especially can surface genuine distress, not just useful discomfort. If a student’s writing, body language, or comments suggest something beyond ordinary appearance concern (signs of disordered eating, persistent hopelessness, self-harm), don’t try to resolve it in class. Follow the site’s mentor safety guidelines (For Mentors page) and loop in the appropriate adult per your church’s child-safety policy.

For the fuller tiered guidance — including what to do if someone discloses something serious — see For Mentors.

A lesson · Is Regret a Fair and Just Teacher?

College Student & Adult (21+)

Is Regret a Fair and Just Teacher?

A practical path from a hard decision to moving forward — without pretending it didn’t happen.

Time50-60 min
Group1:1 or small group
AgeCollege Student & Adult (21+)
Formatin-person or virtual
Also built for:11–14 · Middle School (Grades 6–8)14–21 · Teen & Early College

Assumptions & Caveats

Before you begin

What you should know

By adulthood, most people aren’t short on reflective capacity — if anything, they’ve had years or decades of practice running the same regret on a loop. What’s different here isn’t the thinking skill; it’s the stakes and the time carried. A regret at this age might be a career turn not taken, a marriage or relationship decision, a way a person parented (or didn’t) at a hard moment, a financial call, or a period of life spent a certain way. These carry real, sometimes irreversible consequences, which makes “release the shame without pretending it didn’t happen” a genuinely harder needle to thread than it is for a teenager — don’t rush it. Adults are also more likely to have already tried, and failed, to simply “get over” something through willpower alone; be ready for some skepticism that this will be any different, and let that skepticism be in the room rather than arguing it away.

What we're assuming

  • Everyone present carries at least one decision — or a pattern of decisions — they still think about. Some may be recent; some may be decades old and still live.
  • Participants aren’t expected to disclose the specific regret, and some regrets in the room may involve other people (an ex-spouse, a estranged adult child, a former colleague) in ways that make even vague disclosure feel risky. Full privacy is a legitimate choice, not an evasion.
  • Some in the room may be sitting with regret that’s tangled up with grief, addiction recovery, or a loss that can’t be undone. This lesson can be genuinely useful for that, but it isn’t grief counseling or a substitute for it.

Learning goals

What they walk away with

  1. Participants distinguish between honest acknowledgment of a decision and being permanently sentenced to relive it, even decades later.
  2. Participants gain a concrete, applicable framework: observe clearly, release shame intentionally, identify the underlying fear, make a deliberate choice about how to carry it forward, remain open to further adjustment, and recognize what the experience actually revealed.
  3. Participants recognize that changing course — even very late, even after years of one way of thinking or living — demonstrates growth, not proof the earlier years were wasted.
  4. Participants leave feeling permitted to stop self-punishment around something specific, whether or not anyone else has ever offered them that permission.

How to hold the room

Before you start talking

  • These regrets often carry real, unresolved consequences for other people (a former partner, a child, a business the person left). Don’t rush toward tidy resolution where none may be possible; honest processing doesn’t require a clean outcome.
  • Avoid premature reassurance (“it worked out fine,” “you did the best you could”) — it can land as dismissive of a genuinely complicated decision made with real information at the time.
  • Watch for the difference between someone processing a real regret and someone using the session to re-litigate a decision that was, in fact, reasonable given what they knew — sometimes the work is releasing self-blame for a decision that was never actually a failure.
  • Monitor for shame spirals, which can be more entrenched at this age simply from more years of practice. If someone is deepening into despair rather than processing, pause or decelerate significantly.

The lesson flow

Step by step

Each beat alternates a little input, a little doing, and a little reflecting. Skip, slow down, or stretch any of these to fit your group.

1
~10 min

Naming the Decision, Without Shame

Recall a decision — something enacted or omitted, at whatever scale it actually sits at (a career choice, a relationship, a parenting moment, a financial call, a season of life) — that you continue to carry. Disclosure is optional; naming the category (career, relationship, family, money) is enough if that’s as far as it goes today.

Highlight: “Seeing a decision honestly isn’t the same as being sentenced by it — even if you’ve been carrying the sentence for twenty years.”

2
~12-15 min

The Clean Slate

Deliberately set shame aside — not by forgetting what occurred or minimizing its real consequences, but by ceasing the daily re-trial of it. Notice the fear underneath the story (“I ruined it,” “I’m the reason it fell apart,” “I wasted those years”) and question whether that fear reflects the full reality, or only the harshest possible reading of it.

Discuss: What distinguishes remembering a decision from remaining imprisoned by it? Has the ongoing self-judgment actually changed anything about the outcome, or only added more weight to carry?

3
~12-15 min

Choosing, and Being Willing to Adjust

At some point, circling has to stop and a choice — however modest, however late — becomes necessary: a repair attempted, a different way of showing up starting now, a boundary set, an amends made where one is actually possible. Course corrections made years later are still evidence of growth, not proof the earlier version of yourself was worthless.

Discuss: Recall a moment — recent or long past — where you changed direction after getting something wrong. Did it feel like failure, or like the most honest thing you’d done in a while?

4
~10-12 min

Gratitude, Even for the Rocky Parts

Identify, silently or aloud, one thing the difficult decision actually taught you — about yourself, about what you value, about how you want to show up now. This doesn’t require the decision to have been “worth it” or the outcome to have been good; it only asks what’s genuinely true now, looking back.

Closing statement: “You are not what you regret. You’re allowed to learn from it and put it down — both, not just one — no matter how long you’ve been carrying it.”

What you'll need

The materials

  • Paper and pens, or a shared digital document
  • A private or semi-private space — adult-scale regrets often involve other real people and can be harder to voice in a large group

If it's not going well

When this one gets heavy

Regret at this age can be entangled with clinical depression, complicated grief, or addiction in ways a single session isn’t equipped to resolve. If a participant expresses that they don’t deserve forgiveness, that a relationship or life is permanently ruined beyond any possibility of repair, or anything suggesting hopelessness about themselves or their future, treat it seriously — decelerate significantly, and follow the site’s mentor safety guidelines (For Mentors page), which include guidance on when to point someone toward professional counseling alongside spiritual support.

For the fuller tiered guidance — including what to do if someone discloses something serious — see For Mentors.

You Are Not the Struggle

Can you be uncomfortable, angry, or impatient — and still be demonstrating truth? On sitting with the “feels” mid-healing, and why a slow journey still counts as doing much.

Time45–60 min
Group1:1 or group
AgeAge 21+
Formatin-person or virtual
UpdatedAugust 25, 2026
Also built for:Grade 6-8Teen

Assumptions & Caveats

Before you begin

What you should know

A friend with experience in Christian Science once asked a question that stopped this lesson’s author in her tracks:

“In CS, we’re taught to have a very clear-cut separation between truth and error, spirit and matter. So how do we intentionally ‘sit with discomfort’ to remove the stinger from the serpent — take away its supposed power

and also not sit with it in a way where we make discomfort our own, or legitimize it?”

That question sits at the center of this lesson, and it’s a real tension for anyone doing healing work — professionally or personally. As a social worker, the author often asks people to sit in the “feels” while they’re in the middle of healing. That’s not always a comfortable ask. It leans right into the paradox of being human and spiritually perfect at the same time.

One passage from Science and Health can land hard on this question:

“The ancient prophets gained their foresight from a spiritual, incorporeal standpoint, not by foreshadowing evil and mistaking fact for fiction... When sufficiently advanced in Science to be in harmony with the truth of being, men become seers and prophets involuntarily, controlled not by demons, spirits, or demigods, but by the one Spirit.”

Science and Health with Key to the Scriptures, Mary Baker Eddy, p. 84:3

Read one way, that passage can make a person feel like they’re not “sufficiently advanced” — and that can sting. Most people aren’t “there” in the way that passage describes. But there’s a second, quieter thread running through Mary Baker Eddy’s writing — one about the journey itself, and the compassion it takes to stay on it:

“Imperfect mortals grasp the ultimate of spiritual perfection slowly; but to begin aright and to continue the strife of demonstrating the great problem of being, is doing much.”

Science and Health with Key to the Scriptures, Mary Baker Eddy, p. 254:12

”...they rejoice in the small understanding they have already gained of the wholeness of Deity, and work gradually and gently up toward the perfect thought divine. This meekness will increase their apprehension of God, because their mental struggles and pride of opinion will proportionately diminish.”

Unity of Good, Mary Baker Eddy, p. 5:3

Here’s the reframe this lesson rests on: Christian Science’s clear separation between truth and error was never about avoiding contact with error. It’s about refusing to mistake error for something with independent power or permanence. Those aren’t the same move. A person can look straight at discomfort without flinching, and still refuse to grant it citizenship in their sense of self.

Getting defensive, feeling the sting, reacting before you mean to — none of that is cheating on the Absolute. It’s just being human long enough to notice something needs looking at. The only real cheating is believing the reaction gets the final word.

What we're assuming

  • Some participants will hear “spiritual perfection” and immediately feel behind — not enough, not advanced enough, not there yet. That reaction is welcome in this room, not something to talk anyone out of.
  • This lesson isn’t asking anyone to suppress or perform past their real feelings. It’s the opposite: it’s making room to feel the discomfort honestly and keep it from having the final say.
  • Some participants may connect this to their own recovery, healing, or long slow-going personal journey. If someone shares something vulnerable and specific, follow their lead — don’t chase it, and don’t need it to resolve neatly.
  • “Doing much” is not a consolation prize. It’s the actual claim of this lesson: the slow, human, imperfect middle of the journey counts.

Learning goals

What they walk away with

  1. A working answer to the friend’s original question — how to sit with discomfort without either denying it or making it permanent.
  2. A felt distinction between looking at error honestly and legitimizing it as something with real power over identity.
  3. Permission to be exactly where they are on the journey — including reactive, defensive, or impatient — without treating that as failure or as proof they’re “not there yet.”
  4. One real, current situation where they can practice noticing a reaction without letting it have the final word.

How to hold the room

Before you start talking

Before you start talking: - This lesson can surface real vulnerability — old wounds, addiction or recovery, long personal struggles. Let it land. Don’t rush past it to get to the next beat. - You’re not there to resolve the paradox neatly by the end of the hour. Sitting with an unresolved tension honestly is more the point than closing it. - If someone shares something like a recovery story, receive it the way the author receives her own — without needing to reframe it as regret, tragedy, or a lesson learned the hard way. Growth can be true without the story needing to be sad. - Watch for anyone quietly measuring themselves against “sufficiently advanced” language and finding themselves lacking. Naming that out loud, gently, can do a lot of work.

The lesson flow

Step by step

Each beat alternates a little input, a little doing, and a little reflecting. Skip, slow down, or stretch any of these to fit your group.

1
~8–10 min

Naming the paradox

Read the friend’s original question aloud, slowly:

“How do we intentionally ‘sit with discomfort’ to remove the stinger from the serpent — take away its supposed power

and also not sit with it in a way where we make discomfort our own, or legitimize it?”

Discuss: 1. In your own words, what’s the tension in that question? 2. Have you ever felt like you had to choose between honestly feeling something and “doing it right” spiritually? What did that feel like?

2
~10–12 min

The uncomfortable citation

Read the p. 84:3 passage aloud. Let the room sit with it for a moment before discussing.

Discuss: 1. Be honest — does language like “sufficiently advanced” make you feel like you’re behind, or not there yet? What does that feeling do in your body? 2. Where do you think that feeling comes from — the text itself, or something you brought into the room with you?

3
~12–15 min

The comfort, and the reframe

Read the p. 254:12 and Unity of Good passages aloud. Then introduce the reframe: the clear separation between truth and error was never about avoiding error — it’s about refusing to mistake error for something with independent power or permanence. A person can look straight at discomfort without flinching, and still refuse to let it define them.

Discuss: 1. What’s the actual difference between looking at discomfort honestly and legitimizing it as permanent or powerful? 2. “Getting defensive, feeling the sting, reacting before you mean to — none of that is cheating on the Absolute.” What’s your honest reaction to that sentence? 3. If “doing much” simply means beginning and continuing the strife of demonstrating your own wholeness — not arriving — what does that take off your shoulders?

4
~10–12 min

Where this gets practical

Introduce this reframe: the real question isn’t whether you feel the reaction — it’s whether the reaction gets the final word.

Discuss: 1. Think of a recent moment you reacted before you meant to — irritation, defensiveness, impatience. What happened right after? Did the reaction get the final word, or did something else step in? 2. Is there a version of your own story — a long season, a hard stretch, a slow journey — that you’ve been tempted to file under regret? What would it look like to file it under “doing much” instead?

5
~8–10 min

The experiment

Close with an invitation, not an assignment: this week, when a reaction shows up — sting, defensiveness, impatience — notice it without flinching away and without handing it the final word. Name that this is a practice, not a performance, and that noticing itself is the “doing much.”

“We are all ‘doing much.’ Please don’t let anyone tell you you’re not.”

Discuss: 1. Where do you expect this to show up for you this week? 2. What would it mean to let that moment simply be information, not a verdict on how spiritually advanced you are?

What you'll need

The materials

Wallet card You Are Not the Struggle It’s okay to struggle. Feeling it doesn’t mean you’re behind — believing it defines you is the only real risk. Open printable handout →
  • Paper and pens for each participant
  • A printed or projected copy of the four quotes (p. 84:3, p. 254:12, Unity of Good p. 5:3, and the closing line) for easy reference during Beats 2–3
  • A whiteboard, poster paper, or shared doc if you want to capture responses to Beat 4’s discussion questions as a group
  • The “You Are Not the Struggle” wallet card — printable, two per sheet, matches this lesson’s quotes and three questions exactly

If it's not going well

When this one gets heavy

If the conversation opens into real disclosure — addiction, recovery, grief, abuse, or a genuine safety concern — pause the lesson itself and follow the site’s mentor safety guidelines (For Mentors page). If someone becomes visibly emotional naming where they actually are on their own journey, that’s not a sign to move faster; slow down, and let them lead how much they share.

For the fuller tiered guidance — including what to do if someone discloses something serious — see For Mentors.

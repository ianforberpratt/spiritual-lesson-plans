Age 21+

Words That Still Open Doors

The language you inherited as a child doesn’t have to be the language you’re stuck with as an adult.

Time45–60 min
Group1:1 or group
AgeAge 21+
Formatin-person or virtual
UpdatedAugust 25, 2026
Also built for:Grade 6-8Teen

Assumptions & Caveats

Before you begin

What you should know

By adulthood, most people are carrying decades of religious or spiritual language — some of it from a childhood church or family, some picked up later, some deliberately set down along the way. Some people in the room may have already done real, hard work re-examining their inherited vocabulary; for others, this may be the first time they’ve said any of this out loud. Neither is more “done” than the other. This isn’t a lesson about reaching a conclusion about any particular word — it’s about giving people permission to keep examining language they may have assumed was fixed forever.

What we're assuming

  • People in the room bring genuinely different histories with religious language: lifelong members of a tradition, people who left one, people who returned to one, converts, people raised with no tradition at all.
  • Some words on the table today may carry real grief, relief, anger, or nostalgia — sometimes more than one at once.
  • No one owes the group a tidy resolution by the end of the session.

Learning goals

What they walk away with

  1. Names, across their own adult life, at least one word for the sacred that still opens a door and one that has become — or always was — a wall.
  2. Distinguishes, in their own words, between honoring where a word or teaching came from and treating the person or institution that gave it to them as beyond question.
  3. Practices translating an inherited word into language that reflects who they are now, without discarding what the original word was protecting.
  4. Leaves with explicit permission — regardless of where they land — to keep revising their spiritual vocabulary for the rest of their life, without treating that revision as instability or failure.

How to hold the room

Before you start talking

  • Let this go to real depth. Adults can sit with ambiguity and unresolved feeling longer than younger groups — don’t rush toward a tidy close.
  • Some disclosures may be substantial: leaving a church, estrangement from family over belief, loss of a faith community, or the relief of finding words that finally fit. Receive all of it as valid, not as something to problem-solve in the room.
  • Resist offering your own “better” word, even with good intentions. The goal is their vocabulary, not a shared one.
  • If the group is mixed — some lifelong practitioners, some who’ve left, some new to any of this — name that openly early on, so no one assumes their history is the “default” one in the room.

The lesson flow

Step by step

Each beat alternates a little input, a little doing, and a little reflecting. Skip, slow down, or stretch any of these to fit your group.

1
~10 min

Words That Land, Words That Don’t, Across a Lifetime

Think back across your life — childhood, a particular chapter, or now. Name a few words that have been used, by family, a church, a teacher, or a tradition, to talk about God or “something bigger.” For each: has it been a door, a wall, or has that changed over time?

2
~12 min

The Idea Isn’t the Words

Share: the words we inherit for the sacred were made by people, at some point, doing their best to point at something bigger than any word can hold. Words can outlive their fit even after decades of working just fine. What they were pointing at doesn’t age out. Discuss: Is there a word from your childhood tradition or upbringing that still feels alive to you as an adult? Is there one that carried you for years and then, at some point, stopped? What changed — the word, or you?

3
~15 min

Translate It

Pick one inherited word — master, worship, sin, holy, or another word specific to your own history — and try translating it into language that reflects who you are now, without losing what the original word was trying to protect. If it’s useful: this is, in miniature, what people mean by “deconstructing and reconstructing” a faith vocabulary — taking a word apart to find what it was really protecting, then rebuilding language that still protects it.

4
~10–15 min

You Get to Keep Translating

Discuss: What’s the difference, in your own experience, between respecting where a teaching or word came from and putting the person or institution who gave it to you beyond question? Has translating a word ever felt like a loss, even when it was also a relief?

5
~5–8 min

Closing

Close with: you don’t need the perfect words, and you never will — not at twenty-five, not at eighty. Whatever words you use, or don’t have yet, for the power greater than yourself that loves you, that’s still true, right now. You’re allowed to keep translating for the rest of your life.

What you'll need

The materials

  • Paper and pens, or a shared doc for virtual groups
  • Optional: index cards, one per person, for writing down a single word they’re choosing to keep, retire, or translate — something they can literally hold, and decide whether to keep or set down at the end
  • Optional printables: Handout “Door or Wall?”; Handout “Translate It”

If it's not going well

When this one gets heavy

At this age, what surfaces can be substantial and long-standing: grief over a lost faith community, anger at a tradition or institution, guilt about leaving or about staying, or real estrangement from family over belief. This is not a detour from the lesson — for many adults, this work has been years in the making and deserves room. Don’t rush to resolve it or reassure it away. If what surfaces is beyond what the room can hold, or someone appears to be in real distress, pause and follow the site’s mentor safety guidelines (For Mentors page).

For the fuller tiered guidance — including what to do if someone discloses something serious — see For Mentors.

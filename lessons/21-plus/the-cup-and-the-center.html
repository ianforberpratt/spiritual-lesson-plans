Both in One Hand: The Cup and the Center

How to hold the Absolute and the casserole at the same time, when a real person is hurting and you’re afraid.

Time50–65 min
Group1:1 or group
AgeAge 21+
Formatin-person or virtual
UpdatedNovember 17, 2026
Also built for:K-2Grade 3-5Grade 6-8Teen

Assumptions & Caveats

Before you begin

What you should know

Adults carry this tension quietly: they either know too much to bring a casserole (everything is “really” fine, so what’s there to do?) or they bring casseroles until they’re out of gas and resentful (everything depends on me). Both are fear in good clothes. This lesson doesn’t ask anyone to be fearless first. It says: name the fear, see the picture in thought as a picture, turn to the center, then choose a small cup.

It uses the three degrees (Science and Health, pp. 115–116) as the map for step two and the wheel (cup on the rim, center in the hub) as the picture. It is the practice-sized follow-up to Rescue or Faith?, and the degrees are taught in Seven Names, One Journey. The group can be mixed: some people will know the book well and some will never have heard of it. Passages are read in full so nobody needs prior study. Required prep: none beyond the printouts, a pitcher of water, and cups. The lesson does not ask anyone to take a side on any public issue. Cases are deliberately side-less.

What we're assuming

  • A mix of faith backgrounds, and maybe no faith. “God” and “Love” are used plainly. Nobody is asked to agree.
  • People in the room may be living the hardest version right now: a diagnosis, a caregiving season, a rift in a family, a grief. Nobody has to say. Every sharing beat has a private-write or pass option, and no names are used.
  • Some will arrive wanting a formula. This isn’t one. It’s a practice, in your own words, in your own prayer.
  • Don’t assume the person who looks like they have it together does.

Learning goals

What they walk away with

  1. Recognize their own default (the truth held alone, or the help given alone) and what fear is doing in it.
  2. Use the four steps on a real-feeling case, including the plain naming of fear as the first move.
  3. Write a one-sentence center in their own words that passes five tests.
  4. Leave with one real, small cup to offer this week, and one person to tell.

How to hold the room

Before you start talking

  • State the ground rules and mean them: think first; anyone may pass; no names. Anyone may sit, stand, stretch, or step out.
  • Hold your own part small. Model steps one and two tentatively (“here’s how it might look”), then step back.
  • Do one or two “How clear is it, 1 to 5?” check-ins, and say out loud what you’ll adjust. It models the humility the lesson is about.
  • If a group argues the issue in the case, go back to step one.
  • Let hard things surface without redirecting to something lighter. A diagnosis, a death, a rift: let it be said, then offer the practice.

The lesson flow

Step by step

Each beat alternates a little input, a little doing, and a little reflecting. Skip, slow down, or stretch any of these to fit your group.

1
~8 min

The casserole problem

A small cup of water waits in the middle. Say nothing about it. Welcome, ground rules, and then name the tension: “I keep finding two things that both matter. One is that God is All and every person is whole and loved, never broken. The other is a real person, in a real need, today. On some days I reach for the first and my hands forget the second. On others, the other way around.”

Private write, two minutes: Which is my default: the true thing without the hands, or the hands without the true thing? What does it cost? Then one or two voices, optional.

Read, slowly:

“Jesus said to her, ‘I am the resurrection and the life.’... Jesus wept.”

John 11:25, 35

“Both, in the same moment. He didn’t choose.”

2
~10 min

The cup

“I chose the cup because it’s small enough to do on Monday, and because Jesus and Mary Baker Eddy both name it.” Read:

“And if anyone gives even a cup of cold water to one of these little ones who is my disciple, truly I tell you, that person will certainly not lose their reward.”

Matthew 10:42

Notice three things: the act is small; it’s cold (refreshing, simple); it’s given in a spirit (“in my name,” even to “one of these little ones”), not for thanks, not to be right about how they should drink. Then:

“Give them a cup of cold water in Christ’s name, and never fear the consequences.”

Science and Health, Mary Baker Eddy, p. 570

The people in that passage aren’t specialists. They’re “simple seekers for Truth, weary wanderers, athirst in the desert.” And “never fear the consequences” tells us offering the cup can cost something. The instruction isn’t to be fearless first. It’s to give the cup, and let Love and not fear decide.

“Love is impartial and universal in its adaptation and bestowals. It is the open fount which cries, ‘Ho, every one that thirsteth, come ye to the waters.’”

Science and Health, Mary Baker Eddy, p. 13:2–4

”‘Every one’ supplies a test: would the same cup, with the same warmth, go to the person who’s hard to love?” In ordinary life a cup can be a message with no agenda, a meal left at a door, sitting with someone without fixing, a specific prayer they never learn of, an honest apology.

3
~8 min

A map of what appears in thought

Put up the three degrees and read them (use the printable). Say: the map describes what appears in thought, and the heading reads “mortal mind.” The person is on the line above (“God’s spiritual idea, individual, perfect, eternal”). Each degree calls for a different cup.

  • First degree (fear, hurt, hatred, pain): the cup is safety, a steady presence and voice. Arguing doesn’t help.
  • Second degree (honesty, compassion, hope, faith): the cup is encouragement, truth shared kindly.
  • Third degree (wisdom, understanding, love): the cup is clarity, the plain truth said simply.

“The center comes first. Then the cup.”

If the group hasn’t met the degrees, point to Seven Names, One Journey for more. Don’t re-teach here.

4
~14 min

The four steps on a case

Put up the four steps. Read the case once:

“Two people who’ve known each other for years and care about each other see a divisive public question differently. We are deliberately not saying what the question is. At a gathering, the subject comes up. Voices rise a little. Each feels the other has stopped listening, and each is sure, from the inside, that their view is a matter of principle. Both go home unsettled. You overheard it, and you love them both.”

You model step one, tentatively, with the book:

“Christian scientific practice begins with Christ’s keynote of harmony, ‘Be not afraid!’”

Science and Health, Mary Baker Eddy, p. 410:29–30

“Always begin your treatment by allaying the fear... The great fact that God lovingly governs all, never punishing aught but sin, is your standpoint, from which to advance and destroy the human fear of sickness.”

Science and Health, Mary Baker Eddy, p. 411:27–412:4

“She wrote that about treating another. Today we turn it first toward the fear in our own thought as we hear this case. What is the fear here, humanly? Naming it isn’t dwelling. It’s meeting. And notice her last sentence: ‘God lovingly governs all’ is the standpoint. That’s already the center.”

You model step two: “Which degree is showing up for the first person, for the second, and for me as I listen? What suggestion is at work? ‘They’re enemies.’ ‘I must win.’ ‘It won’t matter.’ I name it as a picture in thought, not as the truth about anyone.”

Groups of three or four do step three (about four minutes) and step four (about four minutes). Step three: What’s true here, in the one Mind, of both people? Say it in your own words. Is it true of both at once? Does it need an opponent? Step four: What’s one cup that fits? Who is it for, and would the same warmth go to both? Small, specific, possible. Reflection on paper, one line each: step one in my own words; what I noticed in thought; what I’m left questioning. Two or three voices, optional.

”...all have one Spirit, God, one intelligent source...”

Science and Health, Mary Baker Eddy, p. 276

Check-in: “How clear is the cup, 1 to 5?” Name what you’ll adjust.

5
~10 min

The center

Bring in the wheel: “The rim touches the road and moves. The hub does not move, and the whole wheel turns on it. A cup without a center is kindness that runs out. A center without a cup is correctness no one can feel.” Then:

“These clearer, higher views inspire the Godlike man to reach the absolute centre and circumference of his being.”

Science and Health, Mary Baker Eddy, p. 262:14–16

“The circumference is the edge, and includes everything inside the circle. The center and the whole are the same, seen from two sides. There is no part of a person the center doesn’t reach.”

“Neither death nor life... nor anything else in all creation, will be able to separate us from the love of God.”

Romans 8:38–39

The five tests for a center statement, on the board:

  1. It’s true in a hospital waiting room.
  2. It’s in the present tense.
  3. It’s not about anyone else and doesn’t require an opponent.
  4. It’s in plain words.
  5. It can stand up to the evening news.

Index card: Whatever is happening, what is true is... Write three fast, circle one, test it against the five. It’ll be partial. “Not yet” is a welcome answer. A borrowed sentence is a cold cup. Then trios, four minutes each: speaker reads the statement aloud, draws a circumstance card, reads it, and answers in their own words. Listener: “It held when...” and “It wobbled when...” Anyone may pass, trade, or pick another. Some will be close to home; handle them gently, and keep them brief and without names.

6
~6 min

My card, and the cup

Hand out the four-step card. One line each: the fear I’ll meet first; the degree I expect in my own thought; my center (revised from Beat 5); my cup this week, small, specific, something I’d offer anyone. Optional: one person I’ll tell. Pairs, with someone not yet spoken to: read the card, every line. The partner says one sentence beginning “I heard...” and one of encouragement. Then pour water and offer a cup to a neighbor by name.

7

Optional extension (~10 min) — Three seats in Luke 15

One story holds the whole practice. Three volunteers read three scenes from the handout. Mark one word that surprises you. The turn happens in thought, not the pigsty (“he came to himself”). The father’s center never moved; his cup was a run, a robe, a ring, a feast. The elder son never left and is outside a party in his own house, and the father goes out to him too. Trios, one seat each (younger son, father, elder brother): What does this one believe? What’s actually true? What would a cup from this seat look like? Then, on paper: which seat am I in today?

If it's not going well

When this one gets heavy

Adults may carry more shame than they show, and a case can land close to a real diagnosis, loss, or rift. If someone has a strong reaction, pause, ground the room, and check in with them one-on-one. Pushback (“this is too neat,” “some things can’t be met with a cup”) is welcome; you don’t need to win it. You can say: “Yes. And the practice asks for something smaller than agreement: the next honest, small thing.” If someone describes persistent hopelessness, a relationship that sounds unsafe, or anything beyond an ordinary hard season, stop facilitating a lesson, respond as a caring adult, and follow the site’s mentor safety guidelines (For Mentors page) and your own local reporting obligations. Point toward professional support alongside the spiritual conversation.

For the fuller tiered guidance — including what to do if someone discloses something serious — see For Mentors.

Age 21+

Suffer It to Be So, Now

The permission you’re waiting to earn was already given — in the water, before anything got fixed.

Time45-60 min
Group1:1 or group
AgeAge 21+
Formatin-person or virtual
UpdatedAugust 30, 2026
Also built for:K-2Grade 3-5Grade 6-8Teen

Assumptions & Caveats

Before you begin

What you should know

Most adults are carrying some version of a “suffer it to be so now” season without a name for it — a low-energy stretch, a body that isn’t doing what it used to, a diagnosis, a deadline, a hard conversation on repeat, a version of themselves this month they’re not thrilled to be. The usual assumption is that permission to rest, to feel loved, to feel whole, arrives after the fixing is finished. This lesson runs the pattern the other direction, using the baptism of Jesus (Matthew 3:13–17) as the anchor: belovedness is spoken before any of the ministry starts, in the water, in the not-yet. Keep any references to a current hard season general and non-clinical — “a long, low stretch,” “heavier than I’d like,” “foggy some mornings” — rather than naming a specific diagnosis or condition; the point is the pattern, not anyone’s particular circumstance. Nothing you don’t already have for prep beyond the printable.

What we're assuming

- Participants are likely in the middle of something real and unresolved right now — career, marriage, parenting, health, energy, mood — and may recognize themselves uncomfortably fast in this material. - This room holds every faith background and none. The framing is offered, not required, and isn’t asking anyone to sign onto a specific theology to get something real from it. - Naming “I’m waiting to earn my own rest” out loud can bring up grief, relief, or both at once — that’s the lesson working, not going off track.

Learning goals

What they walk away with

- Leaves with an accurate, felt sense of the difference between permission that has to be earned and permission that was already given. - Leaves able to name their own specific “once ___” sentence and where it likely came from. - Leaves with language — “suffer it to be so, now” — they can actually use, mid-moment, on a real current situation. - Leaves with a felt sense that this isn’t resignation or giving up on change — it’s not making the change a condition for today’s belovedness.

How to hold the room

Before you start talking

- This can surface real grief or relief fast — people don’t often examine this pattern in themselves until it’s named plainly. Let it land; don’t rush to the next beat. - Stay curious about the “once ___” belief underneath, not about fixing the actual situation someone names. You’re not there to solve their career, marriage, or health season — just to locate the belief running underneath how they’re relating to it. - If the conversation drifts toward debating a specific theology or denomination, gently bring it back: what would change in your own life this week if permission came first?

The lesson flow

Step by step

Each beat alternates a little input, a little doing, and a little reflecting. Skip, slow down, or stretch any of these to fit your group.

1
~10 min

Naming the “once ___” sentence honestly

Ask participants to think of — not share yet — a real, current “once ___, then I’ll let myself rest / feel loved / feel whole” sentence. A career milestone. A marriage repair. A parenting stretch. A body that isn’t cooperating the way it used to. A long, low, foggy season that hasn’t lifted yet.

Discuss the pattern generally: “What does that sentence actually sound like in your own head? Where do you think you picked it up?”

2
~12 min

The story, in full

Read Matthew 3:13–17 slowly, in full: John baptizing people in the Jordan as a public turning-around; Jesus arriving and asking for the same baptism; John balking — “I have need to be baptized of thee,” essentially, this should be the other way around, since baptism was for washing away what needed fixing, and by John’s own reckoning Jesus had nothing to fix. Jesus’s answer:

Suffer it to be so now: for thus it becometh us to fulfil all righteousness.

Matthew 3:15, KJV

“Permit it. Allow it. Not because anything needed fixing first.”

And lo a voice from heaven, saying, This is my beloved Son, in whom I am well pleased.

Matthew 3:16–17, KJV

“That’s spoken before any of the teaching, any of the healing, anything people would later remember. The belovedness isn’t a verdict on finished work. It shows up right there in the water, in the not-yet, in the muck and the mess.”

Discuss: “We tend to think permission has to be earned — that we get to rest, or feel loved, or feel whole, once the low days are behind us, once the hard thing resolves, once the fog clears. What’s actually being claimed here instead?”

3
~12 min

Spiritual baptism, the Christ, and Love in the actual texture of a life

Read together:

Baptism. Purification by Spirit; submergence in Spirit.

Science and Health, Mary Baker Eddy, p. 581

“Not a ritual to get through — a full immersion in a Spirit that’s already surrounding you, whether or not you feel finished with anything.”

Christ. The divine manifestation of God, which comes to the flesh to destroy incarnate error.

Science and Health, Mary Baker Eddy, p. 583

God is love.

1 John 4:8, KJV

“If God is Love itself, the Christ is that Love showing up in the actual, specific texture of a human life — including the low, heavy, foggy ones. Not a Love waiting on the other side of the fixing.”

I am the way, the truth, and the life: no man cometh unto the Father, but by me.

John 14:6, KJV

“The way, the truth, and the life belong to that same Christ — which means it isn’t far off, historical, or reserved for the finished and put-together. It meets a person exactly where they are, now.”

Discuss: “What would it cost you to actually believe that — and what might it free up?”

4
~14 min

Spiritually baptizing an actual moment

Introduce the practice plainly: you can spiritually baptize a moment — the deadline, the diagnosis, the hard conversation you keep replaying, the version of yourself you’re not thrilled to be this month. Say it out loud, the way Jesus did: “suffer it to be so, now.” Not forever. Not without hope of change. Just permitted, for today.

Invite participants to name (privately, or aloud if the group is comfortable) one real, current thing to try this on — career, marriage, parenting, or a long, low, foggy stretch that hasn’t lifted yet, described in general terms rather than clinical detail.

Discuss: “What’s the difference between permitting something to be so for today, and giving up on it ever changing?”

5
~7 min

The experiment

Close with an invitation, not an assignment: this week, pick one real moment and consciously say “suffer it to be so, now” over it — quietly, underneath it, trusting that the same Love that met Jesus in the Jordan never once waited for anyone to be finished before calling them beloved.

6

Closing

“Love was never waiting for the fixing to be finished before it showed up. It shows up in the water. It says ‘beloved’ before the ministry even starts.”

If it's not going well

When this one gets heavy

Silence is completely normal here — let it hold. Disagreement or theological pushback is welcome, not a problem — you’re not trying to land everyone in the same place. A strong emotional reaction — someone recognizing how long they’ve withheld rest or belovedness from themselves — means pausing, grounding the room, and checking in privately rather than in front of the group. A disclosure of real harm — a health crisis, a safety concern, or anything beyond an ordinary hard season — means stopping that thread, responding as a caring adult first, and moving to the site’s mentor safety guidelines and your own local mandatory-reporting obligations, plus a direct referral to appropriate support as needed. This lesson’s script does not extend into crisis response.

For the fuller tiered guidance — including what to do if someone discloses something serious — see For Mentors.

What Actually Lasts

The things that look the most solid — a career, a reputation, a bank account, being right — are not always the ones still standing in twenty years.

Time45-60 min
Group1:1 or group
AgeAge 21+
Formatin-person or virtual
UpdatedSeptember 13, 2026
Also built for:K-2Grade 3-5Grade 6-8Teen

Assumptions & Caveats

Before you begin

What you should know

Most adults have already built a fair amount of life on things that felt solid at the time and turned out not to be — a job, a relationship, a reputation, a sense of security tied to circumstances outside their control. This lesson isn’t asking anyone to distrust everything they’ve built; it’s asking a narrower, more honest question: of everything currently propping up your sense of who you are and whether you’re okay, how much of it can actually take weight over time, and how much of it is impressive right now and quietly already melting? Job’s line — “Canst thou by searching find out God?” — is offered as real permission, not a dodge: you don’t have to fully prove or pin down what’s substantial before you start living from it.

What we're assuming

  • Every adult in the room is currently building their sense of security and identity on something — career, achievement, a relationship, being right, appearance, a bank balance — usually more than one thing at once, and usually without having chosen it consciously.
  • The room holds real range in circumstance: some are financially secure, some are not; some are in stable relationships or careers, some are in real upheaval. Keep examples general and never ask anyone to disclose specifics about income, relationship status, or career standing.
  • This isn’t a lecture that ambition, security, or caring what others think are character flaws. The point is narrower: some of what currently feels most solid is genuinely useful and still won’t be what’s left standing later — and noticing that now is more useful than noticing it under real pressure.

Learning goals

What they walk away with

  1. Can honestly name what they’ve actually been building their sense of security or identity on lately, without it collapsing into self-judgment.
  2. Leaves knowing they don’t have to have it fully proven or figured out before living from what actually lasts — permission, not another performance standard.
  3. Carries a repeatable question into a real decision this week or month.
  4. Locates one real, current area of life — a career move, a relationship, a worry, a decision — to test it on.

How to hold the room

Before you start talking

  • This age can hold real vulnerability — let the discussion go where it goes, including into career, marriage, parenting, and civic or political life, which younger bands intentionally don’t touch.
  • Resist turning this into a referendum on anyone’s actual choices (a career path, a relationship, a financial decision). The question is about what a thing is actually made of, not whether the person was wrong to build there.
  • Offer an explicit way to pass on any activity or question — some adults in the room may be in the middle of exactly the crisis this lesson describes, and observing rather than participating is a complete way to be present.

The lesson flow

Step by step

Each beat alternates a little input, a little doing, and a little reflecting. Skip, slow down, or stretch any of these to fit your group.

1
~8 min

Naming what feels solid right now

Ask: “What’s one thing in your life right now that feels like the biggest deal in the world — the thing you’d protect first, or worry about losing most?” Let a few people share if willing; this can be career, health, a relationship, a reputation, a financial situation, a belief about yourself.

2
~10 min

Ground it: what is substance, actually

Read the question-and-answer form directly: “Question. — What is substance? Answer. — Substance is that which is eternal and incapable of discord and decay. Truth, Life, and Love are substance... Spirit, the synonym of Mind, Soul, or God, is the only real substance.” — Science and Health, Mary Baker Eddy, p. 468:16–24. Sit with it plainly: by this definition, most of what culture calls “solid” — money, status, physical strength, being right — doesn’t actually qualify, however real and useful it is in its place.

3
~12 min

The investment question, at adult stakes

Introduce the same two categories used at 14-21, now at full adult range: career, marriage, parenting, reputation, achievement, security, being right in an argument or a worldview — versus “the substance of a diligent man is precious” (Proverbs 12:27): honesty, understanding, love, integrity. Real discussion: where is each person actually building right now, and does that match where they’d say they’re building?

4
~10 min

Job’s permission

Introduce Job 11:7: “Canst thou by searching find out God? canst thou find out the Almighty unto perfection?” Frame it directly: this isn’t a rebuke, it’s relief — you were never required to fully prove, search out, or intellectually finish the case for what’s substantial before you’re allowed to live from it. Pair with Hebrews 11:1, “faith is the substance of things hoped for, the evidence of things not seen” — you find this kind of substance less by pinning it down and more by living inside it and watching what it does over time.

5
~5-10 min

Carry it

Teach the question to keep: “Is this the kind that’s impressive but melting, or the kind that’s quiet but never runs out?” Ask each person to name one real, current area of life to apply it to — silently is fine.

If it's not going well

When this one gets heavy

Silence from someone mid-crisis in exactly the area this lesson touches is a normal, appropriate response — don’t press for participation. Pushback or disagreement (someone defending that their career or reputation really is their substance) is worth holding open rather than correcting — this is genuinely a discernment question, not a settled verdict on any one person’s choices. A strong emotional reaction — grief over something built on that didn’t hold, real fear about the present — calls for a pause, a private check-in, and permission to step out. A disclosure of real crisis or harm should not be handled as group discussion; respond as a caring adult, point to the site’s mentor safety guidelines, and observe whatever your own setting’s professional or reporting obligations require.

For the fuller tiered guidance — including what to do if someone discloses something serious — see For Mentors.

A lesson · Soul: Another Name for God

Age 21+

Soul: Another Name for God

An 1875 passage about mastering hatred, lust, revenge, and deceit — decoded for adult life: office politics, a marriage, a career, a curated feed.

Time50–60 min
Group1:1, small group, or class
AgeAge 21+
Formatin-person or virtual
UpdatedAugust 25, 2026
Also built for:Grade 6-8Teen

Assumptions & Caveats

Before you begin

What you should know

Adults tend to assume they’ve mostly outgrown the propensities named in this passage — hatred, lust, revenge, deceit sound like adolescent problems, or at least like problems other people have. In practice, they just get quieter and better-dressed: contempt for a rival dressed up as professional disagreement, a marriage’s small dishonesties, a curated LinkedIn or Instagram self that edits out anything inconvenient, a grudge nursed for years instead of an hour. The passage’s claim is the same at every age: none of this is who a person actually is. Soul — the real, permanent source of identity — sits underneath all of it, untouched.

“Christian Science commands man to master the propensities, — to hold hatred in abeyance with kindness, to conquer lust with chastity, revenge with charity, and to overcome deceit with honesty. Choke these errors in their early stages, if you would not cherish an army of conspirators against health, happiness, and success.”

Science and Health, p. 405:5–11

“The good man finally can overcome his fear of sin. This is sin’s necessity, — to destroy itself. Immortal man demonstrates the government of God, good, in which is no power to sin.”

Science and Health, p. 405:18

What we're assuming

  • Adults are often more defended about this material than teenagers are — it’s easier to intellectualize “propensities” as a young person’s problem than to name a current, adult-scale version honestly. Expect some initial distance.
  • Naming workplace, marriage, or ambition-related material is illustrating a pattern, not diagnosing anyone’s actual situation or relationship. Keep it that way explicitly.
  • Some participants may be sitting with real, current guilt or grief connected to one of these four patterns. This lesson isn’t a confessional, and it isn’t couples or individual counseling — see “If it’s not going well” for where the line is.

Learning goals

What they walk away with

  1. A working answer to why Soul, not reputation or role, is the actual source of identity — tested against real adult material, not hypotheticals.
  2. The ability to name a current, honest, adult-scaled version of each of the four propensities.
  3. A concrete sense of what “holding it in abeyance” looks like in a real professional or relational moment, not just as a phrase.
  4. One real, current situation to practice answering from Soul instead of from reputation, ambition, or reflex.

How to hold the room

Before you start talking

  • Adults will often deflect toward the abstract (“well, generally speaking, people should be honest”) — gently bring it back to something specific and current without demanding disclosure.
  • Keep workplace and relationship examples at the level of illustration, not diagnosis of anyone present. If someone brings a real, specific situation, follow their lead rather than generalizing it into a lesson point.
  • This isn’t a morality lecture. If the room drifts toward “I’m just a bad person for feeling this,” redirect immediately — the passage’s whole claim is the opposite.

The lesson flow

Step by step

Each beat alternates a little input, a little doing, and a little reflecting. Skip, slow down, or stretch any of these to fit your group.

1
~10 min

The propensities, grown up

Read both citations slowly. Then ask: “These four — hatred, lust, revenge, deceit — sound almost adolescent. What do you think each one actually looks like at your age, in your actual life, right now?”

Let the group (or, in 1:1, the individual) generate real, current examples before you offer any. Only after that, name the adult-scaled versions directly if the room needs a nudge: contempt dressed as professional disagreement, mishandled attraction or infidelity, petty workplace retaliation or the silent treatment in a marriage, a curated professional or social self that edits out anything inconvenient.

2
~10 min

“Choke these errors in their early stages”

Discuss: “Think of a moment recently where one of these four showed up small — a flash of contempt, a petty thought about getting even, a small dishonesty. What would it have looked like to notice it early, before it grew?”

3
~15–20 min

One pattern, examined honestly

Choose one of the four (or let participants choose their own) and go deeper:

  • Hatred → kindness. Contempt for a rival, a political opponent, someone online. What does holding it “in abeyance” look like without pretending you agree with them?
  • Lust → chastity. Not just fidelity in a narrow sense — honesty about attraction, integrity in how you treat people you’re drawn to, including online.
  • Revenge → charity. The urge to get even — in a marriage, a friendship, a workplace. What’s the difference between accountability and revenge?
  • Deceit → honesty. The curated adult self — a resume, a feed, a version of your marriage or your finances you present that isn’t quite true. What’s the cost of maintaining it?

Discuss: “Where does this one show up for you, honestly? Not your worst version — a real, current, ordinary version.”

4
~10 min

The experiment

Close with an invitation, not an assignment: this week, notice one real moment when one of these four shows up — even something small, a flash of contempt in a meeting, a petty thought — and consciously reach for its opposite before reacting.

“Immortal man demonstrates the government of God, good, in which is no power to sin.”

Science and Health, p. 405:18

Discuss: “What’s one specific, current situation where you might get to try this?”

If it's not going well

When this one gets heavy

If the conversation surfaces real, current relationship harm, infidelity actively in progress, workplace misconduct, or anything resembling abuse, this isn’t a reflection exercise anymore — pause it, and follow the site’s mentor safety guidelines (For Mentors page) rather than trying to resolve it inside the lesson format. This is a spiritual reframe, not couples counseling, HR mediation, or crisis support, and it shouldn’t be stretched to do that work.

For the fuller tiered guidance — including what to do if someone discloses something serious — see For Mentors.

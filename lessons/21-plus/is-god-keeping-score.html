A lesson · Is God Keeping Score?

College Student & Adult (21+)

Is God Keeping Score?

Why the God you were handed might not be the God who’s actually there — and what changes the moment you switch pictures.

Time45–60 min
Group1:1 or group
AgeCollege Student & Adult (21+)
FormatIn-person or virtual
Also built for:5–8 · Early Elementary (K–2)8–11 · Upper Elementary (Grades 3–5)11–14 · Middle School (Grades 6–8)

Assumptions & Caveats

Before you begin

What you should know

Most people carry a picture of God they never chose — inherited from a childhood room, a hard sermon, a fearful relative, a culture. A common one: God as a ledger-keeper. Grace is real, but conditional. Love has to be earned through obedience, and every failure moves the tally the wrong direction. People raised this way rarely call it fear — they call it reverence. But underneath the reverence is often a quiet exhaustion: a hum of never quite enough that follows a person out of their place of worship and into their marriage, their work, their own reflection in the mirror.

Christian Science starts from a different premise: God’s very nature is Love, not judgment first and love second.

“God is Love. Can we ask Him to be more?”

Science and Health with Key to the Scriptures, Mary Baker Eddy, p. 2:23

“Man is God’s reflection, needing no cultivation, but ever beautiful and complete.”

Science and Health with Key to the Scriptures, Mary Baker Eddy, p. 527:4

“The Lord your God is with you, the Mighty Warrior who saves. He will take great delight in you; in his love he will no longer rebuke you, but will rejoice over you with singing.”

Zephaniah 3:17, NIV

If that’s true, there’s nothing left to earn. Not relief that a score has been settled — the realization there was never a scoreboard to begin with.

What we're assuming

  • Some participants currently hold a punishing-God picture, whether or not they’d name it that way — and some don’t carry any God-picture at all. Both are welcome here.
  • This lesson isn’t about correcting anyone’s tradition. It’s “here’s another way to picture this — notice how your body responds,” not “your church was wrong.”
  • Naming a ledger-God out loud can bring up grief, anger, or relief, sometimes all at once. That’s the lesson working, not the lesson going off track.

Learning goals

What they walk away with

  1. A working distinction between reverence that’s actually fear, and reverence rooted in love.
  2. A felt sense — not just an intellectual one — that grace was never a ledger with something left to earn back.
  3. Three questions they can actually reach for, mid-conflict, in real time.
  4. One real relationship or situation where they’ve tried answering from a loving God instead of their default reflex.

How to hold the room

Before you start talking

  • This touches nerve territory. Someone may realize mid-conversation that their whole life has been shaped by a punishing-God framework. Let that land — don’t rush past it to get to the next beat.
  • You’re not there to diagnose anyone’s current faith. Stay curious about the picture of God underneath the tension, not about whether their tradition is “right.”
  • The workplace, geopolitical, and relationship examples below are illustrations of a pattern, not verdicts on any specific conflict. If the group starts debating a specific news event, gently bring it back to the underlying question: which God are we operating from right now?

The lesson flow

Step by step

Each beat alternates a little input, a little doing, and a little reflecting. Skip, slow down, or stretch any of these to fit your group.

1
~10 min

Naming the ledger

Read or paraphrase this portrait aloud: someone deeply devoted to their faith — first to volunteer, last to leave, reads their sacred text before bed, prays before every meal. Raised to believe God was keeping score. They wouldn’t call it fear — they’d call it reverence. But underneath is a quiet exhaustion, a hum of never quite enough, that follows them into their marriage, their job, their own reflection in the mirror.

Discuss:

  1. What word would you use for what that person feels — reverence, fear, exhaustion, all three at once?
  2. Where did your own picture of God come from — a person, a room, a specific moment?
  3. Have you ever caught yourself performing for God rather than resting in God? What did that look like?
2
~10–15 min

A differently-sized God

Introduce the reframe: not a smaller, nicer version of the ledger-God, but an infinitely larger idea — Love itself, as our very nature, not a reward for good behavior. Read the three quotes from “What you should know” together, slowly.

Discuss:

  1. What’s the actual difference between a God who loves you when you behave, and a God whose nature is Love?
  2. “Rejoice over you with singing” — not tolerate, not grade. Sit with that image for a moment. What does it change?
  3. If nothing is left to earn, what would you stop performing?
3
~15–20 min

Where this gets practical

How we picture God quietly shapes how we handle the hard, human stuff. Walk through one or two of these — pick what fits your group:

  • A disagreement at work. Under a ledger-God, being wrong feels like being bad, so a person defends their position past the point of reason. Under a loving God, identity was never on the table — a person can say “I might be missing something” and hold their values firmly without needing the other person to lose.
  • Two countries, one piece of land. When security and worth feel like they depend on possession, scarcity becomes sacred and “God is on our side” becomes a reason for the other side’s erasure. A Love that is impartial and universal opens a crack of room for a different question underneath the negotiation: does our safety actually require another’s loss?
  • A couple who see something differently. Under a ledger-God, a partner’s differing view can feel like a threat that must be corrected. Under a loving God, two whole people can see something differently and remain fully loved and fully loveable, both of them, at the same time. Curiosity replaces correction.

The three questions (write them where everyone can see):

  1. Am I trying to win this, or trying to understand it?
  2. Does their difference threaten who I am — or can we both stay fully whole even if we still disagree?
  3. Does my security actually require their loss?

Discuss:

  1. Which of the three questions is hardest for you to sit with honestly, right now?
  2. Think of a real tension in your life. Answer all three questions truthfully — not impressively, just honestly. What did you notice?
4
~10 min

The experiment

Close with an invitation, not a homework assignment: this week, pick one real conversation and consciously answer the three questions from the loving-God side instead of your default. Name that it might cost something — ground, safety, being right.

“Divine Love always has met and always will meet every human need.”

Science and Health with Key to the Scriptures, Mary Baker Eddy, p. 494:10

Discuss:

  1. What would it cost you to answer from the loving-God side just once this week?
  2. What might it be worth?

What you'll need

The materials

  • Paper and pens for each participant
  • A whiteboard, poster paper, or shared doc to write the three questions where everyone can see them for Beat 3
  • The “Three Questions” wallet card — printable, two per sheet, cut apart and laminate if you like. One for a wallet, one for a mirror.

If it's not going well

When this one gets heavy

If the conversation drifts into disclosure of abuse, self-harm, or a real safety concern, pause the lesson itself and follow the site’s mentor safety guidelines (For Mentors page). If someone becomes visibly distressed naming their own punishing-God history, that’s not a sign to move faster; slow down, and let them lead how much they share.

For the fuller tiered guidance — including what to do if someone discloses something serious — see For Mentors.

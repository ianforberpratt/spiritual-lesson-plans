Age 21+

Big Ego, Little Ego

The version of you that has to win the meeting, win the argument, or win the comparison isn’t actually the one in charge.

Time"45-60 min"
Group"1:1 or group"
AgeAge 21+
Format"in-person or virtual"
UpdatedAugust 25, 2026
Also built for:Grade 3-5Grade 6-8Teen

Assumptions & Caveats

Before you begin

What you should know

In adults, the little ego shows up less as insecurity and more as defensiveness, control, needing credit, competitiveness with peers, or a quiet flash of contempt when someone else succeeds. Because adults are more practiced at hiding it, this version asks for more honesty, not more abstraction — workplace, marriage, parenting, and civic-life material are all fair game here, and they’re where this lesson actually does its work.

What we’re assuming

- Participants may currently be actively defending something — a professional reputation, a marriage disagreement, a parenting choice — and might recognize themselves uncomfortably fast. - Not everyone in the room shares a faith background or belief in a personal God. The pattern (identity as reflection vs. identity as self-made) can be tested against real experience either way. - This isn’t a lesson about anyone’s specific conflict being wrong. It’s about which “I” is running the response.

What we're assuming

Learning goals

What they walk away with

1. Notice the little ego’s specific voice in a real, recent moment — a meeting, an argument, a comparison with a peer or sibling. 2. Feel the real, bodily difference between defending an identity and resting in one already whole. 3. Carry three questions into a real conflict this week. 4. Locate one current relationship — a colleague, a spouse, a child, a rival — where this applies.

How to hold the room

Before you start talking

- This can get genuinely uncomfortable fast for adults — people don’t love recognizing their own ego in real time, including you as the facilitator. - Stay curious, not diagnostic. You’re not there to catch anyone, including yourself. - If the group drifts into debating a specific real conflict rather than the underlying pattern, gently bring it back: which “I” is answering right now?

The lesson flow

Step by step

Each beat alternates a little input, a little doing, and a little reflecting. Skip, slow down, or stretch any of these to fit your group.

1
~10 min

Naming the little ego honestly

Ask participants to think of — not share yet — one recent moment they felt defensive, needed to be right, needed credit, or felt a flash of comparison or envy at someone else’s success.

Discuss the pattern generally first: what does that voice actually sound like? (“I deserve credit for this.” “If they’re right, I lose.” “Why do they get to have that?”)

2
~12 min

The two Egos, adult depth

Read slowly:

“I, or EGO. Divine Principle; Spirit; Soul; incorporeal, unerring, immortal, and eternal Mind. There is but one I, or Us, but one divine Principle, or Mind, governing all existence; man and woman unchanged forever in their individual characters, even as numbers which never blend with each other, though they are governed by one Principle. All the objects of God’s creation reflect one Mind...”

Science and Health with Key to the Scriptures, Mary Baker Eddy, p. 588:9–19

“The material body, which you call me, is mortal mind, and this mind is material in sensation.”

Science and Health with Key to the Scriptures, Mary Baker Eddy, p. 416:16

“And God said unto Moses, I AM THAT I AM.”

Exodus 3:14

Unpack: “I, or EGO” is one of Mary Baker Eddy’s own names for God — the one real “I,” divine Principle and Mind. Contrast that with the “so-called physical ego” — mortal mind, material in sensation, borrowed.

Discuss: “What’s the actual, practical difference between an identity that has to be defended and one that doesn’t?”

3
~18 min

Where this gets practical

Pick one or two scenarios to fit the group:

- A meeting where credit gets contested. Little ego needs the room to know whose idea it was; true identity was never up for a vote. - A disagreement with a spouse or partner. Little ego needs to be right; two whole people can see something differently and remain fully loved and fully themselves, both at once. - A child’s choice or rebellion that stings more than it should. Little ego experiences a child’s differentiation as a referendum on the parent; true identity isn’t threatened by someone else becoming fully themselves. - A colleague’s or friend’s success that produces an unwanted flash of comparison. Little ego treats another person’s light as competition for its own; true identity, like a mirror, isn’t dimmed by a second mirror catching the same sun.

The three questions (write where everyone can see): 1. Am I trying to win this, or trying to understand it? 2. Would I still feel secure here if I got no credit and no one ever knew? 3. Does their success, or their difference, actually cost me anything real?

Discuss: which question is hardest to sit with honestly right now?

4
~10 min

The experiment

Close with an invitation, not an assignment: pick one real relationship or recurring conflict and try answering from the true-Ego side once this week — deliberately not defending, not needing credit, not needing to win. Name that it may cost something — being right, credit, control — and that’s expected, not a sign it isn’t working.

Closing

“You were never actually required to keep proving you’re real. That job was never yours to begin with.”

If it's not going well

When this one gets heavy

Silence is fine, let it sit. Real defensiveness surfacing about the exercise itself is fine, even expected — that’s the ego responding right on schedule, not a failure of the room. If someone becomes visibly distressed recognizing a painful pattern — a marriage, a parenting regret — slow down, don’t press, offer a private word after. If the conversation opens into disclosure of real harm, stop facilitating, respond as a caring adult, and point to the site’s mentor safety guidelines and each facilitator’s own local mandatory-reporting obligations.

For the fuller tiered guidance — including what to do if someone discloses something serious — see For Mentors.

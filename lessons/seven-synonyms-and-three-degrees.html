A lesson

Seven Names, One Journey

Two of Christian Science's most foundational ideas — unpacked for anyone, no faith background required.

Time50–65 min
GroupIndividual, 1:1, or group
AgeTeen & up (14+)
FormatIn-person, virtual, or self-guided

Before you begin

What you should know

Two ideas anchor this lesson, and both are genuinely new territory for most people meeting Christian Science for the first time.

The first: Christian Science doesn't use one word for God. It uses seven — Mind, Spirit, Soul, Principle, Life, Truth, Love.

“God is incorporeal, divine, supreme, infinite Mind, Spirit, Soul, Principle, Life, Truth, Love.”

Mary Baker Eddy, Science and Health with Key to the Scriptures, p. 465.

Not seven gods, and not seven attributes tacked onto one God the way a person might have many qualities. Each name is a complete, full way of understanding the same one God — the way a single person might be fully described as a parent, a friend, and a professional, without being three different people. Someone new to this idea often asks: why seven? isn't one name enough? The honest answer is that no single word — not even “God” — carries the full weight of what's being pointed at. Seven names offer seven doors into the same room.

The second idea: Christian Science describes the human journey toward understanding God — and toward healing — as moving through three degrees. Stripped of the 1875 vocabulary, in plain terms: the first degree is what it feels like to be overwhelmed, underwater, tempted, out of control. The second degree is beginning to see the light — more at peace with yourself. The third degree is that smile of contentment. Mary Baker Eddy, the Discoverer and Founder of Christian Science, offered this as one scientific, practical way of understanding the journey toward healing and harmony — not the only way to see it, but a helpful one. Nobody lives permanently in one degree. Most people move through all three in a single day.

What we're assuming

The starting point

Nobody in the room needs any prior exposure to Christian Science, the Bible, or any faith tradition — both ideas should make sense on their own terms. Some participants will arrive with a very different picture of God already in place — one name, one gender, one temperament — and that's not a problem to correct, it's the starting point this lesson gently expands from. “Three degrees” can sound like a ranking of who's a better or worse person; it isn't — nobody is being graded, this is a map of a shared, ordinary, human experience. And these are just two ideas among many in Christian Science — nobody's being asked to adopt a whole belief system, just to sit with two ways of seeing that might be useful.

Learning goals

What a participant walks away with

  • Leaves with a working answer to “why seven names for God instead of one?” — not a rule to accept, just a lens that makes sense.
  • Leaves able to describe, in their own words, what the physical, moral, and spiritual degrees actually feel like — no 1875 vocabulary required.
  • Leaves with a sense of how the seven names cluster into three natural centers — being (Mind, Spirit, Soul), active (Life, Truth, Love), and governing (Principle, Love) — not just a flat list.
  • Leaves having named one real, current moment from their own week, honestly placed somewhere on the three-degree journey, without judgment.

How to hold the room

Before you start talking

  • Lead with curiosity, not correction. This isn't “here's what you got wrong about God” — it's “here's another way of seeing this, notice what lands.”
  • If someone pushes back — “isn't that polytheism,” “isn't that just words” — that's a great question, not a threat. Sit with it together rather than defending the framework.
  • Keep the three degrees at the level of pattern and shared human experience, not confession. If someone volunteers something personal and specific, follow their lead; don't chase it.
  • This is explicitly one way of seeing it, not a rule. Say that out loud if the room needs to hear it — some people relax the moment they know they're not being asked to sign onto anything.

The lesson flow

Five beats, about 50–65 minutes total

Each beat alternates a little input, a little doing, and a little reflecting. Skip, slow down, or stretch any of these to fit your group.

1
~10 min

One God, Seven Doors

Before naming any of the seven, ask the group: if you had to describe someone you love completely — not just one thing about them, but everything — could you do it in one word? Let that sit for a moment, then introduce the seven names and read this aloud: “God is incorporeal, divine, supreme, infinite Mind, Spirit, Soul, Principle, Life, Truth, Love” (Science and Health, p. 465).

  • Being center — Mind, Spirit, Soul
  • Active center — Life, Truth, Love
  • Governing center — Principle, Love

Discuss: Which of the seven names is most familiar to you already, and which is the most surprising to see on this list — and if someone said “just call it God and leave it at that,” what do you think would get lost?

2
~10–15 min

How the Seven Relate

Point out the three natural centers in the diagram above: Mind, Spirit, Soul (who this is — consciousness, substance, identity), Life, Truth, Love (what it does for you — meets fear, lies, and loneliness), and Principle, Love (firm and kind at once). Note that Love sits at the intersection — present in two centers, echoed at the top of the circle.

Discuss: Pick one of the three centers — where do you already see that quality show up in your own life, even outside anything religious — and what changes about “Principle” once it's paired with Love?

3
~15 min

The Honest Seeker's Journey

Read the plain-language version first — overwhelmed, underwater, tempted, out of control; beginning to see the light; that smile of contentment — before the 1875 vocabulary below. Notice: this is a journey, left to right, not a ranking of good people versus bad people.

“First Degree: Depravity. Physical. Evil beliefs, passions and appetites, fear, depraved will, self-justification, pride, envy, deceit, hatred, revenge, sin, sickness, disease, death. Second Degree: Evil beliefs disappearing. Moral. Humanity, honesty, affection, compassion, hope, faith, meekness, temperance. Third Degree: Understanding. Spiritual. Wisdom, purity, spiritual understanding, spiritual power, love, health, holiness.”

Mary Baker Eddy, Science and Health with Key to the Scriptures, p. 115.

Discuss: Without naming anything private, what would a first-degree moment feel like in an ordinary week — and has anyone felt the shift from effort (second degree) to it just being true (third degree), even briefly?

4
~10–15 min

Where the Two Ideas Meet

Bring both ideas together: the same seven names are what a person leans into at every point along the three-degree journey — not a different name assigned to each degree, but all seven, practiced, at every waypoint. Reaching toward the third degree, a little each day, is how someone comes to understand a real, lived relationship with God — and how they come to understand, more and more, that God shows up as all seven names, not just one.

Discuss: Think of a real moment this week that felt like the first or second degree — which one or two of the seven names might have helped, if you'd reached for it in the moment — and is there any shame in mostly living in the first or second degree? (There isn't.)

5
~10 min

The Experiment

Close with an invitation, not an assignment: this week, notice one ordinary first- or second-degree moment as it's happening, and consciously reach for one of the seven names instead of the usual reflex. Name that this takes practice, not perfection — “the last shall be first, and the first last” is itself a promise that the journey reorders, not a demand to arrive today.

“In the third degree mortal mind disappears, and man as God's image appears. Science so reverses the evidence before the corporeal human senses, as to make this Scriptural testimony true in our hearts, ‘The last shall be first, and the first last,’ so that God and His idea may be to us what divinity really is and must of necessity be, — all-inclusive.”

Mary Baker Eddy, Science and Health with Key to the Scriptures, p. 115.

Discuss: Which of the seven names do you want to practice reaching for first this week, and what do you think you'd notice change if you did?

If it's not going well

When this one gets heavy

Read this one before you lead the lesson, not during it: if the conversation opens into real disclosure — grief, a diagnosis, a loss, a genuine safety concern — pause the lesson itself; this isn't a discussion-prompt moment anymore. If someone gets quiet or visibly moved naming where they actually are on the three-degree journey, that's not a sign to move faster; slow down, and let them lead how much they share.

For the fuller tiered guidance — including what to do if someone discloses something serious — see For Mentors.

A lesson

Is Regret a Fair and Just Teacher?

A practical path from a hard decision to moving forward — without pretending it didn't happen.

Time45–60 min
Group1:1 or group
AgeHigh school (or equiv.)
FormatIn-person or virtual

Before you begin

What you should know

Nothing required. If it helps to know where this came from: there's an old story about a woman brought before a crowd ready to punish her for a mistake. Rather than argue she was innocent, the response was simply: no one here is without fault, so who's left to condemn her? Then, directly to her: you're not condemned — go, and don't do it again. Not "it didn't happen." Not "it doesn't matter." Just: the weight of it doesn't get to crush you forever. This lesson borrows that shape.

What we're assuming

The starting point

Everyone in the room has at least one decision — something they did, or didn't do — that they still carry. We're not assuming anyone will describe theirs. We're assuming regret is already familiar to them, and that it sometimes feels less like a teacher and more like a sentence.

Learning goals

What a young person walks away with

  • Leaves knowing the difference between facing a mistake honestly and being sentenced to relive it forever — those aren't the same thing.
  • Leaves with a real, usable process for moving through a hard decision: see it clearly, set the shame down, name the fear, choose, adjust if needed, and give thanks for what it taught.
  • Leaves understanding that a course correction isn't a failure — it's what growing actually looks like.
  • Leaves feeling like they're allowed to stop punishing themselves for something, even if no one ever told them they could.

How to hold the room

Before you start talking

  • Nobody has to share what their regret actually is. The process works whether it stays private or not.
  • Don't rush to reassure ("it's fine, don't worry about it") — that can accidentally minimize something real. Let the weight be real, and still show them a way through it.
  • Watch for shame spirals. If someone seems to be sinking rather than processing, slow way down or pause — see the safety note below.

The lesson flow

Four beats, about 45–60 minutes total

Each beat alternates a little input, a little doing, and a little reflecting. Skip, slow down, or stretch any of these to fit your group.

1
~10 min

Naming the Decision, Without Shame

Think of a decision — something done, or not done — that you still carry some regret about. You don't have to share it. Just look at it clearly, once, without judging yourself for having it. Seeing a mistake honestly isn't the same as being sentenced by it.

2
~12–15 min

The Clean Slate

Once you've named it, set the shame down — on purpose. That doesn't mean forgetting it happened. It means you stop re-trying yourself for it every day. Alongside that, notice any fear driving the story you tell about it ("I'll always be like this," "I ruined everything") — and ask whether that fear is actually true, or just loud.

Discuss: What's the difference between remembering something and still being punished by it?

3
~12–15 min

Choosing, and Being Willing to Adjust

At some point you stop circling and choose what happens next — even if it's small. And if that choice needs a course correction later, that's not a failure. Adjusting is what a student does. Punishing yourself is what happens when you think you're not allowed to be a student anymore.

Discuss: Think of a time you changed course after a decision. Did it feel like failing, or like learning? What made the difference?

4
~10 min

Gratitude, Even for the Rocky Parts

Close by naming, quietly or out loud, one thing that hard decision actually taught you — even if the lesson was expensive. You don't have to be grateful it happened. You're allowed to just be grateful for what you know now that you didn't before.

Close with this: you are not what you regret. You're allowed to learn from it and put it down — both, not just one.

What you'll need

The materials

  • Paper and pens — or a shared doc / whiteboard if you're meeting virtually
  • Nothing else. No printouts, no purchases.

If it's not going well

When this one gets heavy

Read this one before you lead the lesson, not during it: regret conversations can surface real shame spirals, not just reflection. If someone says or implies they don't deserve to be forgiven, that they're a bad person, or anything that sounds like hopelessness about themselves, don't treat it as just part of the activity — take it seriously and slow all the way down.

For the fuller tiered guidance — including what to do if someone discloses something serious — see For Mentors.

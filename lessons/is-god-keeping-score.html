A lesson

Is God Keeping Score?

Why the God you were handed might not be the God who's actually there.

Time45–60 min
Group1:1 or group
AgeHigh school (or equiv.)
FormatIn-person or virtual

Before you begin

What you should know

Most people carry a picture of God they never chose — inherited from a childhood room, a hard sermon, a fearful relative, a culture. A common one: God as a ledger-keeper, where grace is conditional and every failure moves the tally the wrong direction. People raised this way rarely call it fear — they call it reverence. Underneath is often a quiet exhaustion: a hum of never quite enough that follows a person into their marriage, their work, their own reflection in the mirror.

Christian Science starts from a different premise: God's very nature is Love, not judgment first and love second.

“God is Love. Can we ask Him to be more?”

Mary Baker Eddy, Science and Health with Key to the Scriptures, p. 2:23.

“Man is God's reflection, needing no cultivation, but ever beautiful and complete.”

Mary Baker Eddy, Science and Health with Key to the Scriptures, p. 527:4.

“The Lord your God is with you, the Mighty Warrior who saves. He will take great delight in you; in his love he will no longer rebuke you, but will rejoice over you with singing.”

Zephaniah 3:17 (NIV).

If that's true, there's nothing left to earn. Not relief that a score has been settled — the realization there was never a scoreboard to begin with.

What we're assuming

The starting point

Some people in the room carry a punishing-God picture without ever naming it that way — others carry no God-picture at all. Both are welcome here. This isn't about correcting anyone's tradition; it's “here's another way to picture this, notice how your body responds,” not “your church was wrong.” If naming a ledger-God out loud brings up grief, anger, or relief, sometimes all at once — that's the lesson working, not going off track.

Learning goals

What a young person walks away with

  • Leaves with a working distinction between reverence that's actually fear, and reverence rooted in love.
  • Leaves with a felt sense — not just an intellectual one — that grace was never a ledger with something left to earn back.
  • Leaves with three questions they can actually reach for — whether they're watching the news, thinking about adults in their life, or in the middle of their own conflict.
  • Leaves having named one real relationship or situation, their own size, where they've tried answering from a loving God instead of their default reflex.

How to hold the room

Before you start talking

  • This touches nerve territory. Someone may realize mid-conversation that their whole life has been shaped by a punishing-God framework. Let that land — don't rush past it to get to the next beat.
  • You're not there to diagnose anyone's current faith. Stay curious about the picture of God underneath the tension, not about whether their tradition is “right.”
  • The world-news, adult, and friend-group examples below are illustrations of a pattern, not verdicts on any specific conflict. If the group starts debating a specific news event, gently bring it back to the underlying question: which God are we operating from right now?

The lesson flow

Four beats, about 45–60 minutes total

Each beat alternates a little input, a little doing, and a little reflecting. Skip, slow down, or stretch any of these to fit your group.

1
~10 min

Naming the Ledger

Read or paraphrase this portrait aloud: someone deeply devoted to their faith — first to volunteer, last to leave, reads their sacred text before bed, prays before every meal. Raised to believe God was keeping score. They wouldn't call it fear — they'd call it reverence. But underneath is a quiet exhaustion, a hum of never quite enough, that follows them into their marriage, their job, their own reflection in the mirror.

Discuss: Where did your own picture of God come from — and have you ever caught yourself performing for God rather than resting in God?

2
~10–15 min

A Differently-Sized God

Introduce the reframe: not a smaller, nicer version of the ledger-God, but an infinitely larger idea — Love itself, as our very nature, not a reward for good behavior. Read the three quotes from “What you should know” together, slowly. Sit with “rejoice over you with singing” for a moment — not tolerate, not grade.

Discuss: If nothing is left to earn, what would you stop performing?

3
~15–20 min

Zooming Out, Zooming In

How we picture God quietly shapes how we handle conflict — at every size, from the news down to a group chat. Walk through these three, in order, shrinking the scale each time:

  • The world stage. Two countries, one piece of land. When a nation's security and worth feel like they depend on possession, scarcity becomes sacred, and “God is on our side” becomes a reason for the other side's erasure. A Love that is impartial and universal opens a crack of room for a different question underneath the negotiation: does either side's safety actually require the other's loss?
  • An adult in your life. Think of two adults you know who see the same thing differently — two parents, a parent and a teacher, two people at church. Under a ledger-God, being right can feel like the whole point, so nobody backs down. Under a loving God, they could disagree and both still be whole, still be loved — neither one has to lose for the other to be right.
  • Your own version. Now shrink it all the way down: a friend who took someone else's side, a group chat that turned on a person, a fight with a sibling that felt bigger than it was. Same pattern, same size as your actual life.

The three questions

The same three questions work at every size above. Write them where everyone can see:

  • Am I trying to win this, or trying to understand it?
  • Does their difference threaten who I am — or can we both stay fully whole even if we still disagree?
  • Does my security actually require their loss?

Discuss: Pick one size — the world stage, an adult you know, or your own friend group — and actually answer all three questions about it, honestly. What changed, or didn't, from size to size?

4
~10 min

The Experiment

Close with an invitation, not a homework assignment: this week, pick one real conversation and consciously answer the three questions from the loving-God side instead of your default. Name that it might cost something — ground, safety, being right.

“Divine Love always has met and always will meet every human need.”

Mary Baker Eddy, Science and Health with Key to the Scriptures, p. 494:10.

Discuss: What would it cost you to answer from the loving-God side just once this week — and what might it be worth?

If it's not going well

When this one gets heavy

Read this one before you lead the lesson, not during it: if the conversation drifts into disclosure of abuse, self-harm, or a real safety concern, pause the lesson itself — this isn't a discussion-prompt moment anymore. If someone becomes visibly distressed naming their own punishing-God history, that's not a sign to move faster; slow down, and let them lead how much they share.

For the fuller tiered guidance — including what to do if someone discloses something serious — see For Mentors.

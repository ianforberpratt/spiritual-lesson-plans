Part 2 of 3 in Honest Questions CS, a short series on the questions beneath the surface, the ones that rarely get asked out loud. I’m not the only one asking them. I love Christian Science, and joining in is what I’ve been led to do. New here? Start with the preview and Part 1 above.

I was upset, and I wasn’t sure I was allowed to be.

I’d walked into a beautiful church on a road trip and found a few people in it. When I said so out loud, I was told there was nothing to be aghast at. The essence of Christian Science doesn’t need protecting. So what are we quietly protecting?

Let me be clear about what I’m not doing. The Manual says Christian Scientists “shall not report for publication the number of the members of The Mother Church, nor that of the branch churches” (Manual of The Mother Church, p. 48), and I’m keeping that. I’m not counting anyone, and I’m not asking anyone to. I’m describing what I can see with my own eyes: empty pews, and a handful of people in large, beautiful buildings.

I was on a road trip and wanted to go to church on a Sunday morning, so I looked in the Journal and found one church. In a large, beautiful room there were a few people. The music came from a recording because there was no pianist. The readers came up and read with great formality. I felt tender toward every one of them. They had kept the doors open.

When I got to my destination, I mentioned it to some fellow Christian Scientists. “We don’t count numbers,” I was told. “There’s no reason to stand aghast at nothingness.”

It upset me because what I saw is true. The kids in my Sunday school class tell me their last Sunday school had one person in it. That conversation showed me the shape of the silence: unspoken rules. Don’t sound like you doubt. Don’t talk about the empty Sunday schools.

I don’t think these rules are wrong at the root. They come from love and loyalty, and from not wanting to measure Truth by matter. But if the essence doesn’t need protecting, what do the rules protect?

A fellow Christian Scientist might say that trust is enough, and that it will resurge. I agree with the heart of it: Truth isn’t measured by a headcount, and prayer and faithfulness matter even when no one is watching. I teach a Sunday school class, and I’ve always said that if no one came, I would still sit there, pray for it, and cherish it.

But I would also be sad. Not because the truth needs an audience, but because a young person would have lost a safe place to think about God. So here’s my adjustment: trust and invitation aren’t opposites. If I’m hosting a dinner and nobody comes, I can trust the table is good and still ask whether I sent the invitations. Let’s pray. Let’s be there. And shouldn’t we also be sad, and go out and find people and give them the chance to see for themselves? That’s my highest sense of what’s right.

The silence costs real people. A teenager loses a safe place to think about God. People I love, new to this country and hungry to dive in, can’t find words they can take in.

This isn’t a departure from our roots. Mary Baker Eddy thought in messaging. She founded the Sentinel. She founded The Christian Science Monitor, a daily newspaper meant for everyone, whose object is “to injure no man, but to bless all mankind.” And her Manual says each church shall have a Reading Room, well located: a public place where anyone can walk in and read. She didn’t wait for the world to find the table. She set it where people already were.

The Manual records why the Church was founded. On April 12, 1879, on Mrs. Eddy’s motion, it was voted “to organize a church designed to commemorate the word and works of our Master, which should reinstate primitive Christianity and its lost element of healing” (Manual of The Mother Church, p. 17). Two halves: the word, what he taught about how to live and love, and the works, the healing. If healing is all we lead with, someone looking for the first half may never find it.

The disciples had fished all night and caught nothing. “Cast the net on the right side of the ship, and ye shall find” (John 21:6). Same sea. Same fish. A different cast. Not to catch anyone. To say there’s room.

The heart of this was never the pews. This Science is for everyone.

So what are we quietly protecting? Not the heart of it. I think it’s a memory. In Part 3, I’ll name it, and offer a few plain principles for saying this well.

Two claims to test

I promised each installment would give you claims to test. Here are two, one objection from inside our community, one from outside:

1. “Naming an empty Sunday school means doubting Christian Science, or breaking the Manual.” The next time you notice an empty room, say what you see (not a count), kindly, then pray. Notice whether your trust shrinks or grows.

2. “Christian Science is fading because it doesn’t want newcomers.” When has the door opened easily for someone? When has it stuck?

Honest questions, asked in love: what’s one thing you wish we could say out loud about our empty rooms? If you’ve stepped away, or never came in, I’d especially love to hear from you.

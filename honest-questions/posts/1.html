Here’s the question I’ve been sitting with: when someone is in pain, can we hold to what we know is true and still meet them exactly where they are?

My hypothesis is yes, and that meeting them there isn’t a compromise of the absolute. It’s how the truth gets in.

If you’re already thinking, “But doesn’t acknowledging pain give it power?” good. Hold onto that. It’s the right question, and we’ll face it head-on in a moment. First, the night that made me ask.

Late one night recently, a teenager called me, with a parent beside them, asking for prayerful support. They were in the grip of what felt like an anxiety attack. We started with hymns. We started with calming the fear.

I found myself wondering what would have happened if I’d said, “This isn’t true about you. None of this is happening.” In the absolute (what Christian Science holds to be the ultimate truth about us), that’s clear. But what I wanted them to hear first was: I know this is hard. You’re not alone. This is not a prison. Let’s look at it a different way. And then I’d want to add: You are so loved, and I’d like us to talk about what’s true, because the fear is often about something bigger than what’s in front of us.

Now the question. A fellow Christian Scientist might say that acknowledging the pain doesn’t help, and can compromise the healing. I hear that often, and I agree with the heart of it: dwelling on a problem, or agreeing that it has power, can make it feel more real. So here is my adjustment. I’m not agreeing that the pain is real or that it has the last word. I’m acknowledging the person: they’re afraid, and it feels awful right now. “I know this is hard” speaks to the one who is scared. It gives the condition no authority. I meet the person, not the problem, then turn thought toward what’s true. Science and Health is careful here: we should never deny the everlasting harmony of Soul “simply because, to the mortal senses, there is seeming discord” (390:4–9). Seeming. Naming how it seems is not agreeing it’s so.

Here is what that moment showed me. When our words and expectations forget that understanding unfolds in stages, and we speak as though everyone should arrive already finished, it can feel like a closed door at the entry point. That’s what I mean by packaging: not the truth itself, but the words and expectations we hand it over in. A teenager in the middle of the night, or anyone new, hears: you must already understand before you come in. Wrestling starts to look like failing, so people hide it, and the people who most need the door open feel least able to knock.

I want to say something plainly, and gently. As a community, we haven’t always done this, and it has caused hurt. Too often we’ve answered pain with “that’s not real,” and we’ve treated doubt and wrestling as failing. Some have gone quiet, and some have stayed away. I’m not saying this to blame anyone. Most of us were taught this way by people who loved us and loved the truth, and I’ve closed that door on someone myself. I’m saying it because this series is about the questions we hesitate to ask, and hurt we don’t name keeps the door shut.

It’s true for me, too. Some days Science and Health still seems to pull two ways, the absolute on one hand and the everyday on the other. I used to take that tension to mean I was doing it wrong. Now I see an unfolding, a path of understanding and not a test I’m failing. Jacob wrestled until daybreak and said, “I will not let thee go, except thou bless me” (Genesis 32:26). He left with a new name, Israel (verse 28). The wrestling wasn’t the failure. It was the way through. That’s kinder to me, and kinder to how I see you.

The Bible and Science and Health say this better than I can:

  • Naaman almost walks away from his healing because it arrives in a form he didn’t expect: “Behold, I thought, He will surely come out to me…” His servants answer, “how much rather then, when he saith to thee, Wash, and be clean?” (II Kings 5). Same healing, unexpected packaging.
  • “…it would be foolish to venture beyond our present understanding” (Science and Health, p. 388).
  • “…it is wise not to try to deceive ourselves or others” (Science and Health 8:14–18).

Thought changes in stages, “from fear to hope and from faith to understanding” (Science and Health 125:12–16), so we meet people, and ourselves, where we are.

Two claims to test

Daniel asked to be tested (Daniel 1:12). Two tests for you, one objection from inside our community and one from outside:

1. “Acknowledging someone’s pain compromises their healing.” The next time someone is hurting, acknowledge how they feel (not that the condition is real), then turn toward what’s true. Notice what happens to the conversation, and to your own sense of the truth.

2. “Christian Science says pain isn’t real, so it can’t truly comfort anyone.” This is a common criticism. When has comfort felt cold to you? When has it felt like love?

Same truth. A door that’s easier to walk through. Honest questions, asked in love: have you been on either side of that door? I’d love to hear. Next, Wednesday, November 25: What We’re Quietly Protecting.

I love this theology. I’m not questioning it. I’m asking about the packaging: how it reaches someone who is in pain.

Late one night recently, a teenager called me, with a parent beside them, asking for prayerful support. They were in the grip of what felt like an anxiety attack. We started with hymns. We started with calming the fear.

I found myself wondering what would have happened if I’d said, “This isn’t true about you. None of this is happening.” In the absolute, that’s clear. But what I wanted them to hear first was: I know this is hard. You’re not alone. This is not a prison. Let’s look at it a different way. And then I’d want to add: You are so loved, and I’d like us to talk about what’s true, because the fear is often about something bigger than what’s in front of us.

The Bible and Science and Health say this better than I can. The Christian Science Bible Lesson “Soul and Body” (week of November 16–22, 2026) is full of it:

  • Naaman almost walks away from his healing because it arrives in a form he didn’t expect: “Behold, I thought, He will surely come out to me…” His servants say, “if the prophet had bid thee do some great thing, wouldest thou not have done it? how much rather then, when he saith to thee, Wash, and be clean?” (II Kings 5). Same healing, unexpected packaging.
  • The Golden Text names the feeling first: “Why art thou cast down, O my soul? and why art thou disquieted within me? hope thou in God” (Psalms 42:11).
  • “…it would be foolish to venture beyond our present understanding” (Science and Health, p. 388).
  • “…we should never deny the everlasting harmony of Soul, simply because, to the mortal senses, there is seeming discord” (Science and Health 390:4–9).

Thought changes in stages, “from fear to hope and from faith to understanding” (Science and Health 125:12–16). So the goal isn’t only to calm one symptom. It’s to change our thought about what’s real and what limits us, and to meet people at the stage they’re in.

Two claims to test

Daniel asked to be tested (Daniel 1:12). Here are two for you.

1. “The second I acknowledge what someone is going through, I compromise their healing.” Next time you’re with someone who is hurting, name what they’re feeling first, kindly and honestly, then share what you know is true. Notice what happens to the conversation. Notice what happens to your own sense of the truth.

2. A common criticism of Christian Science is that it says pain isn’t real, so it can’t truly comfort anyone. Test that against your own experience. When has comfort felt cold to you? When has it felt like love?

I love Christian Science. I’m not questioning what it teaches. I’m asking about the packaging: how it reaches someone who is in pain.

Late one night recently, a teenager called me, with a parent beside them, asking for prayerful support. They were in the grip of what felt like an anxiety attack. We started with hymns. We started with calming the fear.

I found myself wondering what would have happened if I’d said, “This isn’t true about you. None of this is happening.” In the absolute (what Christian Science holds to be the ultimate truth about us), that’s clear. But what I wanted them to hear first was: I know this is hard. You’re not alone. This is not a prison. Let’s look at it a different way. And then I’d want to add: You are so loved, and I’d like us to talk about what’s true, because the fear is often about something bigger than what’s in front of us.

Here is what that moment showed me. When our words and expectations forget that understanding unfolds in stages, and we speak as though everyone should arrive already finished, it can feel like a closed door at the entry point. A teenager in the middle of the night, or anyone new, hears: you must already understand before you come in. Wrestling starts to look like failing, so people hide it, and the people who most need the door open feel least able to knock. I’ve stood on the wrong side of that door, and I’ve probably closed it on someone else.

It’s true for me, too. I’ve read Science and Health more times than I can count, and some days it still seems to pull two ways. I eat. I wear clothes. And I’m taught that I’m wholly spiritual. Some days I need the absolute. Some days I need to manage what’s in front of me while my thought changes. For a long time I took that tension to mean I was doing it wrong. Now I see an unfolding: a path of understanding, not a test I’m failing. That’s kinder to me, and kinder to how I see you.

Jacob wrestled until daybreak and said, “I will not let thee go, except thou bless me” (Genesis 32:26). He didn’t leave that night with his old name. He left as Israel (verse 28). The wrestling wasn’t the failure. It was the way through.

The Bible and Science and Health say this better than I can:

  • Naaman almost walks away from his healing because it arrives in a form he didn’t expect: “Behold, I thought, He will surely come out to me…” His servants answer, “how much rather then, when he saith to thee, Wash, and be clean?” (II Kings 5). Same healing, unexpected packaging.
  • “…it would be foolish to venture beyond our present understanding” (Science and Health, p. 388).
  • “…we should never deny the everlasting harmony of Soul, simply because, to the mortal senses, there is seeming discord” (Science and Health 390:4–9).
  • “…it is wise not to try to deceive ourselves or others” (Science and Health 8:14–18).

Thought changes in stages, “from fear to hope and from faith to understanding” (Science and Health 125:12–16). The goal isn’t only to calm one symptom. It’s to change our thought about what’s real and what limits us, meeting people, and ourselves, where we are.

Two claims to test

Daniel asked to be tested (Daniel 1:12). Two tests for you:

1. “If I admit I’m in the messy middle, I’m failing.” The next time you wrestle with a passage, a problem, or someone else’s pain, notice what the wrestling gives you. What did Jacob walk away with?

2. A common criticism is that Christian Science says pain isn’t real, so it can’t truly comfort anyone. When has comfort felt cold to you? When has it felt like love?

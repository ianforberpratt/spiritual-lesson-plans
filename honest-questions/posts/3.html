Part 3 of 3 in Honest Questions CS, a short series on the questions beneath the surface, the ones that rarely get asked out loud. I’m not the only one asking them. I love Christian Science, and joining in is what I’ve been led to do. New here? You don’t need to have read the others, though they’re above.

A few years ago I went to the church I grew up in. I had my lesson printed out, and during the service I was writing in the margins, the way I always do. No squeaky pen. I was in a nice sweater and jeans, sitting cross-legged in the chair.

I could feel the room tighten. That’s not what we do.

And I understood it. That church is comfortable for them. It’s a memory they love, and it isn’t bad. There just wasn’t bandwidth for my way of showing up. I wasn’t angry. I was sad, and I think they were, too.

I’d felt a version of that before. On a road trip, I walked into a beautiful church with a few people in it and music from a recording, and I was a stranger there, too. This time I was a stranger in the room where I grew up.

Same me. Same love for both rooms. Both rooms were keeping something precious safe: the first had kept the doors open, the second had kept the memory intact. Neither was wrong. And in both, someone new, even someone from the family, had nowhere to land.

My first reaction was indignant. That’s the rebel in me. Then something quieter came. I thought this was my home and these were my people, and I didn’t feel accepted. It made me want to find another church. I didn’t, because of my loyalty there. And I wonder how many people feel the same way: wishing for a different setup, and staying out of loyalty. I don’t think they’re few. I think they’re quiet.

Some of what closes the door isn’t said at all. It’s felt. I felt judgment in that room, and I was judging back. We belong to a religion that leaves judgment to God, and most of us don’t notice when we do it, me included.

We say, and I believe, that we are unlimited, whole, complete, and perfect. That’s true, and “it’s already perfect and whole” can still end a conversation before it begins, because it can sound like there are no chapters. But life has chapters, and so do churches. Mrs. Eddy is honest about that, in her own words:

  • Material belief is slow to acknowledge what the spiritual fact implies. (Science and Health with Key to the Scriptures, Mary Baker Eddy, p. 20:24–25)
  • “Work out your own salvation,” is the demand of Life and Love, for to this end God worketh with you. “Occupy till I come!” Wait for your reward, and “be not weary in well doing.” If your endeavors are beset by fearful odds, and you receive no present reward, go not back to error, nor become a sluggard in the race. (Science and Health with Key to the Scriptures, Mary Baker Eddy, p. 22:11)
  • …gain a little each day in the right direction, till at last he finishes his course with joy (Science and Health with Key to the Scriptures, Mary Baker Eddy, p. 21:13)

She is candid about the difficulty and steady about the direction. “An improved belief cannot retrograde” (Science and Health with Key to the Scriptures, Mary Baker Eddy, p. 442:19). What has truly improved stays improved. Saying this better isn’t walking backward. It’s the next step.

To the people in that room, the ones who love it exactly as it is: you’re loved, and this is still my home. I’m trying hard not to judge you back. Most of us were never taught how to have conversations like this. So here’s my question: how can we have both? What you need, what I need, and everything in between?

At our Thanksgiving table this year, we played a game with conversation starters. One asked: would you rather be the best player on the worst team, or the worst player on the best team? Every one of us picked the best team. None of us felt like the worst. We felt like learners, and we all wanted to be pulled up. The best team is Divine Love, and we’re already on it.

There’s a common mistake in change: treating the people who built something as the obstacle. They’re the reason there’s anything to carry forward. Every change begins with an ending, and people who loved the old way need room to mourn it. That isn’t resistance. That’s love.

So the goal isn’t to replace what they love. It’s to keep it alive in a form the next person can walk into.

What is the legacy we’re protecting? Mrs. Eddy answers plainly: “the demonstration of divine Love casting out error and healing the sick… as must be the case in the cycles of divine light” (Science and Health, p. 135). Our chapters will look different. The reflection of Love doesn’t. And your not looking at this is just as divinely inspired as my being led to look at it and ask. It will take all of us: the ones who keep the light on, and the ones who carry it out the door.

So here’s what I’d love to hear in every church meeting and group discussion: the loving questions about how we move forward together, serving the needs of this world. Three table questions, my plain principles in question form:

1. Honor. What do we love about how we gather, and want to honor and carry into the future?

2. Welcome. Who isn’t here yet, or used to be, and what might they need from us?

3. Build. What’s one small thing we could build together before we meet again?

Two claims to test

I promised each installment would give you claims to test. Here are two, one objection from inside our community, one from outside:

1. “Changing how we say it means changing what it is.” Say one thing you often say about Christian Science in plain words to someone new. Did the Love change? Did anything true get lost?

2. “Faith is just nostalgia.” If you could keep only one thing from the church you remember, would it be the room, or the Love?

If you’ve read this far, I hope you feel seen. Maybe you hold the room exactly as it is. Maybe you’ve been quietly wishing for something different. Maybe you stepped away, or never came in. Whoever you are, you’re part of this conversation. That’s all I’m after: to say what usually goes unsaid, in love, and to leave you a seat at the table.

Which of the three table questions would you ask first, and who would you ask? I’d love to hear. Thank you for walking through this series with me.

The Bridge In

We see every young person as whole and complete, perfect and able. Not broken, then fixed. Whole — and then we begin.

We've had nearly every teaching and mentoring experience we can think of. We've had the best lesson prepared and the kids haven't even looked up or engaged. We've had a lesson we thought was horrible and heard later from a parent that the young person loved it. We've also had years where we thought we had this figured out — and then a kid asked us something that undid all of it in about four seconds.

Here's what I've landed on: none of that disqualifies you. You don't have to have arrived to be ready to walk in that door. You just have to be willing to actually be there.

This page used to be a page. Now it's a bridge — six planks, from wherever you're standing to that room. Walk it once before your first class, or walk it again after ten years. Either way, it's yours to cross.

Plank 1 of 6

Presence Is the Whole Job

Every kid who walks into that room is already whole and complete, perfect and able. Not a project to fix. Not a problem to manage. That's the ground you're standing on before you say a single word.

Presence is the whole job — more than the lesson.

Put the phone away. Get on their eye level. Let a kid's tangent matter as much as your outline.

Kids can tell the difference between an adult who's managing them and an adult who's actually with them. It's that second kind of presence — more than any curriculum — that they'll remember as what love felt like.

Managing

“Let's stay on track, okay?”

With them

“Wait — tell me more about that.”

Not weakness management. That's just love, put to work.

Plank 2 of 6

Asking, Not Answering

Great facilitation is less about having the right answer and more about asking the right question — and then having the patience to let silence do its work.

Open with something they can actually answer from their own life, not a trivia question with one right response. Use “I wonder...” instead of “the answer is...” — it invites rather than tests.

Watch the room, not the lesson plan. If something you said just landed hard for one kid, follow that thread — even if the craft doesn't get finished.

Tests

“The answer is: God loves you no matter what.”

Invites

“I wonder what it would feel like if love never ran out.”

Plank 5 of 6

Who's Actually in the Room

Whatever generation raised you, this isn't quite it. Here's what's actually reaching them right now.

Honesty over polish

This generation isn't looking for certainty. They're looking for adults who'll admit doubt out loud.

Real questions, not dangerous ones

They want their questions treated as real — never as a threat to manage.

Consistency over performance

Showing up the same way, week after week, teaches more than performed confidence ever will.

Belonging before belief

They want to feel like they matter in the room before they're asked to sign onto anything.

They can smell a script

What reaches them is a real person, still figuring it out, willing to sit in the question with them instead of rushing to close it.

Belonging before belief.

Plank 6 of 6

However many years you've done this, this plank doesn't change. Read it the same way every time.

If It's Bigger Than You

Trust the moment

Trust the moment you think “this is bigger than me” — that instinct is almost always right, and it's not a failure to act on it. Bring in another adult when a behavior is safety-related, when a child discloses something serious, when a conflict isn't resolving with your usual tools, or when your own emotions are running too hot to respond well.

Asking for backup models exactly what you're trying to teach: that none of us are meant to carry things alone.

If a child discloses

If you're a Sunday school teacher, you may be a mandated reporter under your state's law — which generally means that if a child discloses or you suspect abuse or neglect, you're legally required to report it. Not investigate it. Not confront a parent. Just report it, promptly, to the appropriate authority or hotline.

Reporting laws and processes vary by state, so know your organization's exact policy and reporting number before you're ever in the room alone with kids. This isn't about suspicion of families — it's about being the safe adult a child can tell.

The quiet, structural stuff

Good safety practice is quiet and structural, not dramatic: two adults in a room whenever possible, doors with windows or left open, a clear check-in/check-out process so no child leaves with someone unauthorized, and a known plan for allergies, injuries, and emergencies before you need it. Know where the first aid kit is and who to call.

Safety isn't in tension with warmth — it's what makes real warmth possible, because kids and parents relax when they can feel that someone thought it through.

After the room empties

You carry this too

The planks above are about the young person in front of you. This part is about you.

Leading a lesson on regret, disappointment, or identity — or being the person someone chooses to disclose something serious to — doesn't end when the session does. It's normal to feel unsettled, wrung out, or like you're still turning the conversation over hours later. That's not a sign you handled it wrong.

A few honest suggestions, not rules:

  • Tell someone. Not the details if they're not yours to share — just “today was heavy.” A co-mentor, a friend, a supervisor. Carrying it alone is the part that actually wears people down.
  • Don't skip your own reset. Whatever settles you — a walk, quiet, prayer, calling someone — do it on purpose after a hard session, not just whenever you happen to get to it.
  • If something a young person shared is sitting with you for days, that's worth talking through with a professional yourself — not because you did anything wrong, but because that's what the weight of real trust deserves.
  • You don't have to have the answer by the next session. It's okay to walk back in and say “I've been thinking about what you shared, and I'm still here” — you don't need to have resolved it for them.

You're allowed to need care too. That's not separate from doing this well — it's part of doing this well.

For parents and guardians

If you need something to share

Spiritual Lesson Plans is a free, open resource for mentoring conversations about identity, belonging, and courage. It isn't a church, a curriculum sold by any organization, or an attempt to convert anyone to anything. The ideas draw on Christian Science — mainly the premise that a young person is already whole and worthy, not something to fix — but every lesson is written to welcome any faith background, or none, and no young person is ever asked to believe anything specific. There's no cost, no account, and we don't collect any data ourselves — the only exception is the optional Translate tool, which is powered by Google and follows Google's own terms if you choose to use it. You're welcome to read every lesson yourself first at spirituallessonplans.org.

For Mentors

You don't need training. You need a way in.

These lessons are built so a first-time mentor and an experienced one can both lead them well. Here's how they work, and how to hold the room.

The approach

Built on adult-learning pedagogy — for young people

That sounds backwards, but it isn't. Andragogy (adult learning theory) treats the learner as someone who already has experience worth drawing on, who learns by doing rather than being told, and who needs to see relevance before they'll engage. Gen Z and Gen Alpha research says the same thing about younger learners: they disengage from lecture, and they lean in when a topic feels personally real and they have some control over how they explore it.

So every lesson here follows the same shape:

  • Start from experience. Open with a question about something the young person has already lived, not a definition.
  • Make it active. A short activity, discussion prompt, or reflection — never just reading at them.
  • Leave room for their own answer. The goal is never to arrive at the "right" response. It's to help them trust their own thinking.
  • Close with the premise. Every lesson returns to the same anchor: you are already whole.

From Off Days Are Allowed · Grade 6–8

Start from experience

“Think of a recent day you felt ‘off’ — disconnected, low, more human than usual. No shame in it. Just notice it happened.”

Make it active

Small-group discussion: “Have you ever felt guilty about feeling bad — like the feeling itself was proof you were failing at something?”

Leave room for their own answer

A private written reflection, never shared aloud unless they choose to: “What would change if a hard day stopped counting as evidence against you?”

Close with the premise

“You don't lose your wholeness on a bad day. It was never something you had to keep earning.”

Facilitation basics

Holding the room well

01

Ask more than you tell

Every lesson includes discussion prompts. Resist the urge to fill silence — a pause is often the young person doing real thinking, not being stuck.

02

Meet them without judgment

Whatever a young person brings — doubt, a different faith, a hard question — the response is curiosity, not correction. That's the whole premise of this resource.

03

Make it theirs

Adapt language, examples, and pacing to your group. A lesson that's been reshaped to fit the room in front of you is being used exactly as intended.

This site avoids assuming a young person already has a fixed idea of God, so lessons often use more open language. When it doesn't go as planned

If things get heavy (they will, sometimes)

You don't need to be a therapist to lead these lessons. You do need to know what to do when the room gets quiet, tense, or real. Here's the honest version, worst case last.

That's not you failing — that's often someone actually thinking. Let it breathe. Ask the question once more, gently, then wait again.

Good. That's curiosity showing up, not a problem to fix. Meet it with more questions, not a correction.

Pause the activity. Take a breath together, or just slow down. Check in with them quietly, one-on-one, without making them the center of everyone's attention.

Stop being a "lesson leader" in that moment and just be a caring adult. Stay calm, thank them for trusting you, don't promise to keep it secret. Then follow your own country's and organization's reporting obligations and loop in a trained professional or the right authority — you are not meant to carry this alone, and neither are they. This isn't legal advice, so please know your own local requirements before you're ever in this seat.

After the room empties

You carry this too

The guidance above is about the young person in front of you. This part is about you.

Leading a lesson on regret, disappointment, or identity — or being the person someone chooses to disclose something serious to — doesn't end when the session does. It's normal to feel unsettled, wrung out, or like you're still turning the conversation over hours later. That's not a sign you handled it wrong.

A few honest suggestions, not rules:

  • Tell someone. Not the details if they're not yours to share — just "today was heavy." A co-mentor, a friend, a supervisor. Carrying it alone is the part that actually wears people down.
  • Don't skip your own reset. Whatever settles you — a walk, quiet, prayer, calling someone — do it on purpose after a hard session, not just whenever you happen to get to it.
  • If something a young person shared is sitting with you for days, that's worth talking through with a professional yourself — not because you did anything wrong, but because that's what the weight of real trust deserves.
  • You don't have to have the answer by the next session. It's okay to walk back in and say "I've been thinking about what you shared, and I'm still here" — you don't need to have resolved it for them.

You're allowed to need care too. That's not separate from doing this well — it's part of doing this well.

For parents and guardians

If you need something to share

Spiritual Lesson Plans is a free, open resource for mentoring conversations about identity, belonging, and courage. It isn't a church, a curriculum sold by any organization, or an attempt to convert anyone to anything. The ideas draw on Christian Science — mainly the premise that a young person is already whole and worthy, not something to fix — but every lesson is written to welcome any faith background, or none, and no young person is ever asked to believe anything specific. There's no cost, no account, and we don't collect any data ourselves — the only exception is the optional Translate tool, which is powered by Google and follows Google's own terms if you choose to use it. You're welcome to read every lesson yourself first at spirituallessonplans.org.

## What you should know

At this age kids are expert helpers and expert helpees. They grab the controller, finish the project, fix the group-work slide, answer for the friend who's thinking. They also sometimes stop trying because someone will jump in. Both are normal, and neither one is a flaw. The lesson gives them two pictures they can hold: a **Controller Hand** (takes over) and a **Coach Hand** (stays beside), and a quick five-question check they can run in their head. The big idea underneath: *the power to do it is already in your friend. You're not their battery.* Say it that way, in kid language, and don't turn it into a speech. Nothing to prepare beyond paper and pencils; the handout is optional and printable.

## What we're assuming

- The room likely holds many faith backgrounds. Kid-language wording for the big idea is "something good and bigger than any problem is in charge here, not the problem, not my friend, and not me." If your group is comfortable with God language, you can say "God" in that sentence instead. Offer it, never require it.
- Some kids are the "responsible one" at home, and some are the ones who get things done for them. Don't ask anyone which.
- No one has to share out loud. Drawing and pairing up count as full participation.

## Learning goals

1. Can tell the difference between a Controller Hand (takes over) and a Coach Hand (stays beside).
2. Feels, from both sides, what it's like to have a friend grab the controller, and what it's like to have one ask.
3. Has a quick five-question check to run before stepping in.
4. Knows that if a friend is hurt or in danger, getting a trusted grown-up right away is always the right call.

## How to hold the room

- Keep your tone light and curious. No one in the story, and no one in the room, is the bad guy.
- If a kid says, "I just do it faster, so I do it for them," say, "Yeah, that makes sense. Let's see what else we can try."
- Never ask a child to describe a real friend or family member who "does everything for" them. Offer, don't require.
- Any activity can be passed. Sitting and watching is still taking part.
- Skip the wrap-up moral. If the pairs worked, the kids have it.

## The lesson flow

### Beat 1 (~7 min) — Stuck on level 7

Read this aloud, then pause for a thumbs up, sideways, or down: "Has anything like this ever happened to you, from either side?"

*Dani and Priya are playing a co-op game. Priya keeps dying on level 7. Dani can feel her own stomach tightening. "Give me the controller," says Dani, and takes it. Dani beats the level in two minutes. Priya says, "Okay... thanks," in a very small voice, and doesn't play the next level.*

*Another day, a different game. Priya is stuck again. This time Dani takes a breath. "Do you want me to take over, or give you ideas?" Priya says, "Ideas." Dani says, "What do you think the boss is weak to?" Priya figures it out. It takes ten minutes. Priya yells "YES!" so loud the dog barks.*

Ask lightly: "Same game, same friend. What was different about the two times?" Take two or three answers. No more.

### Beat 2 (~8 min) — Two hands

Introduce the two pictures with a gesture each: **Controller Hand** (grabbing, reaching into someone else's space) and **Coach Hand** (open hand, standing beside, pointing). Sort a few real-feeling examples together, with the group voting by holding up the hand they think fits:

- Finishing a classmate's art project because it's taking too long.
- Saying, "What have you tried so far?" to a friend stuck on a math problem.
- Telling a friend exactly what to say to someone they had a fight with.
- Asking, "Do you want advice, or do you just want me to listen?"
- Doing a little brother's chores every time, so there's no fuss.

Then say one thing plainly, in your own words: **"Your friend isn't a battery that's run out of power, and you're not the charger. The power to figure it out is already in them. You can still help. You're just helping from beside them, not from in front."**

If your group uses God words, you might add, "Some people say God is the one who gives that power, in all of us." Say it once and move on.

Then add the safety rule, clearly, without any drama: **"If a friend is hurt or in danger, that's not a Coach Hand moment. You get a trusted grown-up right away. Their need is real, and you don't have to hold it alone."**

### Beat 3 (~13 min) — Five questions, in pairs

Hand out (or write on a board) the five kid-language questions. Walk through them once together using the Dani and Priya story, then let pairs use them on a new situation of their own choosing (or one of these: a friend keeps forgetting their project; a friend is sad again about the same thing; a teammate is behind on a group slide).

**The Five Questions**
1. **Is anyone hurt or in danger?** If yes, get a grown-up now.
2. **Am I calm, or am I panicking?** If I'm panicking, I take a breath and wait a second.
3. **Do I think my friend can do this?** If not, I name one thing they're good at, then ask again.
4. **Did they ask me?** If not, I ask, "Want help, or want me to listen?"
5. **Is it something they can do themselves?** If yes, I coach. If no, I help, no strings.

Underneath, in smaller letters: **Either way: stay close. I'm a friend, not the battery.**

One partner reads the situation. The other walks the five questions out loud (or silently with fingers). Then switch. Let them argue about it a bit. It isn't a test, and there is not always a single right answer.

### Beat 4 (~8 min) — The hand I'll try

Everyone traces one hand. Inside it, they write or draw one situation this week where they could try the Coach Hand instead of the Controller Hand, or one place where they could ask for coaching instead of waiting for someone to take over. Passing is fine; a blank hand is a hand.

Hand out the wallet card. Don't add a closing moral. Let the pairs and the hands stand.

## If it's not going well

Silence is fine; this age often draws and acts better than it talks. Pushback ("but I'm faster!" or "my friend never even tries") is welcome: "That's real. Let's try the five questions on exactly that." A strong emotional reaction, such as a kid who looks upset because they carry a lot at home or feel nobody helps them, calls for a pause, a quiet word one-on-one, and no pressure to explain. If a child describes real harm or a situation that sounds unsafe, don't work through it in the room: stay warm, receive it ("Thank you for telling me, I take that seriously"), and follow the site's mentor safety guidelines along with your own local mandatory-reporting obligations.

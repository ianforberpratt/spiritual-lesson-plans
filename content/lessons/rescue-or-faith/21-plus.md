## What you should know

Adults carry this one in marriages, in parenting, in caring for an aging parent, in the sibling who needs money again, in the friend who calls with the same crisis, in the colleague who's behind. Some in the room are exhausted caregivers. Some are the person who has been depending, and who knows what it is to wait to be rescued. Many are both, depending on the week. Three mistakes tend to look like love: the *savior thought* ("whether they make it depends on me"), *waiting to be rescued*, and *"I'm just trusting God"* used as a polite way of stepping back. This lesson holds all three honestly. Its center is a steadying claim: **no one is anyone else's salvation, because God, divine Love, is the only power, so the help we give is only as wise as the thought we give it from.** Expect a mix of relief, recognition, and real disagreement. Nothing to prepare beyond paper; the handouts are optional and printable.

## What we're assuming

- The room holds a real range of faith, doubt, and none, and possibly a lot of history with organized religion, some of it painful. The faith language below is offered as an invitation, never a requirement. A plain-language version is: "something good and bigger than any problem is in charge here — not the problem, not them, and not me."
- Some participants are in the middle of a long, hard stretch with someone they love, or carry guilt about a past decision. Some are the one being carried. No one has to say which, or share any detail.
- Nobody has to speak. Private reflection is a complete use of this lesson.

## Learning goals

1. Can tell, in their own life, the difference between help that carries a person and help that stands beside them.
2. Can name the two mirror images of rescuing, *waiting to be rescued* and *"trusting God" as a way to step back*, and why neither is faith.
3. Leaves with a repeatable check to run before stepping in, a way to pause when "grounded" isn't there yet, and the clear knowledge that pausing never delays a real emergency.
4. Holds the paradox, that someone may truly need our hands *and* the thought behind the help matters, without resolving it neatly.

## How to hold the room

- Open with curiosity. You aren't there to model "the right way to help someone you love."
- The big claim here is an invitation to pause, never a verdict on a past decision. If someone says, "I stepped in and it went badly," say, "That sounds like it came from real care. This is just something to try next time."
- Welcome disagreement, including "this is too soft" and "this sounds like permission to walk away." Both are real. The second one deserves a direct answer: it isn't.
- Never ask anyone to share details of another person's private struggle. A category ("my son," "my husband," "my mother") is enough.
- Offer a way to pass on any part, and a private-reflection option before any sharing.
- You're not there to resolve the paradox neatly by the end of the hour. If the room leaves still thinking, that's the lesson doing its work.

## The lesson flow

### Beat 1 (~8 min) — Who comes to mind

Ask the room to bring one person to mind, silently: someone they love, who is struggling, and whom they feel the pull to step in for. A spouse or partner, a child, an adult sibling, an aging parent, a friend, a coworker. Then, still privately: *What does it feel like in my body when I think of them? What is the voice saying?* Offer a few as possibilities, not answers: *It's all on me. If I let go, they fall. I'm the only one who sees it. I'm exhausted and I feel guilty for being exhausted. If I help, I'll finally feel at peace.*

Invite a few volunteers to name just the category and, if they like, one word for the feeling. No details required. Then say: "That is the savior thought: *whether they make it depends on me.* It looks like love. Often it is love. But it's carrying a second thing along with the love."

### Beat 2 (~12 min) — Three grounds, and what the texts say

Offer the three grounds, written where everyone can see:

- **The only power:** Is God the only power here? *Not the problem, not them, not me. Only God has power in this moment.*
- **Them:** Are they able and whole? *Capable of their own good. They don't need me to survive, to make it.*
- **Me:** Am I on solid ground? *Able to hear wisdom and discernment: calm, clear, unhurried, before I act.*

Read these together, one at a time, letting each settle:

> "There is no power apart from God. Omnipotence has all-power, and to acknowledge any other power is to dishonor God." — Science and Health, Mary Baker Eddy, p. 228:25-27

> "Life, Truth, and Love understood and demonstrated as supreme over all; sin, sickness, and death destroyed." — Science and Health, Mary Baker Eddy, p. 593:20-22

> "...to show them how to do theirs, but not to do it for them nor to relieve them of a single responsibility." — Science and Health, Mary Baker Eddy, p. 18:6-9

> "'Work out your own salvation,' is the demand of Life and Love, for to this end God worketh with you." — Science and Health, Mary Baker Eddy, p. 22:11-12

> "One kind of faith trusts one's welfare to others. Another kind of faith understands divine Love and how to work out one's 'own salvation, with fear and trembling.'" — Science and Health, Mary Baker Eddy, p. 23:23-27

Note, plainly: in the Glossary, *salvation* is "Life, Truth, and Love understood and demonstrated." It isn't something one person does to another. Then read two lines of Paul together, and **don't resolve them**:

> "Bear ye one another's burdens, and so fulfil the law of Christ." — Galatians 6:2, KJV

> "For every man shall bear his own burden." — Galatians 6:5, KJV

Ask: "Both are in the same chapter. How do you live inside that?" Let it sit.

### Beat 3 (~14 min) — Same moment, two thoughts

In small groups (or pairs, or privately), take one or two of these moments. Each shows the *same* help from two different thoughts. Ask the group to name each thought, and what might feel different afterward, to the helper *and* to the person helped.

- **Spouse, a rough week.** One thinks, "It's all on me to fix this," so they fix fast, hand over a plan, and quietly keep score. Another thinks, "God is at work in them," so they ask: *help, or a listener?* And do exactly that.
- **Child, forgot the project.** One thinks, "If I don't step in, they'll fail," so they drive it over and panic until it's safe. Another thinks, "They're able," so they ask, "What's your plan?", stay close, and help where truly needed.
- **Sibling, needs money again.** One thinks, "I'm their only rescue," so they wire it and resent it, or refuse and call it tough love. Another thinks, "I'm not their source," so they decide what they can give freely, say it kindly, and trust them.
- **Friend, venting again.** One thinks, "I have to fix this pain," so they hand out advice nobody asked for. Another thinks, "They already hold their next step," so they listen, and often they find it.
- **Peer, a teammate is behind.** One thinks, "Nobody else will catch it," so they quietly redo the work and feel needed. Another thinks, "They're capable," so they ask what's blocking them and give what they can freely.

After a few minutes, name the **mirror images** out loud, since they're the same mistake in different clothes:

- **Waiting to be rescued.** My wellbeing rests on someone else being my savior. (Hoping the right spouse, parent, friend, or boss will finally fix it.) For some in the room, this is the more familiar one.
- **"I'm just trusting God."** Said as a polite way of stepping back from someone who needs a hand. It sounds like faith. It's really stepping away. Faith stays.

Say the paradox directly: "They may truly, humanly need your help: a ride, a meal, a hospital, a listening ear. That is real, and nothing here asks you to pretend otherwise. The help can look identical. The thought behind it never does."

Then ask the room, openly: "Where did you recognize yourself? On either side?" Take a few, then let it sit.

### Beat 4 (~16 min) — A pause, a flow, and a private locate

Offer the **bold claim** as an invitation. Say: "If I'm in panic, having crowned myself the savior, I may not make wise decisions." Then read:

> "[In mental practice you must not forget that] erring human opinions, conflicting selfish motives, and ignorant attempts to do good may render you incapable of knowing or judging accurately the need of your fellow-men." — Science and Health, Mary Baker Eddy, p. 447:6-9

> "This Soul-sense comes to the human mind when the latter yields to the divine Mind." — Science and Health, Mary Baker Eddy, p. 85:4-6

> "Let neither fear nor doubt overshadow your clear sense and calm trust." — Science and Health, Mary Baker Eddy, p. 495:16-18

**Say the honesty note plainly, because it matters:** Eddy wrote 447:6-9 about *mental practice* (a healing context), and 495:16-18 about being tempted by the illusion of sickness or sin. Applying them to everyday helping is an extension, a facilitator's reading, not something she wrote about marriage or parenting. If someone asks, say so. Hold it as an invitation to pause, never as a verdict on anyone's past decisions, and never as a way to shame someone who helped imperfectly.

Walk through **the flow** (post it, or use the handout). Five questions, in order. Quality of thought comes first.

**START:** Someone I love is struggling, and I feel the pull to step in.

1. **Is anyone in danger right now?** If yes: **ACT NOW.** Call, drive, get help, now. Their need is real, so act, and stay as calm as you can.
2. **Am I grounded: God the only power, me able to listen?** If no: **PAUSE · GET GROUNDED.** Breathe. Pray. Listen. A hurried decision isn't wise yet. Come back when calm. (In a mixed room, "pray" and "get quiet and listen" are equal alternatives.)
3. **Do I see them as able, not needing me to survive?** If no: **LOOK AGAIN.** God is their power, not me. Name one real strength in them. Then ask again.
4. **Did they ask for my help?** If not (I decided for them): **OFFER, DON'T IMPOSE.** "I'm here. What would help?" Then listen, and let them decide.
5. **Would I be doing what they can do themselves?** If yes: **WALK BESIDE.** Ask questions. Name their strengths. Stay close without taking over. If no: **GIVE FREELY.** They may truly need a ride, a meal, an ear. Give it, no strings, no scorekeeping.

**Either way:** Stay close. God is the only power, already at work in them. I'm a friend, not the source.

Then the four pressure tests, as a quick gut-check: *If this goes badly, do I feel it all rests on me? Can I name one real strength in them that this can't touch? Is the leading I hear calm and clear, or loud and rushed? If they never thank me or take my advice, am I still steady?* A panic answer means: pause first, and come back.

Close with a private locate: "Who have I been 'carrying'? Or where have I been waiting to be rescued? What's one thing I could do this week that comes from solid ground, and one thing I could stop doing?" Write it or hold it. Nobody shares. Hand out the wallet card and the flow.

## If it's not going well

Silence is fine; let it sit. Pushback ("this sounds like permission to walk away" or "my person really can't do this alone") is welcome and deserves a real answer: nothing here asks anyone to withhold needed care, and "wholeness" is never a reason to leave someone to cope alone. Question 1 always comes first, and the pause never delays a real emergency. A strong emotional reaction, such as an exhausted caregiver, guilt over a past decision, or grief about being the one who was left to wait, calls for a pause, grounding the room, and a private check-in; offer space to step out. If someone describes a person in danger, or a situation of abuse or serious risk, don't work it through in the room. Stop facilitating, respond as a caring adult, encourage them to bring in a professional or emergency services, and follow the site's mentor safety guidelines along with your own local mandatory-reporting obligations.

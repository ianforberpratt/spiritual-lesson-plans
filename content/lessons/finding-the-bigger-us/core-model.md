# Lesson Core Model: "Finding the Bigger 'Us'"

*One theological core, five age-specific deliveries. Everything in this document is shared scaffolding — it does not get taught directly to any age group. It's the thing every age version is checked against, so the different lessons stay recognizably the same truth even though the room, the words, and the activities look nothing alike from one band to the next.*

Note on bands: this lesson is built for 5-8, 8-11, 11-14, 14-21, and 21+. All five genuinely fit — the pull toward "us vs. them" thinking shows up as early as two kids fighting over one toy and stays live, in higher-stakes form, through a lifetime of family, workplace, and political disagreement.

## Central claim

"Us vs. them" is a picture, not a fact. Underneath almost every real disagreement, there is a shared want or shared aim that both sides actually hold — even while their positions, opinions, or priorities genuinely differ. Finding that shared ground doesn't erase the disagreement. It just means the disagreement was never the whole picture to begin with.

## The picture being replaced

A world sorted into fixed, opposing sides, where belonging requires an enemy and being right requires someone else to be wrong. Under this picture, disagreement gets treated as proof of separateness — as if two people who see something differently must therefore want fundamentally different things. This picture makes ordinary disagreement feel bigger and more threatening than it usually is, and it makes common ground feel like something you'd have to give up your own view to find.

## The picture being offered

A "bigger us": whatever two people (or two groups) are actually aiming at underneath their disagreement, once you look for it — understanding, fairness, being heard, safety, belonging, getting to play, getting home safe. Real differences remain real. They just stop being the entire story. Genuine community, in the broadest sense the word can hold, is people gathered around something larger than what currently divides them.

## Anchor citations

Used at whatever depth and reading level fits the age band — younger bands may only ever hear the underlying idea in a story, with no citation at all, and that's enough.

- "One infinite God, good, unifies men and nations; constitutes the brotherhood of man..." — *Science and Health with Key to the Scriptures*, Mary Baker Eddy, p. 340:23
- "That they all may be one; as thou, Father, art in me, and I in thee, that they also may be one in us." — John 17:21, KJV
- "There is neither Jew nor Greek, there is neither bond nor free, there is neither male nor female: for ye are all one in Christ Jesus." — Galatians 3:28, KJV
- "Church: ...The structure of Truth and Love; whatever rests upon and proceeds from divine Principle." — *Science and Health*, p. 583:10

## Learning objective family

Each age band aims at a version of the same four objectives — the verbs and depth shift, the target doesn't:

1. **Notice** the moment they're being pulled toward "us vs. them" thinking — choosing a side, or seeing someone as "the other."
2. **Feel**, not just understand intellectually, that naming a shared want doesn't mean giving up their own view or pretending the disagreement isn't real.
3. **Carry** one repeatable phrase for naming the shared ground out loud, in an actual disagreement.
4. **Locate** one real relationship or situation in their own life where this applies.

## The bridge, by age band

This is the piece that's genuinely different per age, and it's the hinge the whole lesson turns on.

| Age band | What the core tension looks like concretely | Bridge used |
|---|---|---|
| 5-8 | Two kids fighting over one toy or one turn | A story about two kids who both just want to play — not "sides," just "we both want the same thing" |
| 8-11 | Sibling or classroom disagreements — who goes first, what game to play | Practicing "we both want ___" out loud with a partner, in a low-stakes, concrete scenario |
| 11-14 | Friend-group conflicts, disagreements at school, pressure to pick a side | The existing "bigger us" idea and script, with a private-journaling beat added before anyone is asked to share |
| 14-21 | Choosing sides online, at school, at home | The full "bigger us" concept, the "I don't think we're actually on different sides" script, practiced in pairs on real or hypothetical disagreements |
| 21+ | Political differences with family, workplace conflict, disagreement in a marriage or community | The same script, aimed at adult-scale disagreement, with an explicit, unambiguous line that this never applies to situations involving real harm |

## What does NOT get inherited across bands

- **The words "us vs. them."** This framing stays out of 5-8 entirely — it's an abstraction this age doesn't yet reason in. They get "when you and a friend both want the same thing" instead, which describes the identical situation in language that's already theirs.
- **Open, abstract discussion questions.** 5-8 gets no discussion questions at all — only simple, concrete asks and role-play with a puppet or toy. Concrete-operational thinkers at this age can't yet reflect out loud on an abstract idea like "shared ground beneath disagreement"; asking them to produces confused silence or rote mimicry, not real understanding.
- **Private journaling before sharing.** This beat starts at 11-14, where self-consciousness about being watched while vulnerable becomes intense enough to need it. Younger bands don't need it (they share more freely and with less at stake); by 21+ it's assumed and lighter-touch, since adults are generally comfortable choosing whether to share without being prompted to write first.
- **Political, workplace, and marriage-scale examples.** These stay in the 21+ version, and appear only as optional, clearly-hypothetical material at 14-21. They have no honest analogue in a school-age or middle-school child's daily life, and importing them would make the lesson feel like it's talking past the room instead of to it.
- **Any suggestion that this technique applies to situations of real harm.** At every age, the lesson is scoped to everyday disagreement — not abuse, bullying with real power imbalance, or genuine danger. This boundary doesn't loosen as the audience gets older; if anything, it needs to be stated more explicitly at 21+, where the adult-scale examples (a difficult marriage, a hostile workplace) make the line between "disagreement" and "harm" easier to blur by accident.

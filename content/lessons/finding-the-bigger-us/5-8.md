## What you should know
This age doesn't reason in "sides" yet, and shouldn't be introduced to that word here. What they know very well is the feeling of wanting the same toy, turn, or seat as a friend, and the tangle of feelings that comes with it. Skip any language like "us vs. them" — it's an abstraction this age can't yet hold. Instead, stay entirely inside one concrete picture: two kids, one toy, both wanting to play. Don't add a spoken-out-loud "moral" at the end. Let the story and the sentence carry it; naming it out loud afterward often flattens what they just felt.
## What we're assuming
- Every child in the room has fought with a friend or sibling over a toy, a turn, or a seat, and remembers how that felt.
- This is not the moment to sort out who was "right" in a real fight from this week. The point is much narrower: when it feels like a fight over who gets something, it's often really about wanting the same thing.
- Some kids may mention a real fight mid-story ("that happened to me!"). Receive it warmly and keep moving, rather than opening it up into a group conversation.
- No child should be asked to publicly resolve or revisit an actual unresolved conflict with a specific friend in this lesson — keep the practice general and low-stakes.

## Learning goals
1. A felt sense (not a spoken definition) that a fight over a toy is often really about both kids wanting to play.
2. One sentence they can say out loud: "I don't think we're fighting about who gets it — I think we both just want to play."
3. The experience of practicing that sentence once, with a puppet or a partner, so it's already in their mouth before they need it for real.
4. The experience of being welcomed and included regardless of how they answer any question in this lesson.

## How to hold the room
- Keep it moving. This age holds attention for a story and a role-play, not for sitting and talking about feelings in the abstract.
- Use two puppets, dolls, or toy figures for the story if you have them — seeing the fight acted out lands harder than hearing it described.
- If a child shares something vulnerable or names a real ongoing fight with a friend, receive it simply ("I'm glad you told me that") and move on rather than opening a group discussion.
- There are no open discussion questions in this lesson on purpose. Every ask below is concrete: point, show, say the line, act it out.

## The lesson flow
### Beat 1 (~3 min) — Gathering
Sit in a circle. Welcome warmly: "I'm so glad you're here today. Every single one of you." Use names if the group is small enough.

Ask (simple, concrete, one at a time — not a discussion): "Has anyone ever wanted the exact same toy as a friend, at the exact same time? Thumbs up if that's happened to you."

### Beat 2 (~5 min) — The story
Tell this with two puppets or toys if you have them, acting out the parts:

*"Two kids, we'll call them Sam and Rio, both saw the same toy truck at the exact same second, and both grabbed it at the exact same second. 'It's mine, I saw it first!' 'No, it's mine, I had it first!' They pulled and pulled, and neither one was having any fun at all. Then Rio stopped pulling and said, 'Wait — what do you actually want?' And Sam said, 'I just want to play.' And Rio said, 'Me too. I just want to play too.' They looked at the truck. They looked at each other. And then Sam said the thing that fixed it: 'I don't think we're fighting about who gets it — I think we both just want to play.'"*

Ask (concrete, one at a time): "What did Sam and Rio both want, really?" Let a few kids answer — "to play" is the answer, and any version of it is right.

### Beat 3 (~4 min) — Practice the magic sentence, all together
"Let's practice Sam's sentence together, like a magic sentence for when this happens to you." Say it together as a group, two or three times, with energy:

> "I don't think we're fighting about who gets it — I think we both just want to play."

### Beat 4 (~5-6 min) — Try it with puppets or a partner
Pair kids up, or use the puppets again. Give each pair a toy or object to "want" at the same time. Have them act out grabbing it, then pause, and have one of them say the magic sentence out loud. Rotate who says it. Cheer for everyone who tries, however it comes out.

### Closing
Circle up. "Next time you and a friend both want the same thing, you already know what to say. You don't have to fight about it — you can just find out you wanted the same thing all along." Close with the thought that everyone here is loved, exactly as they are, wanting what they want.

## What you'll need
- Two simple puppets, dolls, or toy figures, if available (a story told with your hands works fine too)
- One small toy or object per pair for the role-play in Beat 4
- Nothing else — keep the room simple and uncluttered for this age

## If it's not going well
If a child's account of "fighting over a toy" sounds like more than an ordinary sibling or playground squabble — if a child seems frightened rather than just frustrated, or describes something that sounds like real hurt happening at home or with a peer — don't probe further in the moment. Stay warm, say something like "thank you for telling me, I care about that," and follow the site's mentor safety guidelines (For Mentors page) afterward, including notifying the appropriate adult per your church's child-safety policy. This age sometimes discloses real concerns gently, inside a story that feels safe — take it seriously even though it arrived quietly.

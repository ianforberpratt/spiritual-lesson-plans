## What you should know

This age can genuinely engage the "why seven names, isn't one enough" question as a real question, not just a story — abstract thinking is coming online. Use it: don't hand them the answer, let them sit with why a single word might not carry everything real about someone (or something) complex.

The three-degrees half lands hardest here if you anchor it in something they've actually lived: a real blow-up — an argument, a meltdown, a moment they're not proud of — followed by the effort to hold it together, followed by an actual, non-forced calm. Keep it relational (friendships, family, social pressure), not geopolitical or workplace — that's the older-band version.

## What we're assuming

- Most kids this age have had at least one real "I lost it" moment recently enough to remember it honestly.
- The three-degrees framework can sound like it's ranking people as good or bad based on which stage they're "in." It isn't. Say directly: this is a map of an ordinary day, not a verdict on a person.
- Give kids a private-reflection option (Beat 4) before asking anyone to share specifics out loud — this age needs the option of privacy to be honest at all.

## Learning goals

1. A real answer, in their own words, to "why does Christian Science use seven names for God instead of one?"
2. The ability to name what "first degree," "second degree," and "third degree" actually feel like in a normal week — without the 1875 vocabulary.
3. Carry one real moment — an argument, a group chat, a family tension — mapped honestly onto the three-degree journey.
4. At least one written, private reflection they don't have to share.

## How to hold the room

- Let the "isn't one name enough" question be a real discussion, not a setup for a scripted answer. Sit with the tension before resolving it.
- Don't correct a kid who says the three degrees sound judgmental — that's a fair read of the 1875 wording, and naming it honestly (before reframing it) builds trust.
- Watch for the kid who goes quiet during the private reflection in a way that feels heavier than typical middle-school reserve.

## The lesson flow

### Beat 1 (~6 min) — One name, or several?

Ask: "If you had to describe someone you're close to — completely, not just one thing about them — could you do it in one word?" Let a few answer, then push gently: "What gets lost if you try?"

"Christian Science doesn't use one word for God either. It uses seven: Mind, Spirit, Soul, Principle, Life, Truth, Love."

> "God is incorporeal, divine, supreme, infinite Mind, Spirit, Soul, Principle, Life, Truth, Love." — Science and Health, p. 465

Discuss: "Which of the seven is most familiar to you already — and which one surprises you to see on this list?"

### Beat 2 (~8 min) — Naming the first degree, honestly

"There's also a pattern Christian Science describes for the journey toward actually understanding something — not just believing it, understanding it. It starts somewhere everyone recognizes."

Read, in plain language first: overwhelmed, underwater, tempted, out of control — that's the first stage. Then the actual passage:

> First Degree: Depravity. Physical. Evil beliefs, passions and appetites, fear, self-justification, pride, envy, hatred, revenge. — Science and Health, p. 115

Ask: "Without naming anything private, what does a 'first degree' moment feel like in an ordinary week — a group chat, a fight with a sibling, a bad grade?"

### Beat 3 (~8 min) — The arc of a real blow-up

Walk through the three stages using a real-feeling arc:

1. **Losing it** — the first degree. Saying or doing something you regret in the moment.
2. **Working hard to hold it together** — the second degree. Trying, with real effort, to do better.
3. **Actually being okay** — the third degree. Not forcing it anymore, because something in you actually shifted.

> "In the third degree mortal mind disappears, and man as God's reflection appears... 'The last shall be first, and the first last.'" — Science and Health, p. 115

Discuss (pairs): "Think of a real blow-up — yours or one you watched happen. Can you find all three stages in it, even if the third one only lasted a minute?"

### Beat 4 (~8 min) — Private reflection

Give everyone paper. Not shared aloud unless they choose to.

Prompt: *"Think of a real moment this week where you were somewhere on this journey — first, second, or third degree. What actually happened? Is there shame in having been in the first or second degree, or is that just... normal?"*

Give real silence for this — 4–5 minutes.

### Beat 5 (~6 min) — Where the two ideas meet

"The same seven names are what you reach for at every stage of the journey — not a different name for each stage, but all seven, practiced, wherever you are. Reaching for Truth in a first-degree moment, or Love in a second-degree one, is part of how the third degree actually arrives."

Discuss: "Which one or two of the seven names might help most in the exact kind of moment you wrote about?"

### Closing

> "In the third degree mortal mind disappears, and man as God's reflection appears... 'The last shall be first, and the first last.'" — Science and Health, p. 115

"That's a promise the journey reorders itself — not a demand to arrive today."

## What you'll need

- Paper and pens for each participant
- A whiteboard, poster paper, or shared doc for the three stages in Beat 3

## If it's not going well

This age carries real developmental risk around shame, self-worth, and social comparison, and the private reflection in Beat 4 can surface genuine distress, not just useful discomfort. If a student's writing, body language, or comments suggest something beyond typical middle-school pressure, don't try to resolve it in class — follow the site's mentor safety guidelines (For Mentors page) and loop in the appropriate adult per your church's child-safety policy.

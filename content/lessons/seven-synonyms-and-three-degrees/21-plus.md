## What you should know

Two ideas anchor this lesson, and both are genuinely new territory for most people encountering Christian Science for the first time.

The first: Christian Science doesn't use one word for God. It uses seven — Mind, Spirit, Soul, Principle, Life, Truth, Love.

> "God is incorporeal, divine, supreme, infinite Mind, Spirit, Soul, Principle, Life, Truth, Love." — Science and Health, p. 465

Not seven gods, and not seven attributes tacked onto one God the way a person might have many qualities. Each name is a complete, full way of understanding the same one God — the way a single person might be fully described as a parent, a friend, and a professional, without being three different people. Someone new to this idea often asks: why seven? isn't one name enough? The honest answer is that no single word — not even "God" — carries the full weight of what's being pointed at. Seven names offer seven doors into the same room.

The second idea: Christian Science describes the human journey toward understanding God — and toward healing — as moving through three degrees.

> First Degree: Depravity. Physical. Evil beliefs, passions and appetites, fear, depraved will, self-justification, pride, envy, deceit, hatred, revenge, sin, sickness, disease, death.
> Second Degree: Evil beliefs disappearing. Moral. Humanity, honesty, affection, compassion, hope, faith, meekness, temperance.
> Third Degree: Understanding. Spiritual. Wisdom, purity, spiritual understanding, spiritual power, love, health, holiness.
> — Science and Health, p. 115

Stripped of the 1875 vocabulary, in plain terms: the first degree is what it feels like to be overwhelmed, underwater, tempted, out of control. The second degree is beginning to see the light — more at peace with yourself. The third degree is that smile of contentment. Mary Baker Eddy, the discoverer and founder of Christian Science, offered this as one scientific, practical way of understanding the journey toward healing and harmony — not the only way to see it, but a helpful one. Nobody lives permanently in one degree. Most people move through all three in a single day.

## What we're assuming

- Nobody in the room has to have any prior exposure to Christian Science, the Bible, or any faith tradition at all. Both ideas should make sense on their own terms.
- Some participants will arrive with a very different picture of "God" already in place — one name, one gender, one temperament. That's not a problem to correct; it's the starting point this lesson gently expands from.
- "Three degrees" can sound like a ranking of who's a better or worse person. It isn't. Nobody is being graded — this is a map of a shared, ordinary, human experience, not a scoreboard.
- These are just two ideas among many in Christian Science. This lesson isn't asking anyone to adopt a whole belief system — just to sit with two ways of seeing that might be useful.

## Learning goals

1. A working answer to "why does Christian Science use seven names for God instead of one?"
2. The ability to describe, in their own words, what the physical, moral, and spiritual degrees actually feel like — without needing the 1875 vocabulary to do it.
3. A sense of how the seven names relate to each other (not as a flat list, but through three natural centers: being — Mind, Spirit, Soul; active — Life, Truth, Love; governing — Principle, Love).
4. One real, current moment in their own week they can place somewhere on the three-degree journey — honestly, without judgment.

## How to hold the room

- Lead with curiosity, not correction. This isn't "here's what you got wrong about God" — it's "here's another way of seeing this, notice what lands."
- If someone pushes back — "isn't that polytheism," "isn't that just words" — that's a great question, not a threat. Sit with it together rather than defending the framework.
- Keep the three degrees at the level of pattern and shared human experience, not confession. If someone volunteers something personal and specific, follow their lead; don't chase it.
- This is explicitly framed as one way of seeing it, not a rule. Say that out loud if the room needs to hear it — some people relax the moment they know they're not being asked to sign onto anything.

## The lesson flow

### Beat 1 (~10 min) — One God, seven doors

Show the seven-names circle if you have it. Before naming any of the seven, ask the group: if you had to describe someone you love completely — not just one thing about them, but everything — could you do it in one word? Let that sit for a moment, then introduce the seven names and read the p. 465 quote aloud.

Discuss: Which of the seven names is most familiar to you already — and which is the most surprising to see on this list? If someone said "just call it God and leave it at that" — what do you think gets lost?

### Beat 2 (~10–15 min) — How the seven relate

Point out the three natural centers: Mind, Spirit, Soul (who this is — consciousness, substance, identity), Life, Truth, Love (what it does for you — meets fear, lies, and loneliness), and Principle, Love (firm and kind at once). Note that Love sits at the intersection — present in two centers.

Discuss: Pick one of the three centers. Where do you already see that quality show up in your own life, even outside a religious context? Principle usually sounds cold or rule-bound. What changes when it's paired with Love?

### Beat 3 (~15 min) — The honest seeker's journey

Read the plain-language version first — overwhelmed/underwater/tempted; beginning to see the light; that smile of contentment — before the 1875 vocabulary. This is a journey, left to right, not a ranking of good people versus bad people.

Discuss: Without naming anything private, what would a first-degree moment feel like in an ordinary week — traffic, a hard email, a fight with someone you love? What's the difference between the second degree ("evil beliefs disappearing," moral effort) and the third ("understanding," where it stops being effort)? Has anyone felt that shift, even briefly?

### Beat 4 (~10–15 min) — Where the two ideas meet

Bring both ideas together: the same seven names are what a person leans into at every point along the three-degree journey — not a different name assigned to each degree, but all seven, practiced, at every waypoint.

Discuss: Think of a real moment this week that felt like the first or second degree. Which one or two of the seven names — Mind, Spirit, Soul, Principle, Life, Truth, Love — might have helped, if you'd reached for it in the moment? Is there shame in mostly living in the first or second degree? Why or why not?

### Beat 5 (~10 min) — The experiment

Close with an invitation, not an assignment: this week, notice one ordinary first- or second-degree moment as it's happening, and consciously reach for one of the seven names instead of the usual reflex. Name that this takes practice, not perfection.

> "In the third degree mortal mind disappears, and man as God's image appears... so that God and His idea may be to us what divinity really is and must of necessity be, — all-inclusive." — Science and Health, p. 115

Discuss: Which of the seven names do you want to practice reaching for first? What would you notice change if you did?

## What you'll need

- Paper and pens if running this live, for the Beat 4 personal-mapping discussion
- A printed or projected version of the seven-names circle and the three-degrees diagram — ["The Sevenfold Name of God"](/assets/materials/seven-synonyms-and-three-degrees/seven-synonyms-diagram.html) — if available, though the lesson works fine without it

## If it's not going well

If the conversation opens into real disclosure — grief, a diagnosis, a loss, a genuine safety concern — pause the lesson itself and follow the site's mentor safety guidelines (For Mentors page). If someone gets quiet or visibly moved naming where they actually are on the three-degree journey, that's not a sign to move faster; slow down and let them lead how much they share.

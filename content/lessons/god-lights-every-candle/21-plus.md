## What you should know

Adults carry this one quietly: a spouse or partner who doesn't share their faith, in-laws with a different tradition, adult children who've chosen another path or none, a coworker or neighbor whose views are different, the family table at the holidays. The pressure rarely looks like conversion. It looks like worry, disappointment, or an urge to make one more point. This lesson offers one steadying idea (each person's relationship with God is their own) and one concrete test, then asks adults to locate it in a real relationship. Expect a mix of relief and real disagreement. Nothing to prepare beyond paper.

## What we're assuming

- The room holds a real range of faith, doubt, and none, and possibly a lot of history with organized religion, some of it painful. Don't assume anyone feels warmly toward faith, or that shared belief is a given.
- Some participants carry grief or worry about someone they love, and some have been on the receiving end of someone else's pull. No one has to share which.
- Nobody has to speak. Private reflection is a complete use of this lesson.

## Learning goals

1. Leaves with a sense of relief at not being responsible for anyone else's relationship with God, and a clearer sense of what they *are* free to offer.
2. Can tell, in their own life, the difference between sharing and steering.
3. Has a repeatable test and a clear safety line to carry into one real relationship.
4. Feels free to hold the tension between conviction and respect without resolving it.

## How to hold the room

- Open with curiosity. You aren't there to model "the right" way to handle a difference in belief.
- Welcome disagreement, including "this is too soft" and "this is too accommodating." Both are real.
- Never ask anyone to explain or defend a faith, or its absence. Nobody speaks for anybody.
- Offer a way to pass on any part, and a private-reflection option before any sharing.
- You're not there to resolve the paradox neatly by the end of the hour. If the room leaves still thinking, that's the lesson doing its work.

## The lesson flow

### Beat 1 (~7 min) — Who comes to mind

Ask the room to bring one person to mind, silently, whose way of relating to God (or not) differs from their own, and with whom it matters: a spouse or partner, a parent or in-law, an adult child, a close friend, a colleague. Then ask, still privately: *Where do I feel something pressing here? Worry? Disappointment? A need for them to understand?* Invite a few volunteers to name just the category ("my adult son," "my in-laws," "my husband") if they like. No details required.

### Beat 2 (~10 min) — What the texts say, and what they leave open

Read Psalms 18:28 ("For thou wilt light my candle") and Isaiah 45:5 ("I girded thee, though thou hast not known me"). Let it settle: God was with the person before they knew it, and the one who lights the candle is God. Then read these together:

> "Christians do not continue to labor and pray, expecting because of another's goodness, suffering, and triumph, that they shall reach his harmony and reward." — Science and Health, Mary Baker Eddy, p. 21:6–8

> "...all are privileged to work out their own salvation according to their light, and... our motto should be the Master's counsel, 'Judge not, that ye be not judged.'" — Science and Health, Mary Baker Eddy, p. 443:9–13

> "Let us be faithful in pointing the way through Christ, as we understand it, but let us also be careful always to 'judge righteous judgment,' and never to condemn rashly." — Science and Health, Mary Baker Eddy, p. 444:16–19

Say plainly what the passages hold and what they don't. They hold that each person works out their own salvation with God, that charity toward differing forms of religion is asked for, and that we should be faithful to what we understand. They do not say all paths are the same, and this lesson doesn't attribute that to Eddy. "Different, not wrong" is a posture a facilitator and participant can choose, resting on her call to charity and "judge not."

Ask: "Where do these pull in different directions for you?" Let it sit.

### Beat 3 (~14 min) — Small groups: the real cost of needing agreement

In groups of three or four (or pairs), take one prompt and talk it through. These are real, not rhetorical:

- What has it cost you, or someone close to you, when you needed them to arrive where you did?
- When you've been on the receiving end of someone's pull, what helped, and what made it worse?
- With a spouse or partner, or with your children, where is the line between living your faith openly and pressing it?
- What would it look like to share what you love in this relationship and be at peace however it's received?

Invite a few to bring a thought back to the whole room, never a detail that's someone else's story.

### Beat 4 (~12 min) — A test, a line, and a locate

Teach the test, simply: **"Do I need them to respond a certain way for this to count as a good conversation?"** If yes, I've slid from sharing into steering. If I can say what I love, ask real questions, and be at peace however they respond, I'm sharing.

Name the safety line directly. This idea never means "not my problem." You can't control anyone's beliefs, and you can always bring in help, a caring adult, a professional, or emergency services, when someone you love is unsafe. That isn't pushing a belief; it's love.

Close with a private locate. Take the person from Beat 1: "What's one thing I could do or say this week that shares what I love and lets go of needing a response? What's one thing I could stop doing?" Write it down or hold it. Hand out the wallet card.

## If it's not going well

Silence is fine; let it sit. Pushback ("some beliefs do harm," or "isn't this just relativism?") is welcome and deserves a real answer: this lesson doesn't say every belief is equally true or harmless. It says your relationship with God isn't yours to run for someone else, and safety is never up for negotiation. A strong emotional reaction (grief over an estranged family member, a faith community that rejected someone) calls for a pause, grounding the room, and a private check-in; offer space to step out. A disclosure of real harm (abuse, danger, or someone at risk) is not something to work through in the room. Stop facilitating, respond as a caring adult, and follow the site's mentor safety guidelines along with your own local mandatory-reporting obligations.

# Lesson Core Model: "When Someone Feels Like the Enemy"

*One theological core, four age-specific deliveries (this topic uses four of the five bands — see below). Everything in this document is shared scaffolding — it does not get taught directly to any age group. It's the thing every age version is checked against, so the different lessons stay recognizably the same truth even though the room, the words, and the activities look nothing alike from one band to the next.*

Note on bands: this lesson is built for 8-11, 11-14, 14-21, and 21+. No 5-8 version exists. The core move of this lesson — separating *loving* someone from *liking*, *excusing*, *staying close to*, or *trusting* them — requires holding several relationships to a person in mind at once and telling them apart, a kind of relational reasoning that isn't reliably in place before about age eight. Younger children are better served by something much simpler and completely concrete: if someone is mean to you or hurts you, that's not about how much you love them, and you always tell a trusted adult. That message lives inside every band below, including 8-11, but for 5-8 it would need to stand alone as its own short lesson rather than be built out into this fuller framework — see the note at the end of this document.

## Central claim

Jesus's teaching was "love your enemies" — not "there are no enemies." He never asked anyone to pretend a real adversary wasn't real. He asked for something harder and more honest: see the conflict clearly, and respond with love anyway. Love, in that teaching, is a much narrower and more achievable thing than it sounds — it never requires liking someone, excusing what they did, staying physically or emotionally close to them, or trusting them again.

## The picture being replaced

Two opposite, equally distorting pictures. The first: "if I really loved like I'm supposed to, I wouldn't have any enemies, or wouldn't still feel hurt by this person" — a spiritual-bypass move that shames a person for having an honest, unresolved feeling. The second, more dangerous one: "love your enemies" twisted into "so I have to stay, keep the relationship, and endure being treated badly, or I'm failing at love." Both pictures collapse love into something it was never asked to be — either a feeling you're not allowed to have (an enemy), or a posture you're required to hold near someone unsafe.

## The picture being offered

You can name someone as currently acting like an adversary — a bully, a family member, an ex-friend, a difficult coworker, someone online — without that being a personal or spiritual failure. It's honesty, not weakness. And separately, real and steady: love doesn't require liking someone, excusing what happened, staying close to them, or trusting them again. A person can love someone, mean it fully, and still keep real distance and real boundaries. Loving someone is never a reason to stay somewhere unsafe.

## Anchor citations

Used at whatever depth and reading level fits the age band — younger bands may only ever hear these paraphrased or read aloud without close discussion, and that's enough.

- "But I say unto you, Love your enemies, bless them that curse you, do good to them that hate you, and pray for them which despitefully use you, and persecute you." — Matthew 5:44, KJV
- "But love ye your enemies, and do good, and lend, hoping for nothing again... Be ye therefore merciful, as your Father also is merciful." — Luke 6:35, 6:36, KJV
- "Therefore if thine enemy hunger, feed him; if he thirst, give him drink... Be not overcome of evil, but overcome evil with good." — Romans 12:20, 21, KJV (a concrete, actionable picture of what "loving" an adversary can look like in practice, useful for grounding older bands)

## Learning objective family

Each age band aims at a version of the same four objectives — the verbs and depth shift, the target doesn't:

1. **Notice** that naming someone as currently acting like an adversary, or naming a relationship as hard, is honesty — not a spiritual or personal failure.
2. **Feel**, not just understand intellectually, the relief of separating love from liking, excusing, staying close, and trusting — that love doesn't secretly require all four.
3. **Carry** a repeatable move: sorting out what love actually asks of them in a hard relationship from what it doesn't ask of them at all.
4. **Locate** one real, current strained relationship in their own life where this applies — without ever being required to name who it is.

## The bridge, by age band

This is the piece that's genuinely different per age, and it's the hinge the whole lesson turns on.

| Age band | What the core tension looks like concretely | Bridge used |
|---|---|---|
| 8-11 | A kid at school who teases them, excludes them, or is a repeat source of friction | One simple dial, not four: you can be **kind** to someone without having to be their **close friend**. Being kind and being close aren't the same thing. |
| 11-14 | Real, current peer conflict — a bully, an ex-friend, social exclusion, sometimes family friction — held with enough self-consciousness that it needs a private, non-shared way in | The "two dials" and the full four-way sort: loving, liking, excusing, staying close, trusting are five different things, and love doesn't require the other four |
| 14-21 (native band) | A bully, a family member, an ex-friend, someone online — often ongoing and unresolved | Same four-way sort, framed as "what love actually requires (and doesn't)," plus the "Two Dials" handout (love and closeness graphed as separate dials) |
| 21+ | A difficult coworker, an estranged or strained family member, a friendship that ended badly — often with real professional, financial, or family-system consequences attached to how it's handled | Same four-way sort, re-anchored in adult relationships and adult-scale consequences (a work boundary, a limited-contact decision, declining to rebuild trust that hasn't been re-earned) |

## What does NOT get inherited across bands

- **The full four-way sort (loving / liking / excusing / staying close / trusting) and the "Two Dials" handout.** These stay out of 8-11 entirely. Sorting five overlapping relational categories against each other is a level of abstraction that asks a concrete-operational thinker to reason about relationships *between* relationships — that's not reliably available before roughly age eleven. 8-11 gets one dial, not five: kind vs. close friend. That's the whole lesson at that age, and it's enough.
- **Private journaling before sharing.** This beat starts at 11-14, matching this site's established pattern — that's the age where self-consciousness about being watched while processing something vulnerable becomes intense enough to need a private on-ramp. 8-11 shares more freely in simple pairs without needing that on-ramp; by 14-21 and 21+ it's available but not load-bearing, since older participants are generally comfortable choosing for themselves whether to share.
- **The vocabulary "enemy," "adversary," "estrangement," and "retaliation."** These words are reserved for 11-14 and up. 8-11 uses "someone who's being mean" instead. The loaded, somewhat dramatic weight of "enemy" language isn't necessary to teach the concrete lesson at that age, and can make an ordinary school conflict feel bigger and scarier than it needs to.
- **Adult-role content** — a difficult coworker, workplace boundaries, an estranged parent or in-law, the professional or financial stakes of how an adult conflict is handled. This stays in the 21+ version only. It has no honest analogue in a child's or teen's daily life, and importing it would make the lesson feel like it's talking past the room instead of to it.
- **Open-ended "you don't have to be ready to forgive" framing.** This assumes enough lived relational history to know what "not being ready yet" even means, and appears starting at 11-14. 8-11 doesn't need it and isn't given it — that band gets a much simpler, unconditional message instead: if someone keeps hurting you, telling a trusted adult is not "not loving them enough." It's just what you do to stay safe.

## Note on the missing 5-8 band

No 5-8 version of this lesson was built. The loving-without-staying-close-or-trusting distinction genuinely requires relational boundary-setting capacity a 5-8 year old doesn't reliably have yet — building a scaled-down version of this specific lesson for that age would either be so simplified it taught nothing, or would ask the room for reasoning it can't yet do. This age is better served by a short, direct, standalone safety message rather than a downward adaptation of this lesson: if someone is mean to you or hurts you, always tell a grown-up you trust. That message doesn't need the "love your enemies" theology to work at age five, six, or seven — it can and should stand on its own.

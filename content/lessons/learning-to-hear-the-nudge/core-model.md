# Lesson Core Model: "Learning to Hear the Nudge"

*One theological core, delivery layers for each age band it genuinely fits. Everything in this document is shared scaffolding — it does not get taught directly to any age group. It's the thing every age version is checked against, so the bands stay recognizably the same truth even though the room, the language, and the examples look nothing alike.*

## Why a "core + layers" model instead of one lesson translated by reading level

Age-grading this content isn't a simplification exercise. A 9-year-old and a 25-year-old don't need the same idea in smaller words — they need it delivered through a bridge suited to how they actually think at that age. Concrete-operational thinking (roughly ages 5-11) processes this best through simple, embodied contrasts — a quiet voice and a loud worried voice — not through comparing abstract belief systems. Abstract thought is emerging by 11-14 and largely consolidated by the mid-teens, which is when a lesson can start asking someone to hold two internal states side by side, compare them, and name them across traditions. Adults bring decision history (and often self-blame about past decisions) that no younger band carries, which changes what the room needs from the facilitator even when the underlying framework is identical.

Practically: write this section once. Each age-band lesson document should open with a one-line pointer back here, then never re-litigate the theology — only the delivery changes.

## Central claim

Everyone carries the capacity to sense calm, certain inner guidance — what Christian Science calls an "angel message," and what other traditions know as a still small voice, conscience, intuition, or gut feeling. Telling that guidance apart from anxious, urgent noise is a learnable practice available to anyone, not a rare gift reserved for a spiritually gifted few.

## The picture being replaced

Two pictures, both harmful in different ways: (1) inner guidance as a mystical faculty only certain people have, leaving most people concluding they simply don't get it; and (2) the quietly punishing idea that any bad outcome means the person "didn't listen well enough" — turning a resource meant to comfort and steady a person into one more way to blame themselves after the fact.

## The picture being offered

Everyone already has moments of feeling an inner "wait," "not yet," or "go this way," whether or not they've ever named it. The practice is noticing these moments, not manufacturing something new. Genuine guidance has a recognizable feel — calm, quiet, certain, unhurried, uninterested in controlling the outcome. Anxious pressure feels rushed, forceful, urgent, and needs to be right and in control right now. Practicing this distinction on small, low-stakes decisions builds the capacity to recognize it when the stakes are higher — the goal is the noticing itself, not a perfect record of having followed it.

## Anchor citations (used at whatever depth fits the age band)

- "And after the earthquake a fire; but the LORD was not in the fire: and after the fire a still small voice." — 1 Kings 19:12, KJV
- "And thine ears shall hear a word behind thee, saying, This is the way, walk ye in it, when ye turn to the right hand, and when ye turn to the left." — Isaiah 30:21, KJV
- "ANGELS. God's thoughts passing to man; spiritual intuitions, pure and perfect; the inspiration of goodness, purity, and immortality, counteracting all evil, sensuality, and mortality." — Science and Health, Mary Baker Eddy, p. 581
- "My sheep hear my voice, and I know them, and they follow me." — John 10:27, KJV

## Learning objective family

Each age band aims at a version of the same four objectives — the verbs and depth shift, the target doesn't:

1. **Notice** the felt difference between a calm, certain inner prompt and a rushed, anxious urge.
2. **Feel**, not just understand intellectually, that listening for guidance is an ordinary, learnable practice available to everyone — not a rare gift, and not a test you can fail your way out of being lovable or safe.
3. **Carry** one repeatable question — some version of "is this calm and quiet, or rushed and loud?" — into a real decision.
4. **Locate** one real moment, recent or upcoming, sized to their actual life, where they can practice noticing which voice is speaking.

## The bridge, by age band

| Age band | What the core tension looks like concretely at this age | Bridge used |
|---|---|---|
| 8-11 | Recess games, trying something new, a friend's idea that feels off | "Quiet voice vs. loud worried voice" — two simple, almost cartoon-concrete internal voices, no comparison of traditions |
| 11-14 | Group chats, peer pressure, going along with excluding someone, tryouts, first real secrets kept from parents | Same calm/certain vs. rushed/urgent framework as the teen version, delivered with a private-journaling step before any sharing |
| 14-21 (native) | Everyday choices, first real independent decisions, first choices made away from parents | Direct and unmetaphored: naming the felt difference between guidance and pressure, across traditions and personal vocabularies |
| 21+ | Career moves, relationship decisions, financial or big-purchase choices, when to stay and when to leave | Same framework applied to decisions with real, adult-scale consequences, less certainty about outcomes, and often real regret about past ones |

*(No 5-8 version — see below.)*

## What does NOT get inherited across bands

- **A 5-8 band does not exist for this lesson, by design.** Distinguishing two internal states from each other — calm certainty vs. anxious urgency — requires a level of self-observation (noticing your own noticing) that concrete-operational 5-8 year olds generally haven't developed yet. Simplifying the vocabulary further wouldn't fix this; the underlying skill isn't there yet. A diluted version would produce confusion or rote mimicry, not real understanding, so none was built.
- **The "what's your word for this, across traditions" comparative discussion** (native to the 14-21 version) stays 14+ only. It asks someone to hold and compare abstract belief systems side by side, which is exactly the kind of reasoning that's still consolidating in middle school and isn't reliably present yet in upper elementary.
- **The formal private-journaling-before-sharing beat structure** is 11-14 and up only. Below age 11, kids don't yet reliably use private writing as a processing tool the way early adolescents do; simple out-loud pair discussion does the same protective work at that age instead.
- **Adult-scale stakes** — career, relationships, money, whether to leave a job, a marriage, or a city — are 21+ only. These are not diluted into a "kid version" of a big decision; younger bands stay with decisions that are actually theirs to make at their age.
- **The clinical phrase "anxious pressure"** itself stays out of the 8-11 version, replaced by "loud worried voice" — same idea, concrete words a 9-year-old already uses.
- **The self-blame trap discussion** (naming and setting down a past decision someone has blamed themselves for) is 21+ only. Younger bands haven't accumulated enough decision history for this to be a live issue yet; introducing it earlier would manufacture a worry that isn't organically present.

## Facilitator-coaching principle

The one piece of language that must survive into every band, worded appropriately for that age: an inner voice telling someone to harm themselves or someone else is never the "nudge" this lesson describes, and a facilitator must never suggest that a bad outcome happened because someone "didn't listen well enough." Below age 14, assume the volunteer teacher needs this coached explicitly in the lesson text, not just implied.

## What you should know
This is close to the original teen version of this lesson because it fits this age almost natively — not making a team, a friend group shifting, or a tryout or invitation not going their way are exactly the disappointments this age is living through right now. What's different is how ready they are to talk about it out loud. Abstract thinking is coming online at this age, so they can genuinely grasp the idea of checking their own role in a situation — but self-worth is also unusually tied to peer belonging right now, which means asking them to reflect on this cold, in front of a group, can produce performance rather than honesty. That's why this version adds a private-journaling beat before any sharing happens — give them the option to be honest with themselves first, out loud second, if at all.

Avoid the phrase "main character energy" here even though the 14-21 version uses it — for this age it can read as dated slang borrowed from older teens rather than land as clear. Use the plain version instead: checking whether you've made yourself the center of something that was never just about you.

## What we're assuming

- This lesson addresses everyday disappointment at this age's real scale — not making a team, a friend group falling through, a tryout or invite that didn't go their way. It's not the right tool for major grief or loss, which deserves its own space, not a reframe.
- Some kids in the room may be dealing with real anxiety or peer-belonging pressure that this topic touches close to. That's expected — hold it with real care, not just as a nice discussion topic.
- Give kids the private-journaling option before asking anyone to share out loud. Forced vulnerability at this age often produces performance, not honesty.

## Learning goals
1. Leaves knowing disappointment when a plan (a team, a friend group, an invite) falls through isn't proof they failed or proof nothing good is happening.
2. Leaves with a concrete way to check whether they've made themselves the center of a situation that was never just about them.
3. Leaves understanding that good things can keep happening without them being the one who makes it happen — and that's not a loss.
4. Leaves having named one real disappointment honestly, in private writing first, without being rushed to feel better about it.

## How to hold the room
- Don't silver-line someone's real disappointment. Let it be sad and still true that something good can also be true — both, not one instead of the other.
- Give the private-journaling beat real, quiet time. Don't fill the silence with talking.
- This lesson is for garden-variety disappointment at this age's scale, not major loss or grief. If something heavier surfaces, don't force this framework onto it — see the safety note below.
- Watch for the kid who goes quieter than usual during the journaling beat — note it, check in one-on-one after.

## The lesson flow
### Beat 1 (~8 min) — Naming a Real Disappointment
Think of a time recently you tried hard for something — a team, a group, an invite, a spot in something — and it didn't go the way you wanted. Let the disappointment be real. Don't rush past it to get to the "lesson" — the disappointment is allowed to just sit there for a minute.

### Beat 2 (~8 min) — Private journaling
Give everyone paper. This part is not shared aloud unless they choose to.

Prompt: *"What did it feel like when that plan didn't go the way you wanted? Don't try to make it sound okay yet — just write down what was actually true about how it felt."*

Give real silence for this — 4–5 minutes of actual quiet, not filled with talking.

### Beat 3 (~10 min) — Checking whether you've made yourself the center of it
Sometimes a plan changing isn't a punishment or proof you failed — it might mean you're needed somewhere else, or protected from something you can't see yet. That's a real possibility worth holding, even if you can't prove it in the moment.

Discuss (pairs or small groups, more comfortable than whole-group at this age): "Has something you didn't want to happen ever turned out to matter for a reason you didn't expect at the time?"

### Beat 4 (~10 min) — The Good Doesn't Need You to Be the Hero of It
The work, the good, the point of what you were trying to do usually keeps happening — with or without your specific presence in it. That's not a demotion. It's actually freeing.

Discuss: "Is there a time something good happened and you weren't the one who made it happen — and that was okay?"

### Beat 5 (~6 min) — Trusting the Mover, Not the Map
You don't have to know exactly why a plan changed to trust that something is still moving. Close with this: disappointment is real, and it doesn't get the final say about whether something good is still unfolding.

## What you'll need
- Paper and pens for each participant — private journaling only works if everyone has their own
- A quiet space or a few minutes of real quiet within the room for Beat 2
- Optional printables: Handout ["Main Character Energy"](/assets/materials/trust-the-mover/main-character-energy.html); Visual ["The Map and the Mover"](/assets/materials/trust-the-mover/the-map-and-the-mover.html)

## If it's not going well
"This happened for a reason" can do real harm if it's pointed at genuine tragedy, grief, or injustice — and this age carries real developmental risk around self-worth and peer belonging, so this lesson can surface genuine distress, not just useful discomfort. If a student's private journaling, body language, or comments suggest something beyond typical disappointment at this age (persistent hopelessness, signs of self-harm, disclosure of harm from someone else, ongoing exclusion), don't try to resolve it with this framework. Follow the site's mentor safety guidelines (For Mentors page), loop in the appropriate adult per your church's child-safety policy, and take it seriously even though it may have arrived quietly, inside a written reflection rather than spoken aloud.

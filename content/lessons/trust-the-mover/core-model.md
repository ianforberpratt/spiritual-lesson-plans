# Lesson Core Model: "Trust the Mover"

*One theological core, five age-specific delivery layers (four built for this lesson, one is the native version this lesson started as). Everything in this document is shared scaffolding — it does not get taught directly to any age group. It's the thing every age version is checked against, so the different bands stay recognizably the same truth even though the room, the story, and the pacing look nothing alike from one end to the other.*

## Central claim

Disappointment is real, and it is not proof of anything — not proof a person failed, not proof they were forgotten, and not proof that good has stopped happening. A plan changing and good still unfolding are not opposites. They can both be true in the same moment, and a person doesn't have to rush past the disappointment to get to the good.

## The picture being replaced

A quiet, often unexamined belief that *my* plan working out is the measure of whether good is real or present — so if the plan falls through, either something is wrong with me, or good simply isn't happening right now. This picture makes a person's own outcome the whole scoreboard, and makes disappointment feel like evidence rather than just a feeling.

## The picture being offered

Good — Life, Love, whatever a person's own tradition names it — keeps moving and keeps meeting real need, whether or not any one person's specific plan is the vehicle for it. Not being the one who makes the good thing happen isn't a demotion or a loss. Trusting that something good is still in motion doesn't require knowing why a plan changed, and it doesn't require pretending the disappointment isn't real.

## Anchor citations (used at whatever reading level fits the age band)

- "Trust in the Lord with all thine heart; and lean not unto thine own understanding. In all thy ways acknowledge him, and he shall direct thy paths." — Proverbs 3:5-6, KJV
- "And we know that all things work together for good to them that love God." — Romans 8:28, KJV
- "For my thoughts are not your thoughts, neither are your ways my ways, saith the LORD." — Isaiah 55:8, KJV
- "Divine Love always has met and always will meet every human need." — *Science and Health*, Mary Baker Eddy, p. 494:10

## Learning objective family

Each age band aims at a version of the same four objectives — the language and depth shift, the target doesn't:

1. **Notice** the difference between "my plan didn't work, so I failed" and "my plan changed, and that's a different thing."
2. **Feel**, not just understand intellectually, that being disappointed and trusting that good is still unfolding can both be true at the same time — one doesn't cancel the other.
3. **Carry** a simple, repeatable phrase or question — some version of "the plan changed; the good didn't stop" — into a real moment of disappointment this week.
4. **Locate** one real, honestly-named disappointment in their own life, without rushing to explain it away or feel better about it too fast.

## The bridge, by age band

This is the piece that changes most from band to band, and it's the hinge the whole lesson turns on. Get the bridge wrong and the lesson doesn't land, no matter how sound the rest of it is.

| Age band | What the core tension looks like concretely | Bridge used |
|---|---|---|
| 5-8 | A rained-out picnic, a birthday party that has to move indoors | A simple story where the plan changes and something good happens anyway — no abstract language, just "that's a real bummer, AND good things are still happening too" |
| 8-11 | Not making a team, a canceled sleepover, a rained-out game | A short relatable story plus simple pair discussion: has a plan ever changed on you, and something good happened anyway that you didn't expect? |
| 11-14 | Not making a team, a friend group shifting or falling through, a tryout or invite that didn't go their way | The original 14-21 structure and themes, kept nearly intact (this age lives these exact disappointments), with a private-journaling-before-sharing beat added and the phrase "main character energy" replaced with plain language |
| 14-21 (native) | A plan falling through, a chance missed, something worked for that didn't land | "Trust the Mover, Not the Map" — checking whether you've made yourself the center of something that was never just about you |
| 21+ | A job that fell through, a plan for a family or a move that changed, a project that didn't land | Same framework as 14-21, rescaled to adult stakes — with the caution against applying this to major grief or loss held even more firmly, given what's actually at risk at this age |

## What does NOT get inherited across bands

- **The explicit "checking your main character energy" language.** This stays in the 14-21 native version, where it reads as native slang for that age. The 11-14 version keeps the same idea but uses plain language ("checking whether you've made yourself the center of something that was never just about you") because the phrase risks landing as dated or confusing rather than clarifying for that age. Bands younger than 11-14 don't get this idea in any language, abstract or plain — they don't yet have the self-reflective distance to examine their own role in a situation this way; they just get "plans change, and good things keep happening too."
- **Open-ended, abstract discussion questions.** These belong to 11-14 and up. 5-8 gets simple, concrete, one-at-a-time asks with no pressure to answer. 8-11 gets structured, simple pair discussion with a single concrete prompt — not open reflection.
- **The private-journaling-before-sharing beat.** This is specific to 11-14 and older, matching the established pattern for that band elsewhere on the site. Younger bands don't get a written private-reflection beat — they process this through story, drawing, and simple spoken asks, not private writing.
- **Adult-stakes examples** (a job offer falling through, a family or fertility plan changing, a move that fell apart). These stay in the 21+ version only. Teens use school and social examples; middle schoolers use team and friendship examples; younger kids use picnic- and party-scale examples. No band gets an example whose stakes are bigger than what that age actually carries.
- **"This happened for a reason" applied to real loss, grief, or trauma.** This caution is not unique to one band — it holds at every age, named explicitly in each lesson's closing safety note — but it matters most sharply at 21+, where the disappointment being described can sit right next to something that actually is major loss (a job loss that threatens someone's ability to pay rent, a family plan that changed because of a miscarriage or infertility). No band's version of this lesson should ever be used to explain away someone's real grief or suggest they should be looking for a silver lining in something seriously bad.

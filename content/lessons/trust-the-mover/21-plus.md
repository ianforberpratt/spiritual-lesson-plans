## What you should know
Adults carry this exact same tension — a plan that fell through and the disappointment of watching it not happen — but the plans are bigger and the stakes are realer: a job that didn't come through, a promotion that went to someone else, a plan for starting or growing a family that changed, a move that fell apart, a relationship or living situation that didn't turn out the way it was pictured. The framework is the same as the teen version. What has to be handled with more care is the line between "a real, everyday disappointment" and "a genuine loss" — because at this age, those two things can sit right next to each other in the same story. A job falling through is disappointment. A job loss that threatens someone's ability to pay rent is closer to a crisis. A family plan changing because of a scheduling conflict is disappointment. A family plan changing because of a miscarriage or infertility is grief. Know which one is in front of you before you use this lesson.

## What we're assuming

This lesson addresses everyday adult-scale disappointment — a job that didn't come through, a plan for a family or a move that changed, a project or relationship that didn't land the way it was hoped. It is not the right tool for major grief, loss, or trauma, which deserve their own space, not a reframe — and that caution matters more here than at any younger age, because the disappointments adults name out loud can be genuinely high-stakes even when they're not catastrophic.

## Learning goals
1. Leaves knowing disappointment when a job, a family plan, or a life plan falls through isn't proof they failed, or proof nothing good is happening.
2. Leaves with a concrete way to check whether they've made themselves the center of a situation that was never just about them.
3. Leaves understanding that good things — for their family, their work, their community — can keep happening without them being the one who makes it happen, and that's not a loss of significance.
4. Leaves having named one real, adult-scale disappointment honestly, without being rushed to feel better about it or to reframe it before they're ready.

## How to hold the room
- Don't silver-line someone's real disappointment, especially one with real financial, relational, or family weight behind it. Let it be genuinely hard and still true that something good can also be true — both, not one instead of the other.
- Adults will sometimes minimize their own disappointment ("it's not that big a deal, other people have it worse") before you even get the chance to. Don't rush to agree with that minimizing — let them have the size of the feeling they actually have.
- This lesson is for real-sized disappointment, not major loss or grief. If something heavier surfaces — a genuine loss, a health crisis, a relationship ending, financial crisis — don't force this framework onto it. See the safety note below.

## The lesson flow
### Beat 1 (~10 min) — Naming a Real Disappointment
Think of a time — recently, or one that's still sitting with you — where you worked hard, planned, and prayed for something in your adult life (a job, a promotion, a plan for your family, a move, a relationship) and it didn't happen the way you pictured it. Let the disappointment be real. Don't rush past it to get to the "lesson" — it's allowed to just sit there for a minute, even in a room of adults who are used to moving past things quickly.

### Beat 2 (~12–15 min) — Checking Whether You've Made Yourself the Center of It
Sometimes a plan changing isn't a punishment or proof you failed — it might mean you're needed somewhere else, or protected from something you can't see yet. That's a real possibility worth holding, even if you can't prove it in the moment, and even when the stakes are real (a career, a family's future, a home).

Discuss: Has something you didn't want to happen — a job you didn't get, a plan that fell through — ever turned out to matter for a reason you didn't fully understand until later?

### Beat 3 (~12–15 min) — The Good Doesn't Need You to Be the Hero of It
The work, the good, the point of what you were trying to build usually keeps happening — with or without your specific presence in it, and with or without it happening on your timeline. That's not a demotion of your effort or your importance. It's actually freeing, especially for adults used to carrying the weight of making things happen.

Discuss: Is there a time something good happened in your life, your family, or your work, and you weren't the one who made it happen — and that was genuinely okay?

### Beat 4 (~10 min) — Trusting the Mover, Not the Map
You don't have to know exactly why a plan changed to trust that something is still moving. Close with this: disappointment is real, and even at the scale adult life brings, it doesn't get the final say about whether something good is still unfolding.

## If it's not going well
"This happened for a reason" can do real harm if it's pointed at genuine tragedy, grief, injustice, or crisis — and this risk is highest at this age, because adult disappointments can carry real stakes (income, family, health, housing) that make a too-quick reframe land as dismissive rather than comforting. Never use this lesson to explain away someone's real loss — a job loss that's created a financial crisis, a miscarriage, a divorce, a death, a diagnosis — or suggest they should be looking for a silver lining in something seriously bad. If real grief, trauma, or crisis surfaces, this isn't the right tool: slow down, listen, don't reframe, and follow the site's mentor safety guidelines (For Mentors page), which include when and how to point someone toward pastoral care, counseling, or other professional support beyond what this lesson format can hold.

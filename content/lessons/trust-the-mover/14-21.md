## What you should know
Nothing required. If it helps to know where this came from: sometimes you plan, work, and pray for something, and it still doesn't happen the way you pictured it. This lesson isn't about pretending that's not disappointing. It's about what to do with real disappointment once you've let it be real.
## What we're assuming
This lesson addresses everyday disappointment — a plan falling through, a chance missed, something you worked for not landing. It's not the right tool for major grief or loss, which deserves its own space, not a reframe. The framework assumes your group's disappointment is real-sized, not catastrophic.

## Learning goals
- Leaves knowing disappointment when a plan falls through isn't proof they failed, or proof nothing good is happening.
- Leaves with a concrete way to check whether they've made themselves the center of a situation that was never just about them.
- Leaves understanding that good things can keep happening without them being the one who makes it happen — and that's not a loss.
- Leaves having named one real disappointment honestly, without being rushed to feel better about it.

## How to hold the room
- Don't silver-line someone's real disappointment. Let it be sad and still true that something good can also be true — both, not one instead of the other.
- This lesson is for garden-variety disappointment, not major loss or grief. If something heavier surfaces, don't force this framework onto it — see the safety note below.

## The lesson flow
### Beat 1 (~10 min) — Naming a Real Disappointment
Think of a time recently you tried hard for something and it didn't go the way you wanted. Let the disappointment be real. Don't rush past it to get to the "lesson" — the disappointment is allowed to just sit there for a minute.
### Beat 2 (~12–15 min) — Checking Your Main Character Energy
Sometimes a plan changing isn't a punishment or proof you failed — it might mean you're needed somewhere else, or protected from something you can't see yet. That's a real possibility worth holding, even if you can't prove it in the moment.
Discuss: Has something you didn't want to happen ever turned out to matter for a reason you didn't expect at the time?
### Beat 3 (~12–15 min) — The Good Doesn't Need You to Be the Hero of It
The work, the good, the point of what you were trying to do usually keeps happening — with or without your specific presence in it. That's not a demotion. It's actually freeing.
Discuss: Is there a time something good happened and you weren't the one who made it happen — and that was okay?
### Beat 4 (~10 min) — Trusting the Mover, Not the Map
You don't have to know exactly why a plan changed to trust that something is still moving. Close with this: disappointment is real, and it doesn't get the final say about whether something good is still unfolding.

## What you'll need
- Paper and pens — or a shared doc / whiteboard if you're meeting virtually
- Optional printables: Handout ["Main Character Energy"](/assets/materials/trust-the-mover/main-character-energy.html); Visual ["The Map and the Mover"](/assets/materials/trust-the-mover/the-map-and-the-mover.html)

## If it's not going well
"This happened for a reason" can do real harm if it's pointed at genuine tragedy, grief, or injustice. Never use this lesson to explain away someone's real loss or suggest they should be looking for a silver lining in something seriously bad. If real grief or trauma surfaces, this isn't the right tool — slow down, listen, and don't reframe.

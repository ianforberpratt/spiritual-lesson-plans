# Lesson Core Model: "Does God Make My Decisions For Me?"

*One theological core, three age-specific deliveries (this topic uses three of the five possible bands — see below). Everything in this document is shared scaffolding — it does not get taught directly to any age group. It's the thing every age version is checked against, so the different lessons stay recognizably the same truth even though the room, the words, and the stakes look nothing alike from one band to the next.*

Note on bands: this lesson is built for 11-14, 14-21 (native), and 21+ only. 5-8 and 8-11 are not built. The 10-10-10 framework asks a person to imagine how a present choice will feel from a future vantage point they haven't reached yet, and the assumption-checking piece asks them to examine their own reasoning for hidden motives — both are exercises in hypothetical and metacognitive thinking that depend on formal-operational reasoning, which is not reliably online before roughly age 11. Younger children absolutely make real choices and can be coached through them in the moment, but a reusable *framework* for weighing a decision across imagined future selves isn't a fit for concrete-operational thinkers. Rather than force a diluted version onto 5-8 and 8-11, this lesson simply doesn't offer those bands.

## Central claim

Guidance is not a power outside a person that hands down the correct answer between Option A and Option B, the way a vending machine dispenses a snack once you've put in enough coins of faith or patience. Clarity is something a person can actually cultivate — through stillness, honesty about their own motives, and a willingness to test their own reasoning — and cultivated clarity reveals the next right step, not the whole future.

## The picture being replaced

A transactional, vending-machine picture of divine guidance: a power greater than yourself that is supposed to pick the choice for you, if you pray hard enough, wait for the right sign, or are somehow worthy enough to be told. This picture leaves people either paralyzed — waiting indefinitely for a certainty that isn't coming — or anxious afterward that they picked "wrong" and missed some hidden correct answer they were never actually shown.

## The picture being offered

Clarity is a quality of thought a person can develop, not an answer being withheld from them. Getting some distance from a decision, telling the truth about your own motives, and taking one honest next step — without needing to see the whole path — is not a lesser substitute for "real" guidance. It *is* what guidance actually looks like in practice. A person is not carrying a hard decision alone, and they don't need the entire staircase lit up to take the next stair.

## Anchor citations

Used at whatever depth and reading level fits the age band — younger bands may hear only a line or two read aloud without extended discussion, and that's enough.

- "Desire is prayer; and no loss can occur from trusting God with our desires, that they may be moulded and exalted before they take form in words and in deeds." — *Science and Health*, Mary Baker Eddy, p. 1:1
- "I will instruct thee and teach thee in the way which thou shalt go: I will guide thee with mine eye." — Psalm 32:8, KJV
- "And thine ears shall hear a word behind thee, saying, This is the way, walk ye in it, when ye turn to the right hand, and when ye turn to the left." — Isaiah 30:21, KJV
- "Divine Love always has met and always will meet every human need." — *Science and Health*, p. 494:10

## Learning objective family

Each age band aims at a version of the same four objectives — the scale of the decision and the depth of the language shift, the target doesn't:

1. **Notice** the difference between passively waiting for a sign (or quietly stalling) and actively cultivating the clarity a real decision needs.
2. **Feel**, not just understand intellectually, that they are not carrying a hard choice alone and are not being tested by some hidden correct answer they have to guess.
3. **Carry** a repeatable practice for gaining perspective on a decision — a time-distance question — and a habit of honestly checking their own reasoning before choosing.
4. **Locate** one real decision, at whatever scale actually belongs to their life right now, to genuinely apply this to.

## The bridge, by age band

This is the piece that changes most from band to band, and it's the hinge the whole lesson turns on. Get the bridge wrong and the lesson doesn't land, no matter how sound the rest of it is.

| Age band | What the core tension looks like concretely | Bridge used |
|---|---|---|
| 11-14 | Whether to try out for something, how to handle a friend conflict, how to spend limited time between activities — school- and friendship-scale choices with real but contained stakes | 10-10-10 simplified to "how will I feel about this tomorrow, next month, next year?"; assumption-checking simplified to "am I just looking for someone to agree with me, or actually trying to see clearly?" — plus a private-journaling-before-sharing beat |
| 14-21 (native) | Academics, relationships, communication with family, and occasionally weightier choices with real independence attached | The full 10-10-10 framework (10 minutes / 10 months / 10 years) and full assumption-checking language ("confirmation-seeking vs. genuine assumption-testing," self-image protection) |
| 21+ | Career moves and job offers, relationships and marriage, major purchases, family decisions with dependents or aging parents involved | Same full 10-10-10 and assumption-checking framework as 14-21, rescaled to adult stakes, with more attention to sunk cost, financial fear, and the cumulative weight of past decisions |

## What does NOT get inherited across bands

- **The full "confirmation-seeking vs. genuine assumption-testing" vocabulary.** This abstract framing, including terms like "self-image protection," stays in the 14-21 and 21+ versions. The 11-14 version keeps the same underlying idea but converts it to a single plain-language question a middle schooler can actually hold onto and use in the moment.
- **The private-journaling-before-sharing beat.** This is specific to 11-14, matching the established pattern for that band elsewhere on the site — this age needs the option of privacy to be honest at all. It is not required scaffolding at 14-21 or 21+, where participants are generally comfortable choosing for themselves whether to share without being prompted to write first.
- **Adult-stakes examples** (a job offer, a major purchase, a marriage decision, a choice involving dependents or aging parents). These stay in the 21+ version only. The 11-14 version stays at school- and friendship-scale choices; the 14-21 version stays at the academic, relational, and family-communication scale it was originally written for. No band gets an example whose stakes are bigger than what that age actually carries.
- **Any version for 5-8 or 8-11.** As noted above, this isn't a simplify-the-reading-level omission — the framework itself requires imagining a future vantage point and examining one's own motives, which these age bands aren't reliably equipped to do yet. Facilitators working with younger children should coach individual choices in the moment, not attempt to teach this as a reusable framework.

# Lesson Core Model: "Is Regret a Fair and Just Teacher?"

*One theological core, three age-specific deliveries (this topic uses three of the five bands — see below). Everything in this document is shared scaffolding — it does not get taught directly to any age group. It's the thing every age version is checked against, so the different lessons stay recognizably the same truth even though the room, the words, and the activities look nothing alike from one band to the next.*

Note on bands: this lesson is built for 11-14, 14-21, and 21+ only. No 5-8 or 8-11 version exists. Regret, as this lesson treats it — a lingering, self-punishing narrative wrapped around a specific past decision — depends on a kind of reflective hindsight and life history that concrete-operational children (roughly ages 5-11) don't reliably have yet. A 7-year-old can feel bad about something they did five minutes ago, but they don't yet carry the "I keep replaying this and judging myself for it" structure this lesson is built to interrupt. Forcing a version for those bands would either flatten into generic "say sorry and move on" content that isn't this lesson, or reach for abstract self-reflection those ages can't yet produce. Better to leave the gap than fake the fit.

## Central claim

Regret is not a life sentence. Looked at honestly, it can be a fair and just teacher — but only if it's allowed to finish teaching and then be set down, rather than replayed indefinitely as a private, ongoing verdict against the person carrying it.

## The picture being replaced

A courtroom picture of the self, where one bad decision — or one moment of inaction — becomes evidence in a trial that never ends. Under this picture, feeling bad about a mistake isn't enough; the mistake has to be re-tried, re-argued, and re-felt over and over, as if enough suffering will eventually prove the person has "paid" for it. This turns an honest, useful feeling (I did that, and I wish I hadn't) into a permanent identity (I am the kind of person who does that). The sentence never actually ends, because there's no verdict that would satisfy it — there's only more circling.

## The picture being offered

Drawn from the account of a woman brought before a crowd for public condemnation over a mistake: rather than argue she didn't do it, the response reframes who's qualified to judge at all — "let the one without fault among you cast the first stone." No one moves. She's told directly: "I don't condemn you either. Go, and don't do this anymore." Two things happen in the same breath, not one after the other — the mistake isn't minimized or explained away, *and* the sentence is lifted. She's sent forward, not backward. Regret, rightly used, works the same way: it can be looked at honestly, learned from specifically, and then released — not because the past didn't happen, but because reliving it on a loop was never the same thing as learning from it.

## Anchor citations

Used at whatever depth and reading level fits the age band — younger bands may only ever hear these paraphrased or read aloud without extended discussion, and that's enough.

- "He that is without sin among you, let him first cast a stone." — John 8:7, KJV
- "Neither do I condemn thee: go, and sin no more." — John 8:11, KJV
- "Sorrow for wrong-doing is but one step towards reform and the very easiest step. The next and great step required by wisdom is the test of our sincerity, — namely, obedience..." — *Science and Health*, Mary Baker Eddy, p. 6

## Learning objective family

Each age band aims at a version of the same four objectives — the verbs and depth shift, the target doesn't:

1. **Notice** the difference between honestly seeing a mistake clearly and being permanently sentenced to relive it.
2. **Feel**, not just understand intellectually, the relief of hearing directly that they are not condemned — that this is something offered to them now, not something to earn later.
3. **Carry** a repeatable phrase or question they can return to: something like "Am I remembering this, or still punishing myself for it?"
4. **Locate** one real decision in their own life — sized to what they can actually carry — where they can practice learning from it and setting it down, both, not just one.

## The bridge, by age band

This is the piece that's genuinely different per age, and it's the hinge the whole lesson turns on.

| Age band | What the core tension looks like concretely | Bridge used |
|---|---|---|
| 5-8 | Not built. Children this age can feel bad about a specific moment but don't yet carry a repeating, self-judging narrative about a past decision — the core tension doesn't exist yet in a form this lesson could honestly address. | — |
| 8-11 | Not built. Reflective hindsight over one's own past choices is just beginning to emerge; a lesson about "letting go of an old story about yourself" would be reaching for abstraction most kids this age can't yet hold. | — |
| 11-14 | A friend-sized or school-sized regret — something said in anger, a choice about who to hang out with, a moment of dishonesty at school — replayed with new (and shaky) capacity for "what if I'd done it differently" | Remembering vs. still feeling punished — a simplified, warm four-step version, with private journaling before any sharing |
| 14-21 | A high-school-scale decision or non-decision the student still carries, examined with real capacity for abstract self-reflection | The full six-part framework (observe, release shame, name the fear, choose, stay open to revision, find the gratitude), taught close to as-written |
| 21+ | An adult-scale regret — a career choice, a relationship decision, a parenting moment, a financial call — where the stakes and the time carried are both larger | The same full six-part framework, aimed at adult-scale material with more room for complexity and consequence |

## What does NOT get inherited across bands

- **Clinical or heavy language** — "self-prosecution," "shame spiral," "circling" used repeatedly. This stays out of 11-14 entirely. It's accurate language for describing what regret does, but at 11-14 it can read as diagnostic or alarming rather than warm; the same idea is carried instead through plainer words like "still feeling punished" or "stuck on it."
- **The full six-part framework, taught as six explicit named steps.** This is native to 14-21 and carried into 21+ largely intact — both bands have the attention span and abstract capacity for it. At 11-14 it's simplified down to four beats that cover the same ground without asking middle-schoolers to track six labeled stages.
- **Adult-scale examples** — a career choice, a marriage or breakup, a parenting decision, a financial call. These stay out of 11-14 entirely, where they'd be meaningless (a 12-year-old has no marriage or mortgage to regret) and are the default texture of the 21+ version.
- **School-scale examples as the primary frame** — a single bad grade, a specific classroom incident. These are native to 11-14 and 14-21; they're not removed from 21+, but they stop being the lesson's center of gravity there, since most adults in the room are years past school being their main site of regret.
- **5-8 and 8-11 entirely.** Not a simplification decision — a scope decision. See the note at the top of this document.

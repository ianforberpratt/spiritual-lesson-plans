## What you should know

Kids this age don't process "the news" as a category — but they absolutely know the feeling of being scared by something big: a loud storm, a scary part of a movie, a grown-up's worried voice on the phone. That feeling is the real material here, not any specific headline. This lesson uses a thunderstorm as the whole metaphor on purpose — something almost every kid in the room has actually felt, doing the theological work without ever needing to name anything genuinely frightening happening in the world. Nothing you don't already have for prep — paper, crayons, maybe a chair to sit in a circle.

## What we're assuming

- Kids in this room come from a range of faith backgrounds and home experiences — some may have lived through a real storm or scary event recently; don't assume the storm is purely hypothetical for everyone.
- No assumed shared vocabulary about God — keep language simple and consistent with whatever the group already uses.
- Some kids may not want to talk about being scared out loud, and that's completely fine.

## Learning goals

- Leaves knowing a scary feeling is just weather passing through — not who they are.
- Feels safe naming something that scares them, without being made to explain it.
- Has one simple thing to say to themselves next time something scary happens.
- Gets to make something with their hands they can keep.

## How to hold the room

- Open by asking who's ever been in a loud storm — thunder, wind, rain on the roof. Let a few kids describe it.
- If a kid brings up something genuinely scary from real life (not just weather), receive it warmly and briefly — "that sounds like it was really scary" — without turning it into a group discussion. Follow up with them one-on-one after.
- Always give an easy way to pass: "or you can just listen, that's okay too."
- Silence is fine. Let it sit.

## The lesson flow

### Beat 1 (~5 min) — What a storm feels like

Ask: "Has anyone ever been in a really loud, scary storm? What did it feel like?" Let a few kids answer — the shaking windows, the flash before the thunder, maybe wanting to hide under a blanket.

Say: "A storm feels really big when it's happening. But here's something true about every storm there's ever been: it always passes. And here's something else true — the storm was never you. You were still you the whole time, even during the loudest part."

### Beat 2 (~7 min) — The sky underneath

Show a simple picture of a sunny sky. Attach a removable gray paper cloud with lightning on it (a tape loop or brad so it can lift off).

Say: "When something scary happens — a storm, or something on TV that scared you, or hearing a grown-up sound worried — it can feel like the scary thing is now part of you, like the cloud got stuck over your sunny sky forever." Pull the cloud off. "But it didn't. God made you like the sky underneath — always there, always whole, even when a cloud is passing over it. The cloud moves. The sky underneath never stops being sky."

> God created mankind in his own image, in the image of God he created them. — Genesis 1:27, NIV

Say it simply: "God made you like Himself — good, whole, safe on the inside — and nothing that happens outside can change that part."

### Beat 3 (~8 min) — Craft: my sunny sky

Give each kid paper, a pre-cut yellow circle (the sky/sun), and a gray cloud shape they can lift on and off.

While they work, repeat gently: "Clouds pass. The sky underneath stays the sky."

No spoken wrap-up needed — let the craft and the repetition do the work.

### Beat 4 (~5 min) — One thing to carry

Teach a simple phrase kids can say to themselves: "This is a stormy five minutes. I am not a storm."

Practice saying it together once, out loud, like a chant — not explained further, just repeated.

## What you'll need

Paper, crayons or markers, pre-cut yellow circles and gray cloud shapes (or let older 8-year-olds cut their own), tape loops or brads so the cloud can lift on and off, and the printable [handout](/assets/materials/scared-about-the-news/handout-5-8-full.html) — nothing else required.

## If it's not going well

- **Silence:** Totally normal for this age around big feelings. Let the craft carry it instead of forcing words.
- **A kid says "I'm not scared of anything":** Fine — let them make the craft anyway. No one has to perform fear to belong here.
- **A kid gets genuinely upset:** Pause, sit with them, keep your voice low and calm. Don't push the group activity forward until they're okay.
- **A kid discloses something that sounds like real harm** (not weather, not TV): stop facilitating the group activity, respond as a caring adult, and follow the site's mentor safety guidelines and your own local mandatory-reporting obligations.

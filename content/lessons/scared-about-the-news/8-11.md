## What you should know

Kids this age are starting to hear about things happening beyond their own house — a flood on the news playing in another room, something a friend's family went through, a scary story at recess. They can't yet reason abstractly about it, but they can compare two things side by side — which is exactly what this lesson uses. Nothing you don't already have for prep beyond the printable.

## What we're assuming

- Kids in this room have different levels of exposure to scary news — some may have already heard about something specific and real; keep the framing general enough not to require anyone to know what you mean.
- Varying faith backgrounds and starting points — no assumed shared vocabulary about God.
- No assumed willingness to speak; a simple pair-and-share format so nobody has to talk to the whole room.

## Learning goals

- Leaves able to name the difference between "something scary happened" and "something scary happened to me."
- Feels calmer about things they hear about but can't control.
- Has one simple, repeatable question to ask themselves when they hear something scary.
- Practiced saying it out loud to just one other person, not the whole room.

## How to hold the room

- Use pairs, not whole-group discussion — much lower pressure at this age.
- If a kid brings up something specific and real (a disaster, something violent), acknowledge it plainly and briefly, then gently steer back to the practice rather than opening a debate about the event itself.
- Offer an easy pass for the pair-share: "you can just tell your partner 'pass' and that's totally fine."

## The lesson flow

### Beat 1 (~5 min) — Two kinds of scary

Ask: "Has anyone ever heard about something scary that happened somewhere else — not to you, but somewhere far away?" Let a couple of kids answer if they want to, without dwelling on specifics.

Say: "There are two different kinds of scary. One is 'this is happening to me, right now.' The other is 'I heard about this, and it scared me, even though it isn't happening to me.' Both are real. But they're not the same kind of scared — and today we're going to practice telling them apart."

### Beat 2 (~8 min) — The comparison

Draw two columns on the board or paper: "Happening to me" and "I heard about it."

Work through a few examples together — a scraped knee (happening to me) vs. a flood on the news somewhere else (heard about it); a fire drill at school (happening to me) vs. a scary story a friend told (heard about it).

Say: "When something is 'happening to me,' you do something about it right then — get a bandage, walk outside. But when it's 'I heard about it,' the scared feeling is still real, even though there's nothing to do about it right this second. That's an important kind of scared to learn how to hold."

### Beat 3 (~7 min) — The truth underneath both

Read together:

> Do not fear, for I have redeemed you; I have summoned you by name; you are mine. — Isaiah 43:1, NIV

Say: "This doesn't say 'nothing scary will ever happen far away.' It says something bigger: no matter what you hear about, you are still known, still named, still held — right where you are. The scary thing didn't reach the part of you that's actually you."

### Beat 4 (~8 min) — Pair practice

In pairs, have kids practice this sentence with each other, filling in their own blank:

"I heard about ___, and it scared me. But I am still known and held, right where I am."

They don't have to share the specific scary thing with the whole group — just practice the sentence structure with their partner.

### Beat 5 (~4 min) — What to carry

Teach the question to carry into the week: "Is this happening to me, or did I hear about it — and either way, am I still known and held right now?"

## What you'll need

A board or paper for the two-column comparison and the printable [handout](/assets/materials/scared-about-the-news/handout-8-11-full.html) — nothing else required.

## If it's not going well

- **A kid gets stuck on a specific real event:** Acknowledge it once, plainly ("that sounds really scary, and it's okay that it scared you"), then redirect to the practice rather than debating details.
- **Pushback like "but bad things really do happen":** They're right — don't argue that away. The point isn't that nothing bad happens; it's whether the bad thing reaches who they actually are.
- **A kid seems more shaken than the room's pace expects:** Check in with them one-on-one after, not in front of the group.
- **A disclosure of real harm:** Stop facilitating, respond as a caring adult, and follow the site's mentor safety guidelines and your own local mandatory-reporting obligations.

# Lesson Core Model: "Grief Doesn't Need Fixing"

## Central claim
Grief is not a feeling to get over on a schedule. It's the real work of adjusting to a world that has changed — and that adjustment takes as long as it takes, moves in loops rather than a line, and doesn't require you to hold yourself together while you do it. That's a tough thing to hear, and it's true, and it isn't the whole truth: comfort is present through the work, even though the work itself can't be skipped.

## The picture being replaced
That grief has a correct shape and a correct order: a checklist you move down until you're "done." That if you're not "back to normal" by whatever point people seem to expect, something is wrong with you. That adjusting to a loss means forgetting it, minimizing it, or feeling fully fine again on a timeline everyone else assumes is reasonable.

## The picture being offered
Adjustment is honest, real work — not a mood you're supposed to snap out of, and not proof of weak faith or weak character when it takes a long time. It looks different for everyone and rarely moves in a straight line; the well-known "stages" are a map of common territory, not instructions or a finish line. Adjusting to a changed reality doesn't mean the loss stops mattering or the person or thing is forgotten — it means learning to carry it forward. And while you're doing this hard work, you're not empty-handed: there are real people, real practices, real strengths already present. Some days none of that will feel like enough, overwhelming and unreasonable and unclear — and that's part of the adjustment too, not a failure of it. And throughout every loop of that adjustment, the love and support of a power greater than ourselves is there — not a doctrine to agree on first, just a presence that serves as a comforter, supporting and carrying us through.

## Anchor citations

> "Comfort ye, comfort ye my people, saith your God." — Isaiah 40:1, KJV

> "Divine Love always has met and always will meet every human need." — Science and Health, Mary Baker Eddy, p. 494:10

## Learning objective family
- **Notice** — the false picture that grief must follow a proper order, timeline, or "together" performance, or that struggling to adjust means something's wrong with you.
- **Feel** — that comfort and hard, ongoing adjustment can coexist; you don't have to finish adjusting to be held.
- **Carry** — one small, repeatable move: naming what's actually there right now, without ranking it against what you "should" feel by now.
- **Locate** — a real loss, current or past, they can bring this lens to this week — no pressure to name it aloud.

## The bridge metaphor, by age band
- 5-8: a pet died, a favorite toy got left behind, a friend moved away. Feelings as weather, not an order — and someone's there in every kind of weather while things settle.
- 8-11: a move, a grandparent's death, a friendship group breaking apart. Grief as a shuffled playlist, not a set list — and adjusting to the new normal takes time, not a single afternoon.
- 11-14: friend-group shifts, divorce, a grandparent or pet, first real awareness of someone dying. The stages introduced by name as "a map, not a schedule," private-reflection option first.
- 14-21: breakup grief, a friend's or parent's death, a future that didn't happen the way it was supposed to. Expected-vs-unexpected named directly, and "adjustment" named as the actual work, not just a feeling to wait out.
- 21-plus: full adult scope — parent, spouse, child, career, diagnosis, collective grief. Strengths-based practice named explicitly; nonlinear/spiritual lens at full depth; adjustment named as ongoing, sometimes lifelong, work.

## What does NOT inherit across bands
- The named stages (denial, anger, bargaining, depression, acceptance) — not introduced explicitly below 11.
- Expected-vs-unexpected grief as a named, explicit distinction — starts simply at 11-14, full clinical framing (anticipatory grief, complicated-grief risk) only at 14-21/21-plus.
- "Strengths-based" / "asset-based" as a named practice framework — used explicitly starting 14-21 and 21-plus; younger bands just get "you already have things that help."
- Any specifics of death by suicide, violence, or overdose — never named at any band in this lesson.
- Any theological argument about who "your God" is, or "the power greater than ourselves," or how comfort works doctrinally — no band argues this; every band gets the same posture, that comfort accompanies the work of adjusting rather than replacing it, offered without requiring agreement on the metaphysics behind it.

## What you should know
This is the first band where naming the actual stages (denial, anger, bargaining, depression, acceptance) is useful rather than confusing — many kids this age have already half-absorbed this model from media, without the correction that matters most: even Elisabeth Kübler-Ross, who first described these patterns in 1969 from interviews with dying patients, said people move through them out of order, skip some, repeat others, and feel more than one at once. That correction is most of the lesson. The other half is naming plainly that adjusting to a loss is real work, not a mood that's supposed to lift on its own by a certain point — at this age, self-worth is unusually tied to "doing things right," so a rigid grief-stages chart, or pressure to be "over it," can become one more thing to fail at. This lesson exists to take that pressure off directly. Offer private reflection before any group sharing; quiet from this age group is very often real engagement, not disinterest. Nothing you don't already have for prep beyond the printable.

## What we're assuming
Some kids in the room may be sitting with a real, current loss — a parent's illness, a grandparent's death, a pet, a friend group falling apart, a divorce. No shared faith background or shared grief experience is assumed, and nobody is required to share specifics — private reflection is a real, respected option, not a fallback for kids who "won't participate."

## Learning goals
- Leaves knowing where the "five stages" idea came from and that it was never meant to be a checklist.
- Leaves knowing that grief looping, skipping, or repeating stages is normal, not a sign of doing it wrong.
- Leaves understanding that adjusting to a loss is honest work that takes real time — not proof something's wrong with them if it's still hard.
- Leaves with language for the difference between a loss they saw coming and one that surprised them, and that neither is "worse" — just different.
- Leaves with one concrete practice to carry into a moment when grief feels confusing.

## How to hold the room
Open factually — the history of the stages model is genuinely interesting and gives cover for a topic that can otherwise feel too personal to start with directly. Offer the private-reflection worksheet option before any pair or group sharing, and mean it: some kids will do real work silently and never say a word out loud, and that's a full success. If discussion happens, keep it welcoming of disagreement and confusion — there's no "right" answer to arrive at.

## The lesson flow

### Beat 1 (~8 min) — Where the stages came from
Tell the real history, briefly: in 1969, a doctor named Elisabeth Kübler-Ross interviewed people who were dying, and noticed five patterns show up a lot — denial, anger, bargaining, depression, and acceptance. It became famous as "the five stages of grief." But she said clearly, more than once, that these aren't a straight line you walk down — people bounce between them, skip some, feel two at once, and that's the normal way it works, not the exception. Other researchers since have said something similar in a different way: part of grieving is actually adjusting — learning how to live in a world that's different now that something or someone is missing from it. That's not a feeling. It's work. And work takes time.

### Beat 2 (~10 min) — A map, not a schedule
Hand out (or project) the "loop, not a line" visual from the handout. In pairs or alone (private reflection option), ask: has a feeling from this list ever shown up for you out of "order" — anger before sadness, or feeling okay and then getting hit with sadness way later than you expected? No one has to share the specific loss, just whether that's ever happened. Point out: that's not a malfunction. That's just what the map actually looks like for real people doing real adjusting.

### Beat 3 (~8 min) — Saw it coming vs. blindsided
Introduce, simply: some losses you can see coming — a grandparent who's been sick a long time, a move you knew about for months. Others blindside you — an accident, a sudden goodbye, news you didn't expect. Both are real grief, and neither is "worse" or "more allowed" than the other — they just hit differently: one gives you time to brace (and its own weight of watching and waiting), the other doesn't give you any warning at all. Either way, the adjusting afterward takes its own time.

### Beat 4 (~7 min) — Comfort that doesn't wait for the right stage
Read the anchor citations plainly:

> "Comfort ye, comfort ye my people, saith your God." — Isaiah 40:1, KJV

> "Divine Love always has met and always will meet every human need." — Science and Health, Mary Baker Eddy, p. 494:10

Point out that the Bible verse says "comfort ye, comfort ye" — twice in a row, like once wasn't enough to say. Frame it as: whatever stage-that's-not-really-a-stage you're in right now — mad, numb, weirdly fine, still bargaining — and however long the adjusting is actually taking, this comfort doesn't wait for you to arrive at "acceptance" first or finish the work before it counts. Through every loop on the map, many people describe feeling the love and support of a power greater than themselves — something that acts like a comforter, carrying them through, not just waiting at the finish line.

### Beat 5 (~7 min) — What I already have
Private reflection worksheet (handout): one loss, named or not; one thing/person/practice that already helps; one honest line about a day when nothing on that list feels like enough — and that this, too, is allowed to be true, and doesn't mean the adjusting isn't happening.

## If it's not going well
Silence is expected and fine, especially with private reflection offered — don't chase it with more questions. Disagreement or pushback on the theological framing, or on whether stages are "real" at all, is welcome as engagement, not a problem — you don't need to win the argument, just hold the room open. A strong emotional reaction means pausing, grounding the room, and checking in with that student privately rather than in front of the group. A disclosure of real harm, a recent death by violence/suicide, or anything beyond typical grief means stopping that thread, responding as a caring adult, and moving to the site's mentor safety guidelines and your own local mandatory-reporting obligations — this lesson's script stops there.

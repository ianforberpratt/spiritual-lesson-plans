## What you should know
By 8-11, kids can hold a real comparison ("this is like that") even if full abstract discussion is still a stretch. This lesson uses a shuffle-playlist metaphor instead of naming clinical stages — kids this age already have real losses (a move, parents splitting up, a grandparent dying, a pet dying, a friend group breaking apart), and what helps isn't a vocabulary lesson, it's permission that the order doesn't matter and the adjusting doesn't happen overnight. Keep discussion simple and comparison-based; this is not yet the age for open-ended abstract prompts. Nothing you don't already have for prep beyond the printable.

## What we're assuming
Kids in this room are carrying different kinds and sizes of loss, and some may not have named a loss to themselves yet as something they're "allowed" to be sad about (a move, a friendship ending — not just a death). No shared faith background or shared experience is assumed.

## Learning goals
- Leaves knowing that grief feelings don't have to show up in a "normal" order to be real.
- Leaves knowing that getting used to a big change is real work that takes time, not a feeling you're supposed to snap out of.
- Leaves with a simple way to notice what they're feeling without judging whether it's the "right" feeling.
- Leaves knowing that comfort is available even when the adjusting is still happening and nothing feels settled yet.

## How to hold the room
Open with the shuffle comparison rather than asking directly "has anyone lost someone?" Offer pairs or small groups instead of whole-group sharing so quieter kids aren't performing for the room. Always give an explicit pass option. Resist adding a spoken wrap-up moral at the end.

## The lesson flow

### Beat 1 (~5 min) — Set list vs. shuffle
Ask kids: when you make a playlist, is there a "set list" order, or do you hit shuffle? Most will say shuffle. Introduce the idea: some people think feelings after a loss are supposed to play in a set order — sad, then madder, then okay — and be done fast. Really, it's shuffle, and it plays for longer than people expect. The same songs (feelings) might repeat, skip around, play out of order, for weeks or months, and that's normal, not broken.

### Beat 2 (~10 min) — Match the feeling to the moment
Give small groups a set of feeling cards (sad, mad, relieved, numb, silly, worried, fine, confused) and a set of moment cards (the day it happened, a week later, a month later, a year later, out of nowhere at a totally random time). In pairs, kids match feelings to moments — the twist: there's no wrong match, and different groups will match differently, and feelings a whole year later are just as normal as feelings on day one. Debrief briefly: "Did everyone match the same way?" (No.) "Does that mean someone's doing grief wrong?" (No.)

### Beat 3 (~10 min) — Comfort while you're still adjusting
Introduce, simply: getting used to a big change takes real time — you don't have to be "done" adjusting to also feel some comfort along the way. Read the anchor quote about Love always meeting every need, in plain words:

> "Divine Love always has met and always will meet every human need." — Science and Health, Mary Baker Eddy, p. 494:10

"However you're feeling, and for however long it takes, that comfort doesn't run out." Ask: "Is that more like a light that only turns on once you're all settled again, or a light that's on the whole time you're still getting used to things?" (The whole time.) Some describe this as a power greater than ourselves acting like a comforter — help that carries you through the middle of the shuffle, not just something waiting at the end of it.

### Beat 4 (~7 min) — Comfort card, upgraded
Kids fill out the printable (see handout) — one loss (big or small, their choice, private if they want) on one side, and on the other, one thing/person/practice that helps, plus one line about the "whole-time" comfort, in their own words.

## What you'll need
Feeling cards and moment cards (print or hand-write on slips), the printable handouts (see below), pens.

## If it's not going well
Silence or short answers are normal, especially around a recent loss — don't push for elaboration. Pushback ("this is dumb" / "I don't want to talk about this") is welcome; offer the pass option and mean it. If there's a strong reaction, pause the activity, check in with that kid privately, ground the room before continuing. A disclosure of real harm or danger means stopping the activity, responding as a caring adult, and following the site's mentor safety guidelines and your own local mandatory-reporting obligations — this lesson doesn't script further than that.

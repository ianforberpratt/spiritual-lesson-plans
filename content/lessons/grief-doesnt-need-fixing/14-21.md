## What you should know
This age can hold real depth — the difference between anticipatory grief (a long illness, a slow goodbye) and sudden/traumatic grief (an accident, a shocking loss) is worth naming directly, because teens and early-twenty-somethings are often navigating both for the first time as something closer to adults: a parent's illness, a friend's death, a breakup that ended a future they'd planned around, a rejection that closed a door they'd built an identity on. Anchor examples in independence and identity — leaving home, first serious relationships, first real losses that aren't softened by a parent managing everything — not childhood scenarios. This is also the first band where naming "strengths-based" as an actual practice (borrowed from social work, and resonant with a God-centered view of already being whole) adds something real, and where naming "adjustment" directly matters most: this age is often told grief should resolve by some point (a semester, a year), and the honest truth — that adjusting to a changed life is ongoing work, not a mood with an expiration date — can be a relief rather than a burden when it's said out loud. Nothing you don't already have for prep beyond the printable.

## What we're assuming
Faith backgrounds vary widely in this room, and so will the losses people are carrying — some visible, many invisible (a private family situation, a loss of a future they'd planned on, a friendship or relationship ending). No one is assumed to be "over" anything, and no one is required to name what they're bringing to this out loud.

## Learning goals
- Leaves able to name the difference between anticipatory and sudden grief, and knows neither one is "more valid" than the other.
- Leaves understanding grief as the work of adjusting to a changed reality — not a feeling with a normal expiration date.
- Leaves with a strengths-based way to take stock of what they already have (people, practices, capacities) without it turning into forced positivity.
- Leaves knowing that a day when none of that feels like enough is not a failure of the practice, the adjustment, or their faith.
- Leaves with language for a comfort that doesn't require them to be in the "right" emotional place, or done adjusting, to receive it.

## How to hold the room
This age can handle direct, adult-level framing — say the real words (anticipatory grief, sudden grief, strengths-based practice, adjustment) rather than softening them, and trust the room to sit with real tension rather than resolving it too fast. Discussion can go deeper here, but keep an explicit door open for anyone who'd rather write than speak. Don't rush past silence or discomfort — it's often where the real thinking is happening.

## The lesson flow

### Beat 1 (~8 min) — Two shapes of grief
Name both directly: anticipatory grief is grieving something before it's fully gone — a parent's decline, a relationship you can feel ending, a diagnosis with a clock attached. It lets you brace, say some things out loud, maybe even say goodbye — but it also means living inside the loss for a long time before it's "official," which is its own exhausting weight. Sudden grief is the other shape — no warning, no chance to brace, sometimes not even a chance to say the last thing you wanted to say. Ask (open discussion or private write): which shape is more familiar to you right now, if either? No pressure to share specifics.

### Beat 2 (~10 min) — Neither one is worse
Discuss directly: people sometimes rank grief — "at least you had time to prepare" or "at least it was quick" — as if there's a version of loss that hurts less. Both shapes carry real weight, just different weight, and both require the same real work afterward: adjusting to the world as it actually is now. Sitting with someone's grief (including your own) doesn't require deciding which shape it is or arguing about which one is harder.

### Beat 3 (~10 min) — Strengths-based, not forced-positive
Introduce the term directly: strengths-based (or asset-based) practice, borrowed from social work, means starting from what a person already has — real relationships, real skills, real practices, real faith — instead of starting from what's broken or missing. It is not the same as "look on the bright side," and it doesn't mean the adjustment is finished or easy. Ask the room to actually list, for themselves (write or discuss): a person, a practice, and a strength they already have that's real right now, even in the middle of this. Then name the other half honestly: some days none of that will feel like enough, and that's not a sign the practice failed, or that you're adjusting wrong — it's just what a hard day in the middle of real work looks like.

### Beat 4 (~10 min) — Comfort that doesn't wait for you to be ready
Read both anchor citations:

> "Comfort ye, comfort ye my people, saith your God." — Isaiah 40:1, KJV

> "Divine Love always has met and always will meet every human need." — Science and Health, Mary Baker Eddy, p. 494:10

Point out the doubling in the Isaiah line — "comfort ye, comfort ye" — said twice, as if once wasn't enough for how real the need is. Frame directly: this isn't a comfort that requires you to have finished processing, picked a lane (anticipatory or sudden), or gotten your strengths list right first. It's present through the bracing, through the blindside, through the days the strengths list feels like nothing, and through however long the actual adjusting takes. Throughout every part of that cycle, many people find that the love and support of a power greater than themselves is there, functioning as a comforter that helps carry them through — not something that shows up only once things resolve.

### Beat 5 (~7 min) — Reflection and handout
Fill out the printable (handout) privately: name the shape of a loss they're carrying (or skip this line), one real strength/person/practice, and one honest sentence about a day it wasn't enough — with the reminder that both lines can be true at once, and neither one means the adjustment isn't happening.

## If it's not going well
Silence is often real engagement at this age — don't fill it. Pushback or disagreement, including about the theological content, is welcome; don't argue to win — this room includes people of every faith background and none. A strong emotional reaction means pausing, grounding the room, and checking in privately rather than in front of everyone. A disclosure of real harm — self-harm, abuse, a loss by suicide or violence, or anything beyond typical grief — means stopping that thread immediately, responding as a caring adult, and moving to the site's mentor safety guidelines and your own local mandatory-reporting obligations. This lesson's script does not extend further than that; don't attempt to counsel through a disclosure like this yourself.

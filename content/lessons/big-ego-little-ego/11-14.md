## What you should know

This is the first age where the letter-case idea itself — capital-E Ego versus lowercase-e ego —
becomes usable, and honestly, kind of fun. Abstract thinking is coming online, and this group can
genuinely enjoy discovering that one letter changes everything. Keep the examples relational and
close to their actual week: grades, group chats, who got picked, comparison with a friend or
sibling — not workplace or geopolitical material, which belongs to older bands. Self-worth is
unusually externally anchored at this age; treat this as a known developmental feature, not a
flaw in any kid in the room.

**What we're assuming**

- Most kids this age are already fluent in comparison — grades, followers, group chats, teams,
  siblings.
- Some may be dealing with real anxiety or family pressure around performance right now. This
  lesson can be moving for them, or it can surface something bigger than a class setting can hold
  — see "If it's not going well."
- Give a private option before any sharing. Forced vulnerability at this age often produces
  performance, not honesty.

## Learning goals

1. Notice one specific thing the little-e voice actually says on a normal day — not "insecurity"
   in the abstract, the real sentence.
2. Understand, not just as a slogan, that this voice isn't automatically the truth about who they
   are.
3. Carry one question into a real moment this week.
4. Have at least one private, unshared reflection — this age needs the option of privacy to be
   honest at all.

## How to hold the room

- Don't require sharing. Offer it, make it easy, and let silence be okay.
- Hold both truths: grades and comparisons do matter practically, *and* worth isn't the same
  thing as performance. Don't argue either one away.
- Watch for a kid who goes quieter than their normal reserve — that's worth a private check-in
  after.

## The lesson flow

### Beat 1 (~5 min) — Word study warm-up

"Ego" is Latin for "I." Ask: "When someone says a person 'has a big ego,' is that usually a
compliment or an insult?" (Insult — boastful, full of themselves.)

"What if I told you God actually wants you to have a big Ego — just spelled with a capital
letter, and meaning something completely different?" Let that sit for a second before explaining
anything.

### Beat 2 (~8 min) — Two voices, named

Introduce the distinction: **big-E Ego** — God, the one true "I," the source everything reflects
— and **little-e ego** — the mortal, anxious voice that has to prove and defend itself. Read:

> "I, or EGO. Divine Principle; Spirit; Soul; incorporeal, unerring, immortal, and eternal Mind."
> — Science and Health with Key to the Scriptures, Mary Baker Eddy, p. 588:9

> "And God said unto Moses, I AM THAT I AM." — Exodus 3:14

Ask: "What's different about an 'I' that doesn't have to prove it exists, compared to the 'I'
most of us live inside on a normal day?"

### Beat 3 (~10 min) — The two-voices T-chart (private first)

Give everyone paper, folded in half. On one side: everything little-e ego says on a normal day —
specific, not abstract ("I'm not enough," "what do they think of me," "I have to win this," a
grudge, needing to be picked first). On the other side: what big-E Ego already knows is true
(whole, loved, complete, unafraid, doesn't have to compete for what it already has).

5 minutes, silent, individual. Then optional pairs share (not required).

### Beat 4 (~8 min) — Scenario work

Small groups get one or two real scenarios: didn't make a team, a comment or text that landed
wrong in a group chat, comparing grades with a friend, losing an argument with a parent.

Ask each group: "What's little-e ego's reaction here? What does big-E Ego already know that makes
that reaction unnecessary?"

Groups report back briefly.

### Beat 5 (~6 min) — One question to carry

Teach the question: *"Is this the little-e voice talking, or is this actually true about me?"*

Practice it once together on a shared example before closing.

**Closing**

"You are not the anxious voice arguing for your worth today. You're already the reflection of the
one Mind that doesn't have to argue for anything."

## What you'll need

- Paper (folded in half) and pens, one per participant
- Index cards for the Beat 4 scenarios (optional)
- Print and hand out the ["Big Ego, Little Ego"](/assets/materials/big-ego-little-ego/big-ego-little-ego-wallet-card.html) wallet card

## If it's not going well

This age carries real developmental risk around self-worth and comparison — this lesson can
surface genuine distress, not just useful discomfort. If a student's private reflection, body
language, or comments suggest something beyond ordinary middle-school pressure — persistent
hopelessness, signs of self-harm, disclosure of harm from someone else — don't try to resolve it
in class. Follow the site's mentor safety guidelines (For Mentors page), loop in the appropriate
adult per your church's child-safety policy, and take it as seriously as any other disclosure at
this age — it can arrive quietly, inside a written reflection rather than spoken aloud.

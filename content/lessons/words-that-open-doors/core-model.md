# Core Model: Words That Open Doors

## Central claim
The words we inherit for the sacred — master, worship, sin, holy, God, and countless others — are not the sacred thing itself. They're human attempts, made at particular moments in particular cultures, to point at something bigger than any word can fully hold. When an inherited word stops working for someone, that isn't a crisis of faith or a failure of loyalty — it's an invitation to keep translating what the word was always trying to protect.

## The picture being replaced
The picture that the "right" words for God are fixed, handed down once and for all, and that struggling with or outgrowing inherited religious vocabulary means something is wrong — with your faith, your understanding, or your loyalty to your family or tradition. In this picture, questioning a word feels like questioning the thing itself, so people either force themselves to keep using language that no longer feels true, or walk away from the whole search because the words got in the way.

## The picture being offered
You can hold your inherited words loosely. You can test them against your own lived experience, keep the ones that still open a door, and consciously re-translate the ones that have become a wall — without abandoning what the original word was protecting, and without needing anyone's permission to do the translating. The sacred doesn't change when the words change. Only your access to it does.

## Anchor citations
- Exodus 3:14 — "I AM THAT I AM." The divine identifies itself in a way that refuses to be reduced to a single fixed label, modeling that naming the sacred is inherently an act of pointing, not capturing.
- Science and Health with Key to the Scriptures, Glossary — Mary Baker Eddy offers seven synonymous terms for God (Life, Truth, Love, Mind, Soul, Spirit, Principle) rather than one fixed name, demonstrating that naming the divine has always been a practice of translation, not a one-time transaction settled by a single word.
- 1 Corinthians 13:11 — "When I was a child, I spake as a child... but when I became a man, I put away childish things." Growing into new language for what you believe is part of growing up, not a betrayal of what came before.

## Learning objective family
1. Notice which inherited words for the sacred feel like doors and which feel like walls.
2. Feel the relief of permission — not just understand intellectually that words can be held loosely, but feel a real loosening of the pressure to find or keep the "right" word.
3. Carry a repeatable practice: when a word about the sacred stops working, ask "what was this word trying to protect, and what would say that today?"
4. Locate one real word, in their own life right now, that they're ready to keep, retire, or re-translate.

## The bridge, by age band

| Age band | What the core tension looks like concretely | Bridge/metaphor used |
|---|---|---|
| 11–14 | A young teen starts, often for the first time, to notice that a word an adult or a church used to describe God doesn't quite land the way it used to — without yet having language for why. | Sort words into "doors" and "walls," then privately journal before choosing whether to share a translation of one word — gentler starter words (believe, pray, holy) offered alongside the harder ones. |
| 14–21 (native) | A teen or early-college student is actively forming their own identity and can feel, sharply, that a word handed to them by adults was built by someone else — for someone else's life. | "Words are doors or walls," then translate one old word (master, leader, worship, sin, holy) into language that is genuinely theirs, while naming what the original word was trying to protect. |
| 21+ | An adult looks back across a longer arc — childhood church, family tradition, maybe years of distance, maybe a return — and re-examines which inherited words still work now, gently touching the real experience of deconstructing and reconstructing a faith vocabulary over time. | Adults choose a word carried since childhood, examine it against decades of lived experience rather than a single moment, and decide — with no timeline pressure — whether to keep it, retire it, or translate it. |

*(No 5–8 or 8–11 version exists for this lesson — see below.)*

## What does NOT get inherited across bands
- **5–8 and 8–11 get no version of this lesson at all.** The core exercise — noticing that inherited vocabulary itself has quietly become a "wall," and then consciously re-translating a word while preserving what it was protecting — requires examining language *about* language (meta-linguistic awareness). That capacity isn't reliably online until abstract thought begins emerging around 11. Younger children take words at face value; asking them to hold a word loosely while also honoring it is asking for a cognitive move they haven't developed yet, not a simpler version of this lesson.
- **The words "deconstruction" and "reconstruction" stay out of the 11–14 band.** They're accurate for what's happening but too loaded and abstract for a first encounter with this idea. At 11–14 the exercise stays concrete: this word, does it land or not.
- **The harder example words (master, worship, sin) stay optional, not primary, at 11–14.** Leading with gentler words (believe, pray, holy) keeps the exercise from feeling like it's asking a young teen to relitigate doctrine before they have the footing for it.
- **The explicit adult narrative arc — years of distance from a tradition, then a return, or a long deconstruction — stays out of 14–21 and below.** That's a shape of experience that unfolds over a decade or more of adult life; it doesn't map onto a teenager's timeline and shouldn't be implied as something they're behind on.
- **Extended, open-ended silence as a discussion technique stays reserved for 21+.** Adults can sit with ambiguity and unresolved feeling for longer stretches. Younger bands need more structure and a defined transition prompt so the discussion doesn't stall out or feel like abandonment.

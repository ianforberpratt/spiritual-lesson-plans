## What you should know
Kids this age are right at the edge of comparing two systems side by side, which is exactly what the wheel does: the rim (bumpy, busy, muddy) next to the hub (still, holding everything). Keep it physical and concrete. The scene is a sports bench because it's a place almost every kid at this age has stood: someone misses the last shot, and nobody knows what to do with their face. Don't reach for social media or politics; that isn't this age's actual world.

The "three kinds of weather" are the same arc as the bike-riding picture in [Seven Names, One Journey](/lessons/seven-synonyms-and-three-degrees) (wobbly, practicing, got-it). If the group has done that lesson, say so. If not, nothing is lost; it stands alone. This is also the practice-sized follow-up to [Rescue or Faith?](/lessons/rescue-or-faith). Required prep: zero beyond a pitcher of water and cups.

## What we're assuming
- Every kid here has been on both sides: the one who messed up, and the one who didn't know what to say.
- Faith backgrounds vary. "Love" and "God" are used plainly. Nobody has to say a prayer out loud.
- Some kids will say a real current hurt in a pair. That's fine. Don't turn it into a group discussion on the spot; note it for a one-on-one follow-up.
- This isn't about being a fixer. It's about not freezing, and not overdoing it either.

## Learning goals
1. Know that when someone is hurting, a wobbly feeling in you is normal and not a sign you're doing it wrong.
2. Learn four moves: **breathe, peek, middle, cup**, and be able to say what each one is for.
3. Practice choosing one small, real, kind thing that fits what the person actually needs.
4. Leave with one friend or one moment this week to try it on.

## How to hold the room
- Let the scene and the wheel do the work. Don't explain the point before they've felt it.
- Pairs and trios work better than the big circle for the honest parts.
- If a kid names a real, current situation, receive it ("I'm glad you told me") and follow up one-on-one later, not in front of everyone.
- Everyone may pass. "Pass" is a full answer.

## The lesson flow
### Beat 1 (~5 min) — The silent bench
Before kids arrive, put a small cup of water in the middle of the circle. Don't explain it. Then set the scene out loud: "Maya misses the last shot. The buzzer goes. Her team lost by one. Everybody goes quiet." Ask: "What do people usually do?" Let them list (look away, say 'it's fine' too fast, tell her what she should have done, make a joke, say nothing). Say: "Every one of those is a wobbly move. Wobbly isn't bad. It's just not the best we've got."

### Beat 2 (~8 min) — Hold still, hand out
Use a real wheel to show it (a bike wheel works great). Then do the human version: one kid is the **hub** and stands still holding a marker (a cone, a book). Everyone else is the **rim** and walks slowly in a circle around the hub. The facilitator calls out bumps: "a loud buzzer," "someone says something mean," "a surprise," "a rainy day." On each bump the rim wobbles and stumbles. The hub does not move. Ask the hub: "What was that like?" Then say:

"The outside bumps over everything. The middle doesn't. And the whole wheel still gets where it's going, because it turns on the middle."

Read:

> "Be still, and know that I am God." — Psalm 46:10

"Still in the middle doesn't mean not caring. It means you're holding the thing that doesn't move. Love is in the middle. It's there before the bump and after the bump."

### Beat 3 (~8 min) — Four moves, one scene
Put the four moves on the board and walk through them with Maya's scene. You do the first two out loud so they can see it. Speak tentatively: "here's how it might look."

1. **Breathe, and name the wobble.** "My tummy is tight. I'm scared I'll say the wrong thing." (From Science and Health: the very first thing in a hard moment is *Be not afraid.* Say it as: "Start by calming your own wobble.")
2. **Peek at the picture.** "What's the picture in my head? 'Maya's ruined it.' 'I should fix this.' 'If I say anything it'll be worse.' That's a picture. It's not the truth about Maya."
3. **Middle.** "What's true about Maya, whatever happened? She's not ruined. She's loved. Love is in the middle for her and for me."
4. **Cup.** "What's one small kind thing?"

Then give them the **three kinds of weather** for choosing the cup:
- **Wobbly weather** (very upset): the best cup is *staying close.* Sit next to her. No advice.
- **Working-at-it weather** (calming down, trying): the best cup is *a kind word.* "That was a tough one. You've made worse shots look easy."
- **Got-it weather** (steady already): the best cup is *remembering together.* "Remember the shot you made in the first half?"

"The cup changes with the weather. The middle never does."

### Beat 4 (~10 min) — Trios practice
Form trios. Hand each trio one **scenario card** (see printable cards). Each person plays one part: the one who needs the cup, the one who gives it, and the "coach" who reads the four moves from the pocket card. They have about three minutes per card, then swap roles. Walk around and ask only questions: "What's the wobble? What's the weather? What's the middle? What's the cup?" Don't give answers. If a trio argues about what really happened in the scenario, send them back to move one.

Read, as a closer for the beat:

> "Love is impartial and universal in its adaptation and bestowals. It is the open fount which cries, 'Ho, every one that thirsteth, come ye to the waters.'" — Science and Health, Mary Baker Eddy, p. 13:2–4

"'Every one.' That means the same cup you'd give your best friend, you'd also be willing to give the kid who's been hard to like."

### Beat 5 (~6 min) — Make it yours
Each kid makes the pocket card: fold the sheet, write one *real* cup they could try this week on the inside ("sit with Jaden at lunch," "ask my sister if she wants company or space"), and draw the cup. Fold the **Cup Folder** from the full handout if there's time; it's a paper fortune-teller of eight small cups. They play it with a partner.

### Beat 6 (~3 min) — Pour
Bring the cup from the middle back. Pour a little water for each kid. Everyone offers one to a neighbor by name: "Would you like a cup of cold water?" Close quietly. No spoken moral. "Same time next week."

## What you'll need
- Small paper cups, a pitcher an adult pours (water only), or pretend cups
- A bike wheel or toy wheel (optional but great), a cone or book for the hub
- Board or paper for the four moves
- Scissors, pencils
- Printables: the [Cup Folder](/assets/materials/the-cup-and-the-center/handout-8-11-full.html) (a fold-up paper fortune-teller of eight small cups), the pocket card ["Four Moves"](/assets/materials/the-cup-and-the-center/handout-8-11-wallet.html), and the [scenario cards](/assets/materials/the-cup-and-the-center/cards-8-11.html) (one set per trio, cut apart)
- Optional for the wall: the [wheel poster](/assets/materials/the-cup-and-the-center/the-wheel-poster.html)

## If it's not going well
If the room is silly or loud, shrink the circle, slow the "bumps," and give the hub a job. If a kid gets really upset (real tears), pause, take a breath together, and check in privately. If a kid describes ongoing exclusion, a frightening grown-up, or anything that sounds like real harm, don't probe in the moment: stay warm, thank them, and follow the site's mentor safety guidelines (For Mentors page) afterward, including your organization's child-safety policy and your own local mandatory-reporting obligations. A strong scenario can bring up something real; take it seriously even though it arrives gently.

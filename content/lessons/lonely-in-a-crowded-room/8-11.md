## What you should know
This age group already knows this feeling firsthand, even if they've never named it — sitting at a full lunch table and still feeling on the outside, being on a team and still feeling left out. Keep the bridge concrete and sensory: a specific lunch table, a specific recess moment. Don't reach for phones, likes, or social media as the hook — that's a real part of the ache for older kids, but it isn't this age's actual daily experience, and using it would make the lesson feel like it's about someone else's life instead of theirs.

## What we're assuming
- Most kids this age have felt this at least once — recess, lunch, a team, a birthday party — even if they've never said it out loud.
- Some kids in the room may be dealing with real, ongoing exclusion rather than an occasional off day. Watch for the difference; it changes what kind of follow-up is needed.
- This isn't the moment to promise the feeling will never come back. It's about knowing what's still true when it does.

## Learning goals
1. Know that feeling lonely even with people around doesn't mean something is wrong with them.
2. Learn one simple thing they can do when that feeling shows up — sit with someone new, or tell a friend or grown-up how they feel.
3. Hear, in words simple enough to hold onto, that they're never actually alone, even in that feeling.
4. Name one person they could sit with or talk to this week.

## How to hold the room
- Let the lunch-table story do the work — don't over-explain the point before they've had a chance to feel it themselves.
- Pairs work better than one big circle for the honest parts. Not every 9-year-old will say "I've felt that" out loud to the whole room, but most will tell a partner.
- If a kid names a real, current situation (being left out by a specific friend group, for example), don't turn it into group discussion on the spot — note it for a one-on-one follow-up.

## The lesson flow
### Beat 1 (~5 min) — Has this ever happened to you?
Ask: "Have you ever been around a bunch of kids — at recess, at lunch, on a team — and still felt kind of alone?"
Let a few kids answer out loud if they want to. No wrong answers — this is just noticing, not confessing anything.

### Beat 2 (~8 min) — A story about lunch
Tell or read aloud: *"Sam sits down at a full lunch table. Everyone around Sam is talking and laughing. Sam is right there — but somehow feels like nobody would notice if Sam got up and left."*
Discuss in pairs (2 min each way): "Has that ever happened to you? What did it feel like in your body — your stomach, your chest?"

### Beat 3 (~7 min) — What's still true
Read together: "Where can I go from your Spirit? Where can I flee from your presence? If I go up to the heavens, you are there; if I make my bed in the depths, you are there." — Psalm 139:7–10
"Divine Love always has met and always will meet every human need." — Mary Baker Eddy, *Science and Health*, p. 494
Explain simply: even at that lunch table, even at recess, even feeling invisible — Love is still right there with Sam, and with you.
Discuss in pairs: "What's one thing that might feel different if you knew, for sure, you were never actually alone — even when it feels that way?"

### Beat 4 (~7 min) — One thing you can do
Teach two simple actions: sit with someone new, or tell a friend (or a grown-up) how you're feeling.
Ask: "Which one feels easier to try — sitting with someone new, or telling someone how you feel? Why?"
Let a few kids answer out loud; there's no wrong choice.

### Beat 5 (~5 min) — Someone to reach out to
Each kid says, draws, or writes the name of one person they could sit with or talk to this week.
Close with: "You are not actually alone, even on the days it feels that way. You're seen, and you're loved — right now, at this table, exactly as you are."

## What you'll need
- Paper and pencils or markers, one per child
- A whiteboard or poster paper for Beat 1 (optional)

## If it's not going well
Watch for the difference between an ordinary off day and a pattern of real exclusion or bullying. If a child describes being left out again and again by the same kids, or seems more upset than the moment calls for, don't press for more in front of the group — follow up one-on-one after class, and check the site's mentor safety guidelines (For Mentors page) if what you're hearing sounds like more than ordinary elementary-school friendship trouble.

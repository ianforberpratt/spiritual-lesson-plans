## What you should know
Adult loneliness often carries more shame than teen loneliness, because there's an assumption that by a certain age this should be "figured out" — a full calendar, a spouse, a career, kids, should be proof against it. The research doesn't bear that out: loneliness tracks with felt belonging, not headcount. Married people feel it. Parents surrounded by kids feel it. People years into a career, and people brand-new to a city, both feel it. This isn't a private admission of failure — it's a documented, common experience across every adult life stage. Don't let the room's polish (people who look like they have it together) fool you into skipping the naming step; that's often exactly who needs it most.

## What we're assuming
- This group may include people in very different seasons — a new parent, someone newly divorced or widowed, someone new to the area, someone deep in a demanding career or caregiving role. The ache is the same shape even though the surface cause differs.
- Don't assume being partnered means someone isn't lonely. That assumption itself can make a lonely spouse or partner feel even more invisible.
- Some adults in the room have never said this out loud, and may feel embarrassed to admit it given how their life looks from outside.

## Learning goals
1. Recognize that loneliness in adulthood isn't evidence of a failed life, a failed marriage, or a personal defect — it's common, and it doesn't require an external fix to be spiritually addressed.
2. Distinguish the feeling of loneliness from the fact of their belonging within an infinite Love that doesn't depend on circumstance.
3. Identify one concrete practice — reaching up, then reaching out — to use next time the ache shows up, instead of defaulting to busyness, overwork, or isolation.
4. Name, out loud or in writing, one real relationship or person they could reach out to this week.

## How to hold the room
- Adults may carry more shame around naming loneliness than teens do — don't rush past the naming, even if the room is initially reluctant.
- Be ready for it to surface inside a marriage or long partnership, not just around single or newly-relocated adults. Don't assume partnered people are exempt.
- Adults may connect this to grief, retirement, an empty nest, or caregiving fatigue. Let it surface without redirecting to something lighter.

## The lesson flow
### Beat 1 (~10 min) — The Ache Nobody Admits
We build full lives — careers, marriages, kids' schedules, packed calendars — and the ache can still show up, sometimes loudest in the middle of a family dinner or a crowded work event. Reflect or share: when do you feel this ache the most — in a marriage, at work, in a new city, in a season change?

### Beat 2 (~10–15 min) — Reaching for the Wrong Thing First
Share this out loud: when the ache shows up, the instinct is often to fill it with busyness — overworking, overcommitting, numbing out, or throwing yourself entirely into parenting or a project. It rarely actually fills it.
Discuss: Has staying busy, or distracting yourself, ever masked this ache instead of actually addressing it? What did you notice?

### Beat 3 (~15 min) — What's Still True Underneath
Read together: "For I am convinced that neither death nor life, neither angels nor demons, neither the present nor the future, nor any powers, neither height nor depth, nor anything else in all creation, will be able to separate us from the love of God that is in Christ Jesus our Lord." — Romans 8:38–39
"Divine Love always has met and always will meet every human need." — Mary Baker Eddy, *Science and Health*, p. 494
Discuss: What's one thing that changes if you believe you're never actually outside of that love — even in a marriage where you feel unseen, or a city where you don't yet know anyone?

### Beat 4 (~10 min) — Reach Up, Then Reach Out
Each person names one person they could reach out to this week — a spouse, an old friend, a new neighbor, anyone. Say it out loud or write it down. Close with this: loneliness is a claim of absence. Love's presence is infinite. You are known, seen, and cherished — right now, exactly as your life actually looks.

## If it's not going well
Adult loneliness can sometimes be a symptom of something bigger than a season of life — ongoing marital pain, grief, isolation tied to caregiving, or real depression. If someone describes persistent hopelessness, a relationship that sounds unsafe rather than simply lonely, or anything beyond an occasional ache, pause and check in with them one-on-one. Consult the site's mentor safety guidelines (For Mentors page), and don't hesitate to point toward professional support alongside the spiritual conversation.

## What you should know
Adults carry a version of this sneaky belief that's arguably harder to name than a teenager's, because adult life doesn't pause for an off day. There's a shift meant to be worked, a kid who needs picking up, a spouse who needs presence, a congregation or small group where showing up "blessed" can feel like part of the job. The belief here isn't "if I were doing this right, I'd get better grades" — it's closer to "if my faith or my character were where they should be by now, I wouldn't still be having days like this." Name that gap between "still having off days at this age/stage" and "should have this figured out by now" directly; it's often the real source of shame, more than the off day itself.
## What we're assuming
- Everyone in the room is currently managing at least one role — work, parenting, marriage, caregiving, ministry or volunteer leadership — where an off day isn't allowed to visibly show, and that costs something.
- Some adults in the room may be dealing with more than an ordinary off day — real depression, burnout, or grief. This lesson can open a door to that; it isn't equipped to be the only support on the other side of it. See "If it's not going well" below.
- Nobody needs to disclose specifics about work, marriage, or parenting to benefit from this. Keep the door open for people to speak generally.

## Learning goals
1. Name the specific place in their adult life — work, parenting, marriage, community role — where an off day feels least allowed to show.
2. Understand the difference between an off day and evidence they're failing at their faith, their character, or their responsibilities.
3. Practice saying a hard truth and a hopeful truth in the same breath — "I know ___ is true, but right now I feel ___" — in language suited to an adult role, not a school setting.
4. Locate one real relationship or setting this week where they could risk being honest instead of performing "fine."

## How to hold the room
- Adults will often intellectualize before they feel anything — expect the first few answers to be a little abstract or general, and stay with the group past that point rather than accepting the first answer as the real one.
- Don't let the room slide into venting about work or family logistics generally. Gently redirect back to the feeling underneath, not the schedule.
- Silence is fine and often productive with adults. Don't rush to fill it.

## The lesson flow
### Beat 1 (~8 min) — Naming an off day, adult version
Ask: "Think of a recent day you felt disconnected, low, or just not yourself — while you still had to function. Show up to work. Parent. Be present with a spouse or partner. What did that actually feel like?"

Let people share as much or as little as they want. It's fine if the first answers are general.

### Beat 2 (~10-12 min) — The pressure to seem fine, or blessed
"There's a specific version of this for adults: the pressure to seem fine, put-together, or even 'blessed,' especially in front of a faith community, coworkers, or your own kids. A parent having an off day can feel like it's not allowed — someone's depending on you. Someone at work having an off day can feel like it threatens how competent you seem."

Discuss: "Where do you feel the most pressure to seem okay when you're not — at work, at home, in your faith community, somewhere else? What does that pressure actually cost you?"

### Beat 3 (~12-15 min) — You're not being tested
"You're not a student, or an employee, or a parent, or a Christian being graded on how positive, spiritual, or put-together you're managing to seem today. Being honest — even just with yourself — isn't a failure of faith or character. 'I know things are okay, but right now I feel overwhelmed' can be entirely true."

Read together:

> "God is Love. Can we ask Him to be more?" — *Science and Health*, p. 2:23

> "Divine Love always has met and always will meet every human need." — *Science and Health*, p. 494:10

Try it: Practice saying, out loud or on paper: "I know ___ is true, but right now I feel ___." Give people a chance to try it more than once, aimed at different areas of their life (work, a relationship, their own expectations of themselves).

### Beat 4 (~10 min) — Small group or paired discussion
In pairs or small groups: "Is there someone in your life right now — a spouse, a friend, a coworker — you could actually say a version of that sentence to, instead of just saying 'I'm fine'? What's stopping you?"

### Beat 5 (~8 min) — Information, not indictment
"An off day isn't a punishment, and it isn't a sign you're behind in your marriage, your parenting, your job, or your faith. Sometimes it's just where you are today."

> "Man is God's reflection, needing no cultivation, but ever beautiful and complete." — *Science and Health*, p. 527:4

"You don't lose your wholeness on a bad day, at any stage of life. It was never something you had to keep earning — not at 25, not at 55."

## If it's not going well
If what surfaces sounds like more than an ordinary off day — ongoing hopelessness, burnout that isn't lifting, signs of depression, marital distress that sounds unsafe, or any mention of self-harm — this has moved past a spiritual-practice conversation into a wellbeing one. Don't try to resolve it inside the group format. Take it seriously, follow the site's mentor safety guidelines (For Mentors page), and help the person connect with appropriate support — a counselor, physician, or in cases of safety concerns, appropriate crisis resources — rather than treating this lesson as sufficient on its own.

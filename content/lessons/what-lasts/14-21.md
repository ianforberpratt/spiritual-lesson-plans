## What you should know

This age can hold real self-audit — not just "what do I value" as an abstract question, but "where
is my actual time and attention going, regardless of what I'd say I value." The bridge is an
investment question, not a morals lecture: people this age (like most people) respond far better
to "where's your attention actually going" than to "here's what you should care about." Anchor
every example in real independence-stage territory — social media and how it's built to reward the
impressive-but-melting kind of substance, first jobs, dating, leaving home, college or post-grad
decisions — not childhood scenarios and not fully adult ones like marriage or career either.

## What we're assuming

- Every student in the room is actively building an identity right now, whether or not they'd describe it that way — and most of that building is happening on autopilot, not by conscious choice.
- The room holds real economic and social range — some students have significant family financial pressure, some have real social capital or a large following, some have neither. Keep every example general; never ask a student to disclose their own numbers (followers, GPA, income) out loud.
- This isn't a lecture that social media, ambition, or wanting to be liked are bad. The point is narrower and more useful: some of what feels like the biggest deal right now is genuinely impressive and still won't be what's left standing later — and that's worth noticing before it's the only thing you've built on.

## Learning goals

1. Can name, specifically, where their time, attention, and sense of identity are actually going right now — not where they'd say they're going.
2. Feels the real difference between something built to look impressive immediately and something built to hold weight over years.
3. Carries a repeatable question into real decisions this week: "Is this the ice-sculpture kind, or the kind that doesn't run out?"
4. Locates one real, current situation — a relationship, a job, a social feed, an argument they're trying to win — to test it on.

## How to hold the room

- Ask, don't tell. "Where are you building most of your time and identity right now?" lands very differently than "here's what actually matters" — the first invites honesty, the second invites defensiveness.
- Let discomfort sit. If someone names something they're not proud of ("honestly, probably my Instagram"), don't rush to reassure or fix it — that noticing is the whole lesson working.
- Real discussion works at this age, but offer a private-write option first for anyone who'd rather think it through before speaking.

## The lesson flow

### Beat 1 (~7 min) — Naming the terrain

Ask: "If someone watched your last week on video — not what you'd say matters, but what you
actually spent time and energy on — what would they think you were building?" Let a few people
answer honestly; normalize an uncomfortable answer ("that's an honest answer, not a bad one —
that's the point of asking").

### Beat 2 (~12 min) — The investment question

Introduce two categories, written where everyone can see them: "Where I'm actually building"
(grades, followers, appearance, being right, a relationship, a job) and "Precious substance"
(honesty, understanding, love, courage — Proverbs 12:27: "the substance of a diligent man is
precious"). Ask the group to privately (or aloud, if comfortable) place their own real answer from
Beat 1 into one category or the other, and notice which side feels more automatic to build toward.

### Beat 3 (~10 min) — Ground it: the ice sculpture

Present the image directly: an ice sculpture is the most impressive thing at the party and is
melting from the moment it's finished; a spring is the least impressive thing on the property and
never runs dry. Ask: "What's one thing in your life right now that feels like the biggest deal in
the world? What would it look like if it turned out to be the ice-sculpture kind — impressive, but
melting — versus the kind that's quiet but never runs out?" Ground it in Science and Health, p.
257:12–14: "Mind creates His own likeness in ideas, and the substance of an idea is very far from
being the supposed substance of non-intelligent matter."

### Beat 4 (~8 min) — Real discussion

Open the floor: does this land differently for social media than for grades? For a relationship
than for being right in an argument? Let disagreement stand — some things people build toward are
genuinely both impressive and lasting (real skill, real relationships built with care), and the
point isn't to distrust everything that looks solid, just to actually check.

### Beat 5 (~5 min) — Carry it

Teach the question to keep: "Is this the ice-sculpture kind, or the kind that doesn't run out?"
Ask each person to name (privately or aloud) one real, current situation to test it on this week.

## If it's not going well

Silence or a slow start on Beat 1 is normal — the question is genuinely uncomfortable; give it
room. Pushback ("this feels like you're saying social media is bad") is worth engaging honestly
rather than deflecting — clarify the actual claim (attention, not the platform itself, is the thing
in question). A strong reaction — real shame or distress about where someone's been building —
calls for a pause, a private word, and a reminder that noticing is the win, not a verdict. A
disclosure of something more serious — a real crisis around identity, a relationship, self-worth,
or anything suggesting harm — should not be handled as group discussion; respond as a caring
adult, follow the site's mentor safety guidelines, and observe your own reporting obligations where
applicable.

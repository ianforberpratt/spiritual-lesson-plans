## What you should know

This age is right at the edge of comparing two systems side by side — old enough for a real sort,
not yet ready for pure abstraction like "substance is spiritual." The bridge is something
concrete: two columns, real nouns, an actual argument about which column something belongs in.
Some items (a phone, muscles, a good grade) will get contested — that's the lesson working, not a
wrinkle to iron out. Nothing you don't already have for prep beyond something to write two columns
on (whiteboard, poster paper, or have each pair use their own paper).

## What we're assuming

- Every kid in the room can name at least one thing that currently feels like a very big deal to them — a grade, a friendship, a phone, how they look, being picked for something.
- Some kids in the room may come from families where money, a phone, or a house is a real point of stress, not just a sorting-exercise item. Keep the examples general (use the suggested list, not anything specific to a child's actual situation) and don't let the discussion turn into who-has-what.
- This isn't the moment to say phones, grades, or muscles are bad. The point is narrower: something can be genuinely useful or fun and still not be the kind of substance that holds a life together.

## Learning goals

1. Can sort a handful of real things into "Feels Solid" and "Actually Lasts" and notice the two lists don't fully match.
2. Understands, as an actual comparison rather than just a phrase, that something can look impressive and still not last — and something can look ordinary and never run out.
3. Carries one question into the week: "Is this the kind that melts, or the kind that doesn't run out?"
4. Locates one real thing — a friendship, a grade, an argument — to test the question on.

## How to hold the room

- Let them do the arguing. Don't settle contested items yourself — ask "why do you think it goes there?" and let the disagreement do the teaching.
- Pairs or groups of three work better than one big open floor at this age — more kids actually think out loud that way.
- If the room wants to debate whether muscles or money belong in "Actually Lasts," let it run a little — the debate is the discernment this lesson is trying to build, not a distraction from it.

## The lesson flow

### Beat 1 (~5 min) — Warm-up: name a big deal

Ask: "What's one thing that feels like a really big deal in your life right now?" Let a few kids
share quickly, round-robin. Don't comment yet — just collect.

### Beat 2 (~12 min) — The Sorting Exercise

Draw two columns: "Feels Solid" and "Actually Lasts." Call out items one at a time — money,
muscles, a good grade, being liked, a phone, a friendship, courage, honesty, a house — and have
pairs or small groups sort each one, then compare out loud. Expect disagreement; that's the
exercise. Push gently: "What would make something 'actually last'? What would make it just 'feel'
solid right now?"

### Beat 3 (~8 min) — Ground it

Read, in your own words or aloud: "The immortal, spiritual man is really substantial, and reflects
the eternal substance... which mortals hope for." — Science and Health, Mary Baker Eddy, p.
301:6–12. Put it plainly: the realest, most lasting things about a person — their honesty, their
kindness, their courage — aren't things you can drop or lose the way you can lose a phone. Add Job
11:7, "Canst thou by searching find out God?" — plainly: you don't figure out whether honesty is
real substance by taking it apart like a toy to inspect it; you find out by trying it and watching
what it actually does over time.

### Beat 4 (~5 min) — Carry it

Teach the question: "Is this the kind that melts, or the kind that doesn't run out?" Ask each kid
to silently pick one real thing from their own life — something from the warm-up works well — and
quietly test it with the question. No one has to share their answer out loud.

## What you'll need

- A whiteboard, poster paper, or paper for each pair to make their own two columns
- The printable ["Ice Sculpture and the Spring" handout](/assets/materials/what-lasts/handout-8-11-full.html) and ["What Actually Lasts" wallet card](/assets/materials/what-lasts/handout-8-11-wallet.html) — hand out during Beat 3 or at the close, so kids leave with the sorting idea and the citation in hand

## If it's not going well

Silence during the sort is normal for a kid still deciding where something goes — give it room
rather than filling it. Pushback and arguing about where an item belongs is the lesson working,
not a problem. A stronger reaction — a kid who seems genuinely upset or embarrassed during the
money or house items — calls for a pause and a private, warm check-in, not a public one. A
disclosure of real hardship (a family struggling with money, housing, or a recent loss) should be
received warmly and privately — "thank you for telling me" — and followed up per the site's mentor
safety guidelines and your own church's child-safety obligations, not addressed in front of the
group.

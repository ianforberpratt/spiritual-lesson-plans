# Lesson Core Model: "What Actually Lasts"

*Source: the Christian Science Quarterly Bible Lesson "Substance" (Sept 7–13, 2026) and Ian's own
draft — a Sorting Exercise, the story of Elisha's servant, and Build Two Towers, three angles on
one question: what actually lasts, and what only looks like it does. This is shared scaffolding,
never taught directly to any age group. Every band below is checked against this document, not
against each other.*

## Why five bands, and where each of the three source activities landed

All three of the activities Ian sketched are used somewhere below — none of them got stretched
across every band, per the site's "fit what naturally works" rule. Build Two Towers is, at its
core, a piece of concrete kinesthetic play (something goes up fast and something goes up slow,
then gravity votes) before it's an investment question — so the physical building lives at 5-8 as
pure experience, and the "where are you actually building your time and identity" reading of the
same prop moves to 14-21 and 21-plus, where an investment question is something a person can
actually hold. The Sorting Exercise is a natural 8-11 activity — concrete comparison, two columns,
argue it out in pairs — and doesn't need to travel further; older bands get the same underlying
discernment through discussion instead of a worksheet. Elisha's servant carries 11-14 almost
entirely on its own: a real story, privately-held reflection instead of forced sharing, and a fear
that turns out not to be the whole picture — which is exactly the shape of self-worth at that age.
Nothing was diluted to fit a band it didn't belong to.

## The spiritual core (constant across all built bands)

**Central claim:** Real substance — what's actually permanent, dependable, and worth building a
life on — is spiritual: the qualities and ideas that come from God (honesty, love, understanding,
courage, integrity), not material accumulation, appearance, performance, or approval. What looks
the most solid in a given moment is often the least durable; what looks quiet or unremarkable is
often what never runs out.

**The picture being replaced:** that "solid" means whatever looks biggest, shiniest, or most
urgent right now — money, status, a grade, being liked, being right, a body, a possession — and
that building a life around those things is simply the sensible, safe move.

**The picture being offered:** an ice sculpture is the most impressive thing in the room and is
melting from the moment it's finished; a spring is the least impressive thing in the room and
never runs dry. Substance isn't the thing that looks biggest at first glance — it's Spirit and
Spirit's ideas, reflected in a person, found less by proving or searching it out than by living
from it.

**Anchor citations:**
- "Substance is that which is eternal and incapable of discord and decay. Truth, Life, and Love
  are substance... Spirit, the synonym of Mind, Soul, or God, is the only real substance." —
  Science and Health, Mary Baker Eddy, p. 468:16–24
- "The immortal, spiritual man is really substantial, and reflects the eternal substance, or
  Spirit, which mortals hope for." — Science and Health, p. 301:6–12
- "Spirit is the only substance and consciousness recognized by divine Science... Spirit can have
  no opposite." — Science and Health, p. 278:16–19
- "Mind creates His own likeness in ideas, and the substance of an idea is very far from being the
  supposed substance of non-intelligent matter." — Science and Health, p. 257:12–14
- "The substance of a diligent man is precious." — Proverbs 12:27
- "Canst thou by searching find out God? canst thou find out the Almighty unto perfection?" — Job
  11:7
- "Now faith is the substance of things hoped for, the evidence of things not seen." — Hebrews
  11:1
- II Kings 6:15–17 — Elisha's servant, eyes opened to see the mountain "full of horses and
  chariots of fire"

**Learning objective family** (same four verbs, shifting depth per band):
1. **Notice** the moment something feels like the biggest, most solid thing in the room — and
   name it.
2. **Feel** the difference between resting your weight on something that can crack and resting it
   on something that can't — not just understand it as an idea.
3. **Carry** one repeatable question into a real moment: *Is this the kind that's impressive but
   melting, or the kind that's quiet but never runs out?*
4. **Locate** one real, current thing in their own life to test the question on.

## The bridge metaphor, by age band

| Age band | What "feels solid" looks like to them | Bridge metaphor used |
|---|---|---|
| 5-8 | A tower built as tall and fast as possible | Build Two Towers, played straight — no investment framing, just gravity |
| 8-11 | Money, a phone, muscles, a grade, being liked | The Sorting Exercise — two columns, "Feels Solid" vs. "Actually Lasts" |
| 11-14 | Whatever's looming largest right now — comparison, a grade, a group chat | Elisha's servant (II Kings 6:15–17) — the army that isn't the whole picture |
| 14-21 | Followers, grades, being right, a relationship, how you look | Build Two Towers reframed as an investment question — where are you actually building? |
| 21-plus | Career, reputation, a bank balance, being right, security | The same investment question at adult stakes — career, marriage, parenting, civic life |

## What does NOT get inherited across bands

- **The "where are you building your time and identity" investment framing** — this reflective,
  self-auditing move needs abstract self-assessment that isn't available until early adolescence.
  It appears only at 14-21 and 21-plus. 11-14 gets the same underlying idea in narrative form
  (Elisha's servant) instead.
- **Open-ended abstract discussion questions** — never below 11. 11-14 uses private reflection
  with an optional share, never a required one.
- **A spoken-aloud "moral" or wrap-up sentence** — never for 5-8, essentially never for 8-11. The
  falling tower and the two-column sort are built to make their own point; naming it out loud
  flattens what the kids just noticed themselves.
- **Career, marriage, parenting, and civic/political material** — 21-plus only.
- **Dating and romantic-relationship examples** — 14-21 and 21-plus only; 11-14 keeps its examples
  to comparison, grades, and social pressure, not relationships.
- **Direct discussion of family financial hardship** — every band that touches money or
  possessions (8-11's sort, 14-21 and 21-plus's investment question) carries a facilitator aside
  about economic diversity in the room; it's never turned into a discussion topic itself, just
  held with care.
